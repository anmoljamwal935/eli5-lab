Attention: words looking at words

A word’s meaning depends on the words around it. Attention is how a model lets each word look back at the earlier words, decide which ones matter, and pull in their meaning. It is the single most important idea inside today’s language models.

Built from the original papers (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio 2014; Vaswani and others 2017; Su and others 2021) and Meta’s Llama 3 report, facts checked against them · Read the source ↗

Part 1 gave every token a list of numbers, a spot on a map of meaning. Part 2 showed the model guessing the next token. This part shows how the tokens talk to each other in between. Facts carry a tag: public means a published paper or plain arithmetic, example means a made-up number used to show how something works, and not public means the company has not said. The problem

One word, two meanings

Part 1, chapter 17, left a puzzle. “Bank” gets the same starting numbers whether it means a riverside or a place for money. Only the other words in the sentence can tell them apart, so the model needs a way to let them in.

The problem

The deciding word can be far back

The clue is not always next door. In Part 2’s example, the word that settles “I speak fluent ___” is “France”, sixteen words earlier. Looking at the last couple of words, the way a phone keyboard does, misses it.

The textbook shortcut

Just average every earlier word?

The simplest fix is to mix in every earlier word equally. But most words, like “the” and “to”, say nothing about which bank. An equal mix lands in a vague middle that means nothing much.

What the model needs is a weighted mix: a lot of “river” and “boat”, very little “the”. Attention is a way to work out those weights from the words themselves, fresh for every sentence.

The analogy

A question asked to a room

Imagine “bank” standing in a room with the earlier words. It calls out a question: “Who can tell me what kind of bank I am?” Every word wears a name tag describing what it can help with. The better a tag matches the question, the more “bank” listens to that word.

Mapping it back: the question is the query, each name tag is a key, and what each word then shares is its value. Where the analogy breaks: nobody chooses the questions or tags. They are numbers, made by tables learned in training (Part 6), and every word asks and answers at the same time.

The building block

Every word makes three lists

Each word’s list of numbers is multiplied by three learned tables. Out come three new, shorter lists: a query (what this word is looking for), a key (what it can offer) and a value (what it passes on if chosen).

Go deeper: how long are these lists?

In the original 2017 Transformer, each word’s list had 512 numbers and each query, key and value had 64 public. In Llama 3 8B, the word’s list has 4,096 numbers and each head’s query has 128 public. The three tables are part of the model’s learned numbers, set by training.

The mechanism

Step 1: score every earlier word

Compare the query of “bank” with the key of every earlier word, using the multiply-and-add closeness score from Part 1, chapter 14. A matching key gives a high score; an unrelated one gives a score near zero.

The mechanism

Step 2: scores become shares

The scores go through softmax, the recipe from Part 2, chapter 4, which turns them into shares adding up to 100%. Here “river” gets about half of the attention and “boat” a quarter; the small words share what is left.

Go deeper: the formula, and why it divides by a square root attention = softmax( query · keys ÷ √(list length) ) × values

Before softmax, the scores are divided by the square root of the key length. The 2017 paper explains why: with long lists the dot products grow large, which pushes softmax into regions where it is almost all-or-nothing and very hard to train public.

The mechanism

Step 3: blend the values

Finally, “bank” takes each earlier word’s value, scales it by that word’s share, and adds them up. The result is added to its own numbers, which moves “bank” on the map of meaning towards the riverside sense.

At zero focus every word gets 20%, the plain average from chapter 3. Raise the focus and attention piles onto the words whose keys point the same way as the query. Point the query between water and money and it splits its attention, like a reader who cannot yet tell.

Go deeper: exactly what the lab computes

Each word has a two-number key at a fixed angle: river 10°, boat 30°, the 95°, paid 140° and money 170° example. The query is a two-number list pointing at the slider’s angle. Each score is focus × cos(angle between query and key), softmax turns the scores into shares, and the ledger shows every step. Real models do the same with lists of 64 to 128 numbers, learned rather than chosen.

The rule

Only look backwards, never ahead

In a model that writes, each word may only look at itself and the words before it. Later words are hidden. Partly this is because, while writing, they do not exist yet. And in training (Part 6, chapter 12), peeking at the next word would turn every quiz into copying the answer.

This has a sharp consequence. In “I opened a bank account”, the word “bank” cannot see “account”, which comes after it. It stays unsure at first, and “account”, looking back at “bank”, does the work of settling the meaning. The 2017 paper does this by setting the scores for later words to minus infinity before softmax, so their shares are zero public.

The mechanism

Many heads ask different questions

One question per word is not enough. A word may need to know who did the action, what “it” refers to, and which words are next to it, all at once. So each layer runs many attention heads side by side, each with its own three tables and its own kind of question.

The original Transformer used 8 heads per layer; Llama 3 8B uses 32 heads in each of its 32 layers, 1,024 in all public. Nobody assigns the heads their jobs. Some end up tracking grammar, some tracking names, and many do things no one has yet put into words. The labels in the drawing are illustrations example.

The mechanism

Layer after layer, meanings sharpen

Attention runs again in every layer, each time on the updated lists from the layer before. Early layers mostly sort out nearby words; later ones combine ideas from across the whole text. Step by step, “bank” ends up meaning exactly the bank in this sentence.

A missing piece

Attention alone ignores word order

Look at the scoring step again: nothing in it knows where a word sits. Shuffle the earlier words and the shares stay the same. But “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” differ only in order (Part 1, chapter 18).

The common fix today, called rotary position embedding (RoPE), turns each query and key by an angle that depends on the word’s position public. Two words’ score then depends on how far apart they are, not just on what they are. Llama 3 uses this method public.

The cost

Every word compares with every earlier word

Each word scores every word before it, in every head of every layer. Double the text and the number of comparisons roughly quadruples. The 2017 paper lists this cost as growing with the square of the length public.

At Llama 3’s full context of about 128,000 tokens public, that is about 8.2 billion comparisons for one head in one layer. This is why very long chats are slow and expensive, the subject of part 14.

The shortcut that works

Saved keys make writing faster

Because words only look back, an earlier word’s key and value never change once made. So while writing, the model saves them. Each new token only computes its own query, key and value, then compares against the saved pile. This pile is the KV cache.

The cache saves arithmetic but costs memory, and it grows with every token. Llama 3 shares each key and value among four query heads (8 key-value heads for 32 query heads) to keep it smaller public. The bonus study, Inside LLM serving, is largely about this pile.

The common mistake

Attention shares are not explanations

It is tempting to read the shares as the model’s reasons: “it answered that because it looked at this word”. A 2019 study tested this and found that, often, very different attention patterns gave the same prediction public.

Attention is one ingredient among many: the blended values pass through the rest of each layer and dozens more layers. Where a head looked is a clue, not a confession. Part 15 comes back to this when asking why models make things up.

Where it came from

From translation to every model

Attention was invented for translation. In 2014, Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio let a translator “search” the source sentence for the words relevant to each word it wrote public. In 2017, “Attention Is All You Need” built a whole model out of attention, the Transformer public. Almost every large language model today descends from it.

So what

Next: why memory is the bottleneck

Attention lets every word reach back across the whole text, which is what makes these models good at language. But each new token must read every learned number and the growing KV cache from memory. Part 8, back on the chip track, shows why moving those numbers, more than multiplying them, now sets the speed of AI.
