Satyam, decoded

On 7 January 2009 the chairman of Satyam Computer Services, an IT services company with 53,000 people by his own count, wrote to his board that most of the cash on its balance sheet did not exist. This study reads that fraud through the red flags of Part 20: which checks would have tripped on the published accounts, which passed, and why.

Built from primary documents: B. Ramalinga Raju’s letter of 7 January 2009 as Satyam filed it with the US SEC (Form 6-K) · Mahindra Satyam’s restated Annual Report 2008-09 and 2009-10, which reprints the FY2008 accounts “as published” · SEBI’s orders of 2014, 2018 and 2023 · the SEC’s 2011 actions · Tech Mahindra’s Annual Report 2013-14 · Read the letter ↗ · restated report ↗ · SEBI order 2014 ↗

The letter

Five lines that undid a company

Satyam was listed in Mumbai and, through American depositary shares (ADS), in New York. On 7 January 2009 its chairman, B. Ramalinga Raju, wrote to the board, with copies marked to the chairman of SEBI and the stock exchanges. The letter says the balance sheet at 30 September 2008 carried ₹5,040 cr of non-existent cash and bank balances out of ₹5,361 cr in the books, ₹376 cr of non-existent accrued interest, debtors overstated by ₹490 cr out of ₹2,651 cr, and a ₹1,230 cr liability left out of the books “on account of funds arranged by me” public.

“It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten.”RAJU’S LETTER, 7 JAN 2009 · FORM 6-K EXHIBIT 99.2, AS FILED WITH THE SEC

The letter says the gap “has arisen purely on account of inflated profits over a period of last several years”, limited to Satyam standalone, and that what began as a marginal gap grew as the company grew. It says the promoters held a small share of the equity and feared a takeover would expose the gap. It also says neither he nor the managing director took “even one rupee/dollar” from the company, and that no board member knew. Those are his statements; later panels show what regulators found.

The quarter

A 24% margin that was really 3%

For the September 2008 quarter the letter says Satyam reported revenue of ₹2,700 cr and an operating margin of ₹649 cr, 24% of revenue, against actual revenue of ₹2,112 cr and an actual margin of ₹61 cr, 3% public. The ₹588 cr of invented revenue fell straight to the margin, because a fake invoice has no cost behind it. The letter adds that this “resulted in artificial cash and bank balances going up by Rs. 588 crore in Q2 alone”.

That last line is the mechanism of the whole fraud. Invented sales need invented collections, so the books showed customers paying, and the money “arrived” in bank accounts that did not hold it. Part 5 showed how one sale moves through all three statements; here a fake sale moved through all three as well, which is why the statements still balanced.

Years in the making

The fake share of sales kept growing

SEBI’s investigation matched invoices against the books. Its 2014 order lists, quarter by quarter, the sales recorded through fictitious invoices: ₹4,782.75 cr from April 2003 to September 2008 public. As a share of published sales it rose from 8.4% in FY2004 to 21.3% in FY2008 and 22.5% in the half year to September 2008 (my arithmetic).

The published operating margin barely moved, 22% to 27% every year. Remove the fake invoices and SEBI’s table shows the margin sliding from 20.6% in FY2004 to 1.5% in FY2008 public. A steady margin is usually comfort (Part 17 reads it as a sign of a moat); here the steadiness was manufactured, while the real business was being squeezed.

The forensic investigation commissioned by the government-nominated board, summarised in the restated 2008-09 accounts, puts fictitious revenue at ₹5,352.8 cr and fictitious interest income at ₹899.8 cr over April 2002 to September 2008 public. Different scopes give different totals; both say the same thing.

Flag 1 · profit and cash

The cash test passed, because cash was faked

Part 13’s first check is cash from operations against profit after tax: look harder below 0.8 for two or more years. Satyam’s FY2008 accounts, as published and audited, show profit after tax of ₹1,715.7 cr and CFO of ₹1,412.9 cr: 0.82 public. It passes, narrowly.

It passes because the fraud did not stop at profit. Each fake invoice was followed by a fake collection, recorded as cash received from a customer, so CFO rose with profit. Part 20’s check catches profit that is booked but not collected; it cannot catch a collection that is itself invented. When a check passes, the next question is what it rests on. CFO rests on the bank balance being real, and nothing in the cash flow statement proves that.

A passing check is not a clean bill of health. Most of Part 20’s checks compare one reported number with another, so a fraud that falsifies both sides consistently can pass them all; only evidence from outside the company, such as a bank’s own confirmation, breaks the circle.

Flag 2 · interest on cash

Interest booked, but not received

If the cash was fake, its interest had to be faked too. Satyam booked ₹270.0 cr of interest on bank deposits and advances in FY2008 public. On year-end cash and bank of ₹4,461.7 cr that is a 6.1% yield, and on the deposits alone 8.1% (my arithmetic): ordinary numbers that raise no eyebrow. The yield check passes because the invented rate was plausible.

The check that trips is collection. The same cash flow statement shows only ₹62.4 cr of interest and dividends received public, 23% of what was booked (my arithmetic). And the balance sheet carried ₹272.5 cr of interest accrued on bank deposits, more than the whole year’s interest income: over twelve months of interest earned but not paid. The restatement later found ₹0.4 cr of that accrual was real public. A bank pays deposit interest on a schedule; a year’s worth sitting unpaid is a question for the treasurer and the auditor.

Flag 3 · the bank statements

Deposits nobody confirmed independently

The SEC’s complaint against Satyam, which Satyam settled in 2011 without admitting or denying the allegations, sets the fixed deposits and their interest as reported against what the banks actually held public. At 31 March 2008 Satyam reported $912.7m; the real figure was $2.2m. The gap was there at every year end from 2004.

SEBI’s 2014 order found that Satyam kept two sets of statements for its main current account at Bank of Baroda, New York: a daily statement received by email, used for entries, and a monthly statement that came by internal courier from the chairman’s office, used for the month-end reconciliation public. At 30 September 2008 the bank confirmed $10.8m; the monthly statement showed $379.6m. No ratio sees this. It is found by asking the bank directly.

Flag 4 · cash-rich

Half the assets in cash, and no debt

Part 20 flags a company with high cash and high borrowings. Satyam’s published balance sheet at 31 March 2008 showed the opposite pattern: cash and bank of ₹4,461.7 cr, 50.6% of total assets of ₹8,822.2 cr, against borrowings of ₹23.7 cr (my arithmetic on published figures) public. The check does not trip, because the borrowing was off the books.

Two kinds of hidden debt surfaced later. The letter’s ₹1,230 cr “arranged” by the chairman matches ₹1,230.4 cr claimed by 37 companies as “temporary advances”, which the restated accounts hold in a suspense account and which the company, as of that report, had not acknowledged as owed public. And the forensic team found ₹720.1 cr of bank loans taken between 2002 and 2008 that never entered the books public. The question the published numbers did raise was a different one: why would a company with half its assets in deposits, and a growing business, keep piling up cash instead of returning more of it? That question led straight to the next flag.

Flag 5 · debtors

Debtors that would never pay

Part 12 turns debtors into days. Satyam’s published debtors at 31 March 2008 were ₹2,223.4 cr on sales of ₹8,137.3 cr: about 100 days of sales (my arithmetic, year-end balances, including ₹276.4 cr of unbilled revenue) public. For an IT services firm billing large clients that is long, and a fair question. The restatement later removed ₹571.9 cr of the ₹2,365.4 cr of gross debtors at that date as fictitious public.

Fake invoices that are never “collected” pile up as debtors, so the fraud had a choice: leave them unpaid, which lengthens receivable days, or fake the collection, which inflates cash. The forensic summary shows it did mostly the second: of ₹5,352.8 cr of fictitious revenue, ₹4,870.2 cr was turned into fictitious cash and bank balances, and ₹501.0 cr stayed as fictitious debtors. That is why the debtors flag looks only moderately bad while the cash flag, read properly, is enormous.

Flag 6 · a related-party deal

Spend $1.6 billion on the founders’ firms

On 16 December 2008 Satyam announced it would buy 100% of Maytas Properties and 51% of Maytas Infra, a total expected outflow of US$1.6 bn public. The SEC’s complaint describes both as companies then controlled by Raju and his brother, the managing director. Part 19 explains why a related-party deal needs extra eyes; Part 18 why an unrelated acquisition with spare cash deserves scrutiny. This one was both.

Investors objected at once: the ADS fell from $12.55 to close at $5.70 that day, and the next day Satyam said it was “not going ahead” public. The letter calls the deal “the last attempt to fill the fictitious assets with real ones”: paying for real land and projects with cash that, by its own account, did not exist. Three weeks later the letter followed. Market reaction was the only check that worked in time, and it worked on the deal, not on the accounts.

Flag 7 · pledged shares

Promoter shares pledged, then sold

SEBI found that by 30 September 2008 almost all promoter-group shares sat with SRSR Holdings, a company formed by the two Raju brothers and their wives, which pledged them for loans to group entities public. About 6.29 crore shares were pledged; as the price fell, lenders invoked about 5.50 crore and sold about 5.06 crore between 23 December 2008 and 7 January 2009. SEBI notes the loans usually required shares worth 2.25 times the amount borrowed, so each pledged share backed about ₹179 of loan, almost exactly where the price stood the day before the letter.

This is Part 19’s margin-call spiral in real life. The letter itself calls the lenders’ selling “the last straw”. Rising pledges are a flag because they tell you the owners need cash elsewhere; here, the letter says, the pledges raised money to keep the company running while the books showed it awash in cash. That contradiction, a cash-rich company whose owners borrow against their shares, is the question a reader could have asked.

Flag 8 · the auditor

Confirmations that went through the client

Price Waterhouse audited Satyam from 2000. Its reports on the FY2005 to FY2008 accounts were unqualified, and on 13 January 2009 it told the board its reports from June 2000 to September 2008 should no longer be relied on public. In a 2011 settled order, without admitting or denying the findings, the SEC found the PW India firms let Satyam’s management send and receive bank confirmations, and never contacted the banks directly public.

The SEC order lists cases where a bank’s own reply reached the auditor and showed a fraction of the balance in the copy Satyam supplied, and both were accepted as genuine. Part 20’s auditor flags are a resignation, a qualified opinion or an emphasis of matter. None appeared here before the letter. An unqualified opinion is only as good as the evidence behind it, and an outside reader cannot see the working papers.

The pattern is the lesson. The ratio checks that compare one reported number with another mostly passed, because the fraud kept the books internally consistent. What tripped were checks on timing and behaviour: interest that never arrived, a related-party deal that made no sense for a cash-rich company, and, at the very end, lenders selling the owners’ pledged shares. Flags together, all questions, none proof.

What followed

A government board, then Tech Mahindra

Two days after the letter, the Company Law Board suspended the board, and the Central Government nominated six directors public. On 13 January 2009 Price Waterhouse withdrew reliance on its reports. The nominated board appointed counsel and forensic accountants, and ran a global bidding process under former Chief Justice S. P. Bharucha. On 13 April 2009 it declared Venturbay Consultants, a wholly owned subsidiary of Tech Mahindra, the highest bidder.

Venturbay was allotted 30.28 crore new shares on 5 May 2009 at ₹58 each (₹2 face value plus ₹56 premium), 31% of the company, for ₹1,756.0 cr, and more after its open offer, reaching 42.67% public. The restated FY2009 accounts, signed on 29 September 2010, show a net loss of ₹7,935.2 cr after ₹6,242.8 cr of prior-period adjustments for the fraud and earlier errors public. Renamed Mahindra Satyam, the company merged into Tech Mahindra on 24 June 2013, with 2 Tech Mahindra shares for every 17 Satyam shares ↗.

Regulators and courts

What the orders say, and their status

SEBI. Its order of 15 July 2014 found Raju, his brother B. Rama Raju, the former CFO Vadlamani Srinivas, the former VP Finance G. Ramakrishna and the former internal audit head V. S. Prabhakara Gupta had violated the fraud and insider-trading rules; it barred them from the securities market for 14 years and directed disgorgement with 12% simple interest from 7 January 2009 ↗ public. The noticees, then facing the criminal trial, had made general denials and asked for time. The Securities Appellate Tribunal upheld the findings on the merits in May 2017 but sent the amounts and the ban back to SEBI. SEBI’s fresh order of 2 November 2018 ↗ was set aside by the Tribunal in February 2023, and SEBI’s order of 30 November 2023 ↗ restrains the Raju brothers until 14 July 2028 and directs each noticee to disgorge an individually calculated gain.

The 2023 order says it takes effect from the date the Supreme Court directs, where appeals by the Raju brothers, Srinivas and Ramakrishna against the 2017 Tribunal order were pending on that date. I could not confirm anything later. The auditors. SEBI’s order of 10 January 2018 ↗ barred firms practising under the Price Waterhouse brand from issuing audit certificates for listed companies for two years, barred the two engagement partners for three, and directed disgorgement of the ₹13.09 cr audit fee with interest public; SEBI lists Tribunal appeals against it, whose outcome I could not open. In the US, the SEC settled with the PW India firms for a $6m penalty and with Satyam for $10m in April 2011 ↗ ↗.

The criminal case. A Special Court in Hyderabad delivered judgment in the CBI’s case on 9 April 2015; the Supreme Court’s judgment of 14 May 2018 quotes from it ↗. Press reports describe the convictions and sentences, and later appeals, but I could not open the judgment or the appeal orders, so this page does not state the sentences.

Every status on this page is as of the latest document cited. Orders in this case have been appealed, set aside and remade several times since 2014.

So what

Four lessons from one fraud

  1. Check that interest arrives. Interest received against interest booked, and the accrued-interest balance, are on the face of the accounts. Satyam’s were the clearest numeric flag.
  2. Distrust consistency you cannot verify. A margin that never moves and a CFO that tracks profit are comforting only if the cash is real.
  3. Read behaviour, not just ratios. A cash-rich company buying the founders’ firms, and founders borrowing against their shares, contradict the balance sheet. Contradictions are the question.
  4. Know what an outsider cannot check. Bank confirmations sit in the auditor’s files. When the numbers look perfect, the right response is a smaller position and more questions, not certainty.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Satyam’s fake profits came with fake cash, so most ratio checks passed. What gave it away was interest that never arrived, a deal with the founders’ firms, and pledged shares: check what should arrive, and read behaviour that contradicts the books.
