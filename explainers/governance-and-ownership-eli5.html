Governance and who owns the company

Accounts only mean something if the people behind them are watched and are not quietly taking value out. This part asks who owns the shares, who has borrowed against them, who deals with the company on the side, and who checks the books.

The problem

Whoever holds the votes holds the company

Ramu Chai has 10 crore shares. The founding family, the promoters, hold 5.5 crore of them, 55% example. A simple majority is more than half the votes, so they can pass most resolutions alone and choose the board. Outside investors own the other 4.5 crore shares, and their votes together fall short of half.

Control is not bad in itself: a promoter with money in the company has a reason to care. The risk is that the people with the votes can also borrow against their shares, deal with the company on the side, or pick the people who watch them. The panels below take those in turn, then check each against TCPL’s own filings.

Field guide

The shareholding pattern, box by box

Every listed company files a shareholding pattern with the exchanges within 21 days of each quarter end (SEBI LODR regulation 31(1)(b)). It sorts every share into promoters and promoter group, or public. “Public” simply means everyone else, and the filing then splits it, so FPIs and DIIs sit inside it.

Borrowing against shares

Why promoters pledge shares

Promoters often want cash without selling, because selling lowers their stake and can look like doubt. So they pledge: they hand shares to a lender as security for a loan and keep the votes. Ramu’s promoters pledged 0.55 crore shares, a tenth of their holding, against a ₹22 cr loan example. At ₹100 those shares are worth ₹55 cr, 2.5 times the loan. The lender requires cover of at least 2.0 times, so ₹44 cr of shares for ₹22 cr borrowed example.

cover = shares pledged × price ÷ loan = 0.55 × 100 ÷ 22 = 2.5× margin-call price = cover required × loan ÷ shares pledged = 2.0 × 22 ÷ 0.55 = ₹80

The ₹11 cr gap between ₹55 cr and ₹44 cr is the headroom: a 20% fall in the price. Below ₹80 the cover drops under 2.0×, and the lender calls for more security. That call is a margin call.

The spiral

A margin call can feed itself

Below the line, the promoters must add cash or shares. If they cannot, the lender sells pledged shares on the market. Those sales push the price down, which cuts the cover again and forces more sales. Take Ramu if the price falls from ₹100 to ₹75 and each ₹1 cr of forced sales knocks 2% off the price one-off. Real market depth differs by stock and day; 2% is only for the drawing.

From ₹75 the price slides to about ₹63.3 over eight rounds, 15.6% lower again and 36.7% below ₹100. The lender sells about 0.119 crore shares, a fifth of the pledged block, and the loan shrinks from ₹22 cr to ₹13.7 cr one-off. Each round’s sale is smaller than the last, because each one closes most of the remaining shortfall. A thinner market, a higher impact figure, drives the price much lower before it settles: at 4% per ₹1 cr the same fall ends near ₹33 in this model one-off.

This is why investors watch rising pledges. The pledge is invisible while the price rises. After a fall, selling comes from a holder who is not choosing to sell. A pledge does not predict a fall; it raises the damage if one comes.

Go deeper: how many shares must be sold

To restore cover c by selling x crore shares at price P, with S crore pledged and a loan L, the sale proceeds xP repay loan: (S − x) × P ÷ (L − xP) = c. Solving gives x = (cL − SP) ÷ (P(c − 1)). At ₹75: (44 − 41.25) ÷ 75 = 0.0367 crore shares, the first round above. The lab uses the same rule, and stops once cover is back within 0.005 of the requirement.

Neither pattern proves anything alone. A sale can fund a project, diversify the family’s wealth, bring in a partner, or reflect doubt. A pledge can fund the business without diluting control, or paper over a cash squeeze. The pattern is also a snapshot: a quarter can pass between a trade and its filing. Ask who bought, at what price, what the loan is for, and what the company said. Part 20 returns to these as red flags.

Dealing on the side

Related-party deals need extra eyes

A related-party transaction (RPT) is a deal between the company and someone close to its controllers: a promoter, a promoter-group company, a director or their relatives. It can be perfectly fair. It deserves scrutiny because one person may be on both sides of the table, and value can leak from all shareholders to the insider through a price that is a little too high or too low. Ramu buys ₹15 cr a year of cups and packaging from a company owned by the promoter’s brother, 3% of sales example. The next lab treats it as a related party.

SEBI’s rules (LODR regulation 23): every RPT needs the prior approval of the audit committee, and only its independent members vote on it. A material RPT also needs shareholders’ approval by resolution, and no related party may vote to approve it, whether or not it is a party to that deal. Material means above the line in Schedule XII: for a company with consolidated turnover up to ₹20,000 cr, 10% of that turnover, in force since 19 December 2025. Before then the line was the lower of ₹1,000 cr or 10% of turnover. Ramu: 10% of ₹500 cr is ₹50 cr, so ₹15 cr is not material example.

Go deeper: the bigger-company tiers and the royalty rule

Schedule XII sets three bands, by last audited consolidated turnover. Up to ₹20,000 cr: 10% of turnover. Over ₹20,000 cr up to ₹40,000 cr: ₹2,000 cr plus 5% of turnover above ₹20,000 cr (SEBI’s own illustration: turnover ₹30,000 cr gives ₹2,500 cr). Over ₹40,000 cr: ₹3,000 cr plus 2.5% of turnover above ₹40,000 cr, or ₹5,000 cr, whichever is lower. Payments to a related party for brand use or royalty are material above 5% of turnover (regulation 23(1A)).

The watchers

Independent directors and the audit committee

The board hires management and answers to shareholders. SEBI requires at least half of it to be non-executive and one director to be a woman (regulation 17(1)(a)). It also sets a floor for independent directors, people with no material tie to the promoters or management: at least one-third of the board where the chair is non-executive, and at least half where there is no regular non-executive chair, or where that chair is a promoter or related to one (regulation 17(1)(b)). Ramu has 8 directors, 4 independent, exactly half example.

The audit committee is the board’s sub-group that checks the numbers. It has at least three directors, at least two-thirds of them independent, all financially literate, and one with accounting or financial-management expertise (regulation 18(1)). It approves RPTs, as above, and oversees the audit and the internal financial controls.

Independent on paper is not independent in practice. A director can meet every formal test and still be chosen by, and loyal to, the promoters. The rules set a floor on structure; they cannot measure courage.

The checker

What the auditor’s opinion says

An independent firm of chartered accountants audits the statements and signs a report, under the Standards on Auditing (SA) issued by ICAI. Four things to look for in it. Ramu’s auditor, a mid-sized firm, gave an unmodified opinion example.

Unmodified opinion (SA 700): the statements give a true and fair view. It is the normal result. Qualified opinion (SA 705): “except for” a specific misstatement, or a part the auditor could not verify; the rest is accepted. Worse versions are adverse (the statements mislead) and disclaimer (no opinion). Emphasis of matter (SA 706): an extra paragraph pointing readers to something already disclosed, such as a large lawsuit, with the opinion unchanged. Key audit matters (SA 701), required for listed companies: the areas that took most of the auditor’s attention and judgement, each with how it was tested.

A key audit matter is not an accusation. It shows where the numbers rest on the most judgement, which is where Part 6’s choices bite. Who the auditor is, how long they have served, and whether they resign early also matter; Part 20 covers auditor exits.

Small shareholders

How minority holders still have a say

A holder with a few shares cannot outvote the promoters on an ordinary resolution, which passes if more votes are cast for it than against. Four protections still apply. Listed companies must offer remote e-voting on every shareholder resolution (regulation 44(1)) and publish the voting results within two working days of the meeting (44(3)). Related parties cannot vote to approve a material RPT (regulation 23(4)). Bigger decisions, such as a special resolution, need a larger majority than a simple one under the Companies Act. And the independent directors and audit committee exist to represent holders who have no board seat.

Ramu’s promoters pass an ordinary vote alone. A material RPT is different: their 5.5 crore shares are excluded, and the public’s 4.5 crore decide, so a majority of those votes is what counts. Turnout is never total in practice, but the arithmetic shows why the rule matters: it hands the deciding vote to the people the deal does not benefit.

TCPL: a material related-party vote

In FY26 TCPL put an ordinary resolution, “Approval for Material Related Party Transactions with Capital Foods Private Limited”, to a postal ballot public. Capital Foods is a 75%-owned subsidiary, acquired on 1 February 2024 (Form AOC-1, page 487). E-voting ran from 24 September to 23 October 2025, and the results were announced on 28 October 2025. Votes for: 37,97,87,311, or 99.9884%. Votes against: 43,967, or 0.0116% (annual report page 249).

The directors’ report (pages 188–189) says the audit committee approved the transaction on 22 July 2025 after confirming it was in the ordinary course of business and on an arm’s-length basis; the proposal was then voluntarily placed before the board, which approved it, and the members passed the ordinary resolution. The report does not print the transaction’s combined value on those pages. The standalone related-party note (pages 387–388) shows three FY26 lines with Capital Foods: purchases of goods and services ₹832.56 cr (FY25: ₹713.99 cr), inter-corporate loans and deposits taken ₹388.00 cr (FY25: ₹100.00 cr) and repaid ₹281.00 cr (FY25: nil). The three lines add to ₹1,501.56 cr, my sum; other lines in the note, such as reimbursements, are not included.

The vote was held under the rule then in force, the lower of ₹1,000 cr or 10% of turnover. Under Schedule XII today, TCPL’s consolidated turnover of ₹20,290.43 cr (taking revenue from operations as turnover) falls in the ₹20,000–40,000 cr band, so the line is 2,000 + 5% × (20,290.43 − 20,000) = ₹2,014.52 cr, my arithmetic. Whether the same dealings would need a vote today depends on their combined value, which the pages I read do not give.

Go deeper: who voted

The percentages are of votes cast, not of all shares, and the report does not split them by holder. Regulation 23(4) bars related parties from voting to approve a material RPT, and promoter-group entities are deemed related parties, so the promoter group’s votes cannot count towards approval under the rule. The votes for total 37.98 crore, against 65.47 crore shares held by the public.

So what

Three questions before you trust the numbers

  1. Who holds the votes, and has anyone borrowed against them? Read the promoter box and the pledge declaration every quarter.
  2. Who does the company deal with on the side? Find the related-party note, see what was material, and check the price against the market.
  3. Who is watching? Count the independent directors, read the audit committee, and read what the auditor flagged.

Part 20, “Red flags and forensic checks”, puts these governance warnings beside the accounting ones: receivables outrunning sales, cash well below profit, contingent liabilities, auditor exits and dilution.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
The shareholding pattern shows who holds the votes, a pledge shows who has borrowed against them, and related-party deals show who trades with the company on the side. The board, the audit committee and the auditor’s report show who is watching.
