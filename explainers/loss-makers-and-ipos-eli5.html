Loss-making companies and IPOs

When a company makes no profit, P/E has nothing to say. Ask three other questions: does each sale make money, how fast is the cash going, and how long will it last? And when it lists, read the offer document to see who gets the money.

The problem

No profit, so P/E is silent

Teesta Fresh delivers groceries in 15 minutes. Each month it takes 1 crore orders and keeps ₹100 cr of revenue, but spends ₹105 cr on costs that grow with every order and ₹40 cr on fixed costs. It loses ₹45 cr a month before depreciation, interest and tax example.

With earnings below zero, P/E is negative and tells you nothing (Part 7’s third trap). Many of India’s recent listings look like this. Swiggy lost ₹4,154 cr in FY26 on revenue from operations of ₹23,053 cr public. For such a company, investors ask three questions instead. Does each order make money? How fast is cash going? How long will it last?

The building block

Unit economics: one order, in rupees

A customer’s basket is worth ₹400, but most of that belongs to the shops whose goods Teesta delivers. Teesta keeps ₹100: its commissions, the customer’s delivery fee and ads. Then it pays ₹55 to the rider, ₹20 in offers, ₹25 for packing and handling and ₹5 in payment fees example.

What is left after the costs that come with each order is the contribution: here, minus ₹5. As a share of revenue that is a contribution margin of −5%; as a share of the ₹400 basket, −1.25%. Both are used, so always check the denominator. Swiggy reports Instamart’s contribution margin as a percentage of gross order value (GOV), the value of baskets at full price public. Part 16 did the same sum for one cup of tea and found ₹2.25 left over.

Go deeper: what counts as a cost of each order

A cost belongs in the contribution if it rises and falls with orders: delivery pay, discounts the company funds, packing, payment fees, and for a dark-store model the cost of picking and packing in the store. Rent on the store, technology, head-office salaries and brand advertising are fixed for the month. Swiggy’s definition for quick commerce is adjusted revenue less delivery and other charges, platform-funded discounts, the cost of fulfilment services and other variable costs, as a percentage of GOV (shareholders’ letter, PDF page 18). Companies draw the line in slightly different places, which is one reason two companies’ contribution margins are rarely comparable to the decimal point.

The next step

How many orders pay the rent?

Fixed costs of ₹40 cr a month are covered only when contribution per order × orders reaches ₹40 cr. At ₹5 an order that takes 8 crore orders a month, eight times today’s volume. At ₹20 it takes 2 crore. At minus ₹5, no number of orders is enough example.

So the order of events matters: first make each order earn something (contribution breakeven), then grow until the contributions cover fixed costs (EBITDA breakeven). This is Part 8’s operating leverage, starting below zero.

orders needed = fixed costs ÷ contribution per order = ₹40 cr ÷ ₹5 = 8 crore a month

The common mistake

“We’ll make it up on volume”

Growth is often sold as the cure for losses. That is true only when each order makes money. Losing ₹5 an order, doubling orders to 2 crore adds ₹5 cr to the monthly loss; making ₹10 an order, the same doubling cuts it by ₹10 cr example.

This is why a loss-maker’s shareholders’ letter talks about contribution margin first and growth second. Swiggy’s Q4 FY26 letter says that in quick commerce it chose “to prioritize margin integrity over vanity volume”, cutting low-value orders even though volume growth slowed public.

What actually happens

Cash burn and runway

A loss is an accounting number; what keeps a company alive is cash. Teesta’s cash burn is the cash that leaves each month: the ₹45 cr operating loss plus ₹5 cr of capex for new dark stores, ₹50 cr in all. It has ₹600 cr in the bank. Its runway, how long the cash lasts at this pace, is 600 ÷ 50 = 12 months example.

Runway is the clock every loss-maker races. Before it reaches zero the company must reach breakeven, cut costs, or raise money, and money raised in a hurry comes on the investors’ terms. Companies define burn differently: some start from EBITDA, some from operating cash flow, some leave out working capital or leases. Read the definition before you divide.

runway (months) = cash ÷ monthly burn = 600 ÷ 50 = 12

Go deeper: the assumptions

Month 1 has 1 crore orders; orders then grow by the same percentage every month. Contribution per order stays fixed for all 36 months, so the monthly result is contribution per order × orders − fixed costs and capex. Burn is that result with the sign flipped; it ignores interest earned on cash, working capital, tax and any change in prices or costs as Teesta grows. Runway is counted to the day the cash reaches zero within a month, assuming the month’s burn is spread evenly. The cash keeps going below zero after that to show how much Teesta would need to raise. A real company would cut costs or raise money long before; none of that is modelled.

The surprise

An IPO buys time, not profit

Teesta lists. Its IPO raises ₹1,000 cr, but only ₹600 cr is a fresh issue: new shares whose money goes to the company. The other ₹400 cr is an offer for sale (OFS): early investors sell existing shares, and the money goes to them example. Teesta’s runway doubles from 12 months to 24, not to 32.

Run the lab’s “IPO cash, same losses” preset: with a minus ₹5 contribution and fast growth, even ₹1,200 cr runs out in under two years. New money resets the clock; only better unit economics stop it. The new shares also dilute existing owners (Part 21). Swiggy’s IPO was 40% fresh issue and 60% OFS; the panels below read its prospectus.

The offer document

Draft, red herring, prospectus

An Indian IPO goes through three versions of one document. The draft red herring prospectus (DRHP) is filed with SEBI and the exchanges for comments. The red herring prospectus (RHP) is the version investors bid on: everything except the final price. The prospectus adds the price once bidding closes. Swiggy took SEBI’s confidential “pre-filing” route, so its first public draft was an updated DRHP public.

Swiggy’s pre-filed draft is dated 26 April 2024; the updated DRHP-I, published after SEBI’s and the exchanges’ observations, 26 September; the RHP, 28 October. Bids ran from 6 to 8 November, and the prospectus, dated 8 November, fixed the price at ₹390 a share public. Versions differ: between the updated draft and the RHP, the fresh issue grew from ₹3,750 cr to ₹4,499 cr and the shares on sale fell from 18.53 crore to 17.51 crore.

Where to look

Seven places in 536 pages

Nobody reads an RHP cover to cover; Swiggy’s runs to 536 printed pages public. Read the cover first: the fresh issue, the OFS and each seller’s average cost. Then go to the sections that answer an investor’s questions, highlighted below at their true lengths.

Risk factors (page 33): read the first five, which the company chose to put first. Capital structure (92): who owns what, and at what cost. Objects of the issue (142): where the fresh money goes. Basis for offer price (162): EPS, return on net worth and the company’s own key performance indicators (KPIs). Restated financials (279): three years of accounts, recast on one consistent basis. MD&A (359): management’s explanation of the numbers. Litigation (394): cases against the company and its directors. Page numbers are Swiggy’s.

Objects of the issue

Who gets the money, and for what

Swiggy’s IPO raised ₹11,327 cr. The company received the ₹4,499 cr fresh issue; the ₹6,828 cr OFS went to the selling shareholders public. The largest seller, MIH India Food Holdings B.V., offered up to 10.9 crore shares at an average cost of ₹131.15 each; Accel’s fund offered 1.06 crore at ₹11.17 public. At ₹390 they sold at about 3 and 35 times what they paid.

Each use of the fresh issue is fixed in the RHP, and an independent monitoring agency (here, Crisil Ratings) reports every quarter on how much has been spent. By 31 March 2026 Swiggy had used ₹3,785 cr of the ₹4,499 cr, with no deviation from the stated objects public. Watch the vaguest line, “funding inorganic growth through unidentified acquisitions and general corporate purposes”: ₹1,197 cr, 27% of the fresh issue, not tied to any named use.

Go deeper: the figures and the rules

Amounts from the monitoring agency’s report for the quarter ended 31 March 2026 (Crisil Ratings, approved by Swiggy’s board on 8 May 2026), pages 6–8, converted from ₹ million: debt repayment at Scootsy 164.80 (fully used); dark stores 755.40 (615.05 used); dark store lease payments 423.30 (150.30); technology and cloud 703.40 (475.21); brand marketing 1,115.30 (1,102.72); acquisitions and general corporate purposes 1,196.78 (1,137.07); issue expenses 140.02 (140.02). The report notes that the general corporate purposes and unidentified acquisitions together do not exceed 35% of gross proceeds, and neither alone exceeds 25%. It also notes that dark store lease payments ran slightly behind the prospectus’s schedule (₹150.30 cr used by the end of FY26 against ₹154.60 cr planned) because of delays in invoice submission and processing, with the balance to be used in the next quarter. Sellers’ costs are from the RHP cover; each “up to” number of shares was the maximum offered.

Risk factors and restated financials

Losses in every year since incorporation

Swiggy’s first risk factor reads: “We have incurred net losses in each year since incorporation and have negative cash flows from operations” public. Below it, the RHP prints three years of restated accounts. Here they are with the two years audited since listing.

Read both lines of the label. The loss as a share of revenue fell from 64% to 18%: the business is growing faster than its losses. But the rupee loss in FY26 was the largest of the five years, while Instamart, its quick-commerce arm, lost ₹3,511 cr at the Adjusted EBITDA level as it expanded. Because it had no record of profits, SEBI’s rules required at least 75% of the IPO to go to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs: mutual funds, insurers, foreign funds), with no more than 10% for retail investors public.

Go deeper: the eligibility rule, and what “restated” means

Regulation 6(1) of the SEBI ICDR Regulations lets a company list with an ordinary book if, among other tests, it had an average operating profit of at least ₹15 cr over the three preceding years. Swiggy did not, so it listed under Regulation 6(2), which the RHP quotes: the issuer “undertakes to allot at least seventy-five per cent of the net offer to qualified institutional buyers and to refund the full subscription money if it fails to do so” (RHP, “Other Regulatory and Statutory Disclosures – Eligibility for the Offer”, pages 409–410). Restated financials recast each past year with the same accounting policies and correct errors in the year they belong to, so the three years are comparable. FY22–FY24 losses (₹3,628.90 cr, ₹4,179.31 cr, ₹2,350.24 cr) and revenue (₹5,704.90 cr, ₹8,264.60 cr, ₹11,247.39 cr) are from RHP risk factor 1, page 33; FY25 and FY26 from the audited FY26 results.

The KPIs section

The company’s own yardsticks

The RHP’s KPIs are numbers the company chose, approved by its audit committee and certified by an accountant, but they are not in the audited accounts. For a loss-maker the most useful is contribution margin by segment. Instamart’s went from −32.3% of GOV in FY22 to −6.0% in FY24 in the RHP, and Swiggy’s letters track it since public.

A KPI is only as good as its definition. GOV counts baskets at full price, so a margin on GOV looks smaller than the same rupees on revenue. And Swiggy’s Adjusted EBITDA, −₹2,871 cr in FY26, leaves out ₹966 cr of employee share-based payments public: a real cost to shareholders, paid in new shares instead of cash (Part 6 covers adjusted numbers). Read the reconciliation to the audited loss before quoting any “adjusted” figure.

Read it as Teesta at scale. Swiggy burned about ₹3,300 cr in FY26 by its own measure, or ₹3,800 cr counting working capital and all capex, and raised ₹9,931 cr, so its cash more than doubled. Go deeper: how Swiggy builds its cash burn

From the shareholders’ letter, PDF page 16: Adjusted EBITDA −2,871 + treasury income 301 − capex 911 + loan movement 63 + other items 116 = cash burn −3,302. Then working capital −670, QIP proceeds 9,931 and the sale of its Rapido stake 2,399: change in cash +8,358, from 6,695 to 15,053. The cash flow statement (page 24) shows net cash used in operating activities ₹2,898 cr and purchases of property, plant, equipment and intangibles ₹919 cr. Adjusted EBITDA reconciles to the audited loss by adding back finance costs, depreciation, share-based payments (₹966 cr), exceptional items and the associate’s loss, and deducting other income and Ind AS 116 rent. So what

Questions before any verdict

  1. Does each unit make money? Contribution per order, its trend, and whether it is measured on revenue or GOV.
  2. How long is the runway? Cash and liquid investments divided by burn, using a burn that includes capex and working capital.
  3. What is the path to profit? Contribution breakeven first, then the volume that covers fixed costs, and whether growth is helping or hurting on the way.
  4. In an IPO, who gets the money? Fresh issue against OFS, what the sellers paid, and how vague the objects are.

That completes the sector parts. Stage 7 puts the series together, starting with finding companies worth this much reading: Part 36, “Screening for ideas”, builds filters that find candidates without falling into value traps and survivorship bias.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A loss-maker is a race between its cash and its unit economics: growth helps only once each order earns something, new money only buys time, and in an IPO, only the fresh issue reaches the company.
