Banks

A bank borrows from savers and lends to everyone else, so debt is its raw material, not a risk on the side. Its accounts read differently: the questions are what it earns on the spread, how many loans go bad, and how much capital is left to absorb them.

The problem

Debt is a bank’s raw material

Ramu Chai borrows ₹100 cr against ₹250 cr of equity example, a debt-to-equity of 0.4 in Part 11’s terms. Pipal Bank owes its depositors ₹900 cr against ₹100 cr of equity example: read the same way, a ratio of 9 that would alarm anyone judging a tea company. For a bank it is simply the business: deposits are what it lends out.

So the ratios built for ordinary companies break. Debt-to-equity is always huge. EBITDA means nothing, because interest is not a financing cost for a bank but its cost of goods, like tea leaves for Ramu. The money question becomes: how wide is the spread, how good are the loans, and how much equity stands behind them? The site’s How money actually works course explains how a loan creates a deposit and why regulators ration a bank’s capital.

Go deeper: Pipal Bank’s accounts

Balance sheet at 31 March 2026 example: loans ₹800 cr (₹812 cr lent, less ₹12 cr set aside for bad loans), government securities (G-secs) ₹150 cr, cash and balances with RBI ₹50 cr; deposits ₹900 cr (₹360 cr current and savings, ₹540 cr term), equity ₹100 cr (10 crore shares, so book value is ₹10 a share). Opening equity was ₹85 cr; profit of ₹15 cr, no dividend. Assets are treated as their year-end level all year, so averages equal closing figures.

How a bank earns

NIM: the spread on every rupee

Pipal earns 9.5% on ₹800 cr of loans (₹76 cr) and 6% on ₹150 cr of G-secs (₹9 cr): ₹85 cr of interest earned. It pays ₹45 cr to depositors. The gap, ₹40 cr, is net interest income (NII), and NII divided by assets is the net interest margin (NIM) example.

NIM = (interest earned − interest paid) ÷ average assets = (85 − 45) ÷ 1,000 = 4.0%

NIM is the bank’s gross margin. It moves with interest rates: when RBI cuts the repo rate, loan rates often reprice faster than deposits, and the spread narrows for a while. Banks differ on the denominator (all assets, or only interest-earning assets), so check which one a report uses.

Cheap money

CASA: deposits that cost little

Current and savings accounts (CASA) pay little or no interest; term deposits pay a lot more. Pipal’s CASA is ₹360 cr of its ₹900 cr, a CASA ratio of 40%, at 2%; its term deposits pay 7% example. Shift a tenth of deposits from CASA to term and the interest bill rises from ₹45 cr to ₹49.5 cr.

Same loans, same deposits in total, and NIM falls from 4.0% to 3.55% because the funding mix changed. That is why a high, stable CASA ratio is a lasting advantage: it takes branches, salary accounts and payment habits that rivals cannot copy quickly (Part 17 on moats). Savings deposits can leave fast when rates rise elsewhere, so watch the trend, not one year.

The bank’s P&L

From spread to profit

A bank’s P&L (Part 3) runs in a different order. Start with NII ₹40 cr, add fees ₹10 cr, take off operating costs ₹18 cr: that leaves pre-provision operating profit (PPOP) of ₹32 cr. Then come provisions, money set aside for loans that will not be repaid: ₹12 cr. Tax at 25% leaves ₹15 cr example.

Costs over net revenue is the cost-to-income ratio: 18 ÷ 50 = 36%. PPOP is what the bank earns before bad loans; provisions are where good and bad years differ most. Pipal’s profit is 1.5% of its assets (return on assets, ROA). With ₹10 of assets for every ₹1 of equity, that becomes a 15% ROE: in Part 10’s DuPont terms, ROE = ROA × equity multiplier.

When loans go bad

The 90-day rule

RBI tells banks exactly when a loan stops counting as good. A term loan becomes a non-performing asset (NPA) when interest or principal is overdue for more than 90 days; a credit card when the minimum due is unpaid 90 days after the due date public. Classification is borrower-wise: one bad loan makes all that borrower’s loans NPAs.

A substandard asset has been an NPA for up to twelve months; after twelve months in that category it becomes doubtful; a loss asset is one where the bank or its auditors have identified the loss public. Once a loan is an NPA the bank books its interest only when it is actually received, and reverses interest charged but not collected public. The minimum provision for a substandard loan is 15% of what is outstanding, 25% if it is unsecured public; banks may set aside more.

Go deeper: where the rule lives

Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks – Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning) Directions, 2025, dated 28 November 2025: paragraph 42 lists the NPA conditions (term loans overdue more than 90 days, overdraft or cash credit accounts “out of order” for 90 days, bills overdue more than 90 days, credit cards unpaid 90 days after the due date); paragraph 44 makes classification borrower-wise; paragraph 5 defines substandard, doubtful and loss; paragraphs 85–86 set the 15% and 25% provisions; paragraphs 125 and 132 put an NPA’s interest on a cash basis and require uncollected interest to be reversed. The page cited is RBI’s consolidated text, updated as on 1 October 2026.

Gross, net and coverage

GNPA, NNPA and provision coverage

This year ₹16 cr of Pipal’s loans went bad example. That is its gross NPA (GNPA): 16 ÷ ₹812 cr lent = 1.97%. It set aside ₹12 cr against them, so ₹4 cr is uncovered: the net NPA (NNPA), 4 ÷ ₹800 cr of net loans = 0.50%. Provisions over gross NPAs is the provision coverage ratio (PCR): 75%.

GNPA ratio = bad loans ÷ gross loans · NNPA = bad loans − provisions held · PCR = provisions held ÷ bad loans

The year’s provision charge divided by the loan book is the credit cost: 12 ÷ 800 = 1.5% example. Read the four together. A low GNPA with a low PCR leaves losses waiting in future years; a high PCR means today’s profit has already paid for them. The fresh flow of bad loans in a year, over the loans that started it good, is the slippage ratio.

The cushion

Capital adequacy: CRAR and CET1

Equity is what absorbs losses before depositors lose anything. Regulators measure it against risk-weighted assets (RWA): each asset counts by how risky it is. Pipal’s G-secs and cash count 0%; its loans count 75% on average; ₹150 cr is added for operational and market risk example. RWA: ₹750 cr.

Capital over RWA is the capital to risk-weighted assets ratio (CRAR, or capital adequacy ratio). The best-quality part, ordinary equity and reserves after deductions, is Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1); certain perpetual bonds add Tier 1, and subordinated bonds and some provisions form Tier 2. Pipal has only equity, so its CRAR and CET1 are both 13.3% example. HDFC Bank’s report prints its own minimums: total capital 11.9% and CET1 8.4%, including a 2.5% capital conservation buffer and 0.4% because it is a systemically important bank public. Pipal’s 11.5% is that total without the 0.4%.

Go deeper: why a bank can run out of capital while profitable last year

Every rupee of new provisions cuts profit, and through it equity, the numerator. Losses do not reduce RWA much, because the loan book is still there. A bank whose capital falls near the minimum must stop growing, raise new equity (diluting shareholders, Part 21) or keep more of its profit instead of paying dividends. Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

One year, starting from Pipal’s accounts. PPOP stays ₹32 cr; the bad loans turn bad at year end, so this year’s interest was already earned (from next year each ₹1 cr of NPA earns nothing). Provisions = bad loans × coverage. Tax 25%, and a loss earns a 25% tax credit (in reality a deferred tax asset, which capital rules partly deduct). Opening equity ₹85 cr, no dividend. Gross loans stay ₹812 cr; net loans = 812 − provisions. RWA = 75% of net loans + ₹150 cr. The minimum is 11.5% of RWA. Each extra ₹1 of provision cuts net loans by ₹1, equity by ₹0.75 and tax paid by ₹0.25, so the balance sheet still balances. The price stays at ₹20, P/B 2.0 at the base.

Valuing a bank

P/B against ROE

For a bank, book value is mostly loans and bonds, at close to what they are worth if the loans are sound. So banks are priced on P/B (Part 14), and the P/B a bank deserves depends on how much it earns on that book. Pipal earns a 15% ROE and trades at ₹20, twice its ₹10 book value example.

justified P/B = (ROE − growth) ÷ (cost of equity − growth) = (15% − 10%) ÷ (12.5% − 10%) = 2.0

The formula is the dividend discount model of Part 23 rearranged: a bank that earns more on its book than shareholders demand deserves more than book; one that earns less deserves less example for the 12.5% and 10%. HDFC Bank’s points public show the direction: as its ROE slipped from 17.4% to 14.3% over three years, the market cut its P/B from 3.21 to 2.00. Points need not sit on the line: the market’s cost of equity and growth hopes differ from ours, and change.

Go deeper: why not P/E alone?

A bank can lift this year’s profit by providing less for bad loans, so P/E is easy to flatter for a while. Book value also moves with provisions, but the market can check it against NNPA and coverage. Use P/B with ROE, then check the quality of the book (GNPA, NNPA, PCR) before trusting either. P/E is still a useful second view (HDFC Bank: 15.05 at 31 March 2026 public).

The common mistake

Higher profit, weaker bank

Two banks each see ₹56 cr of loans go bad, 6.9% of the book example. One provides 75% and reports a loss of ₹7.5 cr. The other provides 40% and reports a profit of ₹7.2 cr. The second looks better and is worse: it still carries ₹33.6 cr of uncovered bad loans, more than a third of its ₹92.2 cr of equity, into next year.

So never read a bank’s profit without its coverage and its NNPA. A jump in profit that comes from lower provisions, not from higher PPOP, is borrowed from future years. The reverse also happens: a bank may build extra, general or floating provisions in a good year, which depresses profit now and protects it later.

Read it as a story. HDFC Bank’s spread is thinner than Pipal’s made-up 4%: NIM 3.34%, and the bank says loans repriced faster than deposits after RBI cut the repo rate by 1.25 points between February and December 2025 public. Capital is far above the minimum. That combination, a modest ROE on a very safe book, is what a P/B of 2.0 is pricing.

Go deeper: the one-offs in FY26

Other income of ₹62,532.57 cr includes a ₹9,179.4 cr gain from selling part of the bank’s stake in its subsidiary HDB Financial Services; total provisions of ₹23,389.58 cr include the ₹9,000 cr floating provision. The two roughly cancel before tax, so profit after tax of ₹74,671.30 cr (+10.9%) is a fair base, but ratios that use only one of them (cost-to-income, total credit cost of 0.84%) move. The bank also prints its own “debt equity ratio” of 0.53 on its own definition, a reminder that D/E means little for a bank (pp. 259–260). So what

Questions before buying a bank

  1. How wide is the spread, and why? NIM, and how much of the funding is CASA.
  2. How good are the loans? GNPA, NNPA and slippage over several years, not one.
  3. Has profit already paid for the bad loans? PCR, credit cost, and any floating provisions.
  4. How much room is left? CET1 and CRAR against the bank’s own minimums.
  5. Is the price fair for the return? P/B against a sustainable ROE, not this year’s.

Banks take deposits and are watched closely by RBI. Part 30, “NBFCs and insurers”, turns to lenders that cannot take most deposits, where the cost of funds and the gap between when money is borrowed and when it comes back matter even more, and to insurers, whose accounts read differently again.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A bank is borrowed money lent out again: judge the spread it earns, the share of loans that go bad and how much of that is already provided for, the capital left to absorb the rest, and then pay a P/B that its ROE can justify.
