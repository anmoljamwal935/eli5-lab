NBFCs and insurers

A non-bank lender lives on borrowed money, so what matters is what that money costs and when it falls due. An insurer is paid first and pays out later, so what matters is what its promises will earn. Each needs its own yardsticks.

The problem

A lender that cannot take deposits

A non-banking financial company (NBFC) lends like a bank: car loans, consumer loans, loans to small firms. The difference is where its money comes from. In RBI’s words, “NBFCs cannot accept demand deposits”: no savings or current accounts. The few allowed to take deposits take only fixed deposits of 12 to 60 months, without deposit insurance public. So an NBFC borrows from banks and from the bond and money markets. Sahyadri Finance lends ₹10,000 cr, funded by ₹8,000 cr of borrowings and ₹2,000 cr of its owners’ money example.

Two numbers follow from that picture: what the borrowed money costs, and when it has to be paid back. The rest of the NBFC half of this part is about those two. Source: RBI, FAQs on NBFCs, question 3 and the section on public deposits (read 19 November 2026).

The building block

Earn 15%, pay 8%: the spread

Sahyadri charges its borrowers 15% a year and pays 8% on what it borrows, its cost of funds. The difference, 7 percentage points, is the spread. Because ₹20 of every ₹100 lent is the owners’ money and costs no interest, interest paid is only ₹6.40 per ₹100 of loans, so net interest income is ₹8.60: a net interest margin (NIM) of 8.6% example.

For the whole book: interest earned ₹1,500 cr, interest paid ₹640 cr, net interest income ₹860 cr, operating costs ₹300 cr, bad loans (credit cost, 2% of loans) ₹200 cr, profit before tax ₹360 cr, tax ₹90 cr, profit ₹270 cr. That is a 2.7% return on assets, and with assets five times equity, a 13.5% return on equity (Part 10’s equity multiplier at work).

NIM = (interest earned − interest paid) ÷ loans = (1,500 − 640) ÷ 10,000 = 8.6%

The first risk

When borrowing costs one point more

The spread is thin compared with the loans it sits on. If Sahyadri’s cost of funds rises from 8% to 9% and it cannot raise its own rates, interest paid rises by ₹80 cr and profit before tax falls from ₹360 cr to ₹280 cr, 22% less example. A one-point move in the cost of funds is a 22% move in profit: Part 11’s rate shock, with five times the leverage.

Not all borrowing reprices at once. Short-term paper is replaced at the new rate within months; a three-year bond keeps its old rate until it matures. So the cost of funds follows the market with a lag, and the more short-term borrowing a lender has, the faster a rate rise reaches its profit. That brings in the second risk, which is about timing rather than price.

The second risk

Loans repay slowly; paper falls due fast

Sahyadri’s loans run for three years and are repaid in monthly instalments, about ₹400 cr a month including interest. But ₹3,200 cr of its borrowing is commercial paper: short-term IOUs to the money market that fall due within three months example. Normally each paper is paid off with a new one, a rollover. If the market stops buying, ₹3,200 cr is due in three months while the loans have repaid only about ₹833 cr of principal.

This is an asset–liability mismatch: what the company is owed (its assets) comes in more slowly than what it owes (its liabilities) goes out. Borrowing short and lending long is cheap while markets are calm, because short-term rates are usually lower. It is also how a lender with good loans can still miss a payment. Part 12 asked how long a rupee stays stuck; for a lender, the question is whether it comes back before the lender’s own bills fall due.

How regulators measure it

The maturity ladder RBI asks for

RBI makes NBFCs sort every rupee in and out by when it is due: the structural liquidity statement. Since its November 2019 circular, the first month is split into 1–7, 8–14 and 15–30 day buckets, and “the net cumulative negative mismatches … shall not exceed 10%, 10% and 20% of the cumulative cash outflows” in those buckets public. Here is Sahyadri’s first month, counting its ₹500 cr of cash and spreading each month’s flows evenly over its days example.

By day 30, ₹1,253 cr is due (paper ₹1,067 cr, a long-term instalment ₹133 cr and interest ₹53 cr) against ₹900 cr in hand (cash ₹500 cr and repayments ₹400 cr). The shortfall of ₹353 cr is 28% of outflows: Sahyadri breaks RBI’s 20% limit. The same circular added a liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) for NBFCs with assets of ₹10,000 cr or more and all deposit-taking NBFCs: liquid assets of at least 100% of the net cash that could leave in 30 days of stress, phased in from 50% in December 2020 to 100% by December 2024 public.

It has happened

2018: when the rollover stopped

In September 2018 the IL&FS group, which finances and builds infrastructure, defaulted on its debt. RBI’s December 2018 Financial Stability Report put the group’s total debt at around ₹90,000 cr, with mutual funds holding about ₹6,500 cr of it, and noted that NBFC and housing-finance borrowing had been shifting to short-term commercial paper and certificates of deposit “at the cost of long-term instruments” public.

RBI’s own account a year later: “In the aftermath of the IL&FS episode in September 2018 sudden changes in sentiment, risk perceptions and asset liability mismatches surfaced” across NBFCs. Lenders turned to bank loans and term borrowing “for better asset-liability management”, and RBI tightened the rules shown in the previous panel public. The lesson is the one in Sahyadri’s ladder: funding that must be renewed every few weeks can vanish exactly when it is needed.

Go deeper: the sources, and what this page does not claim

RBI, Financial Stability Report, December 2018, Chapter I, paragraph 1.20 (“about ₹ 65 billion of IL&FS group exposure out of a total debt of around ₹ 900 billion”), and Chapter II (the shift to short-term CPs and CDs); RBI, Report on Trend and Progress of Banking in India 2018-19, Chapter VI, 24 December 2019, paragraph VI.14 and Box VI.1. These describe the sector’s funding and the aftermath. This page does not reconstruct IL&FS’s own maturity ladder, which these sources do not give.

Go deeper: the assumptions

Loans ₹10,000 cr repay about ₹400 cr a month (one 36th of the book plus a month’s interest at 15%). Borrowings ₹8,000 cr at 8%. Short-term paper runs three months, one third falling due each month; paper renewed in month m falls due again in month m + 3. Long-term borrowing is repaid evenly over 36 months and is not refinanced. The freeze starts in month 1; after it, all maturing paper is renewed. Interest is paid monthly on the debt outstanding at the start of each month. The buffer earns nothing here. A real lender would also stop or slow new lending, sell assets, or draw bank lines; none of that is modelled.

The surprise

Good loans, and still out of cash

Run the freeze preset and look at the twelfth month: Sahyadri’s cash is back above zero, because its borrowers kept paying. Nothing was wrong with its loans. It was illiquid (short of cash this month), not insolvent (owing more than it owns) example. But a missed payment in month 1 is a default all the same, and lenders who see a default stop lending.

This is why ratings agencies, regulators and careful investors read a lender’s maturity ladder, its cash buffer and its funding mix, not just its profit. Profit tells you whether the loans are good; the ladder tells you whether the lender survives long enough to collect them.

Insurers: life

Life insurers: profit arrives over decades

A life insurer is the lender turned around: customers pay first, and the insurer pays out years later. When it sells a policy it pays commission and set-up costs at once, then earns a small profit each year the policy stays in force. Kaveri Life’s new policies this year lose ₹120 cr in year one and then make ₹57 cr a year for 14 years example.

Discounted at 11% a year example, that stream is worth about ₹250 cr today: the value of new business (VNB). Divide it by the year’s new premium, measured as annualised premium equivalent (APE: first-year regular premiums plus 10% of single premiums), and you get the VNB margin: ₹250 cr ÷ ₹1,000 cr = 25%. Growth in APE says how much was sold; the margin says how profitable it was.

Go deeper: the working, and what the toy leaves out

PV = −120 ÷ 1.11 + 57 × (1 ÷ 1.11² + … + 1 ÷ 1.11¹⁵) = −108.1 + 57 × 6.290 = 250.4. Real VNB is computed by actuaries policy by policy, under rules set by the Institute of Actuaries of India, and also deducts the cost of the capital the insurer must hold against the policies. The APE definition is HDFC Life’s, from its FY26 press release.

Insurers: life

Embedded value: the insurer’s own book value

Because profits arrive over decades, a year’s accounting profit says little. Life insurers publish an embedded value (EV) instead: adjusted net worth (ANW, the shareholders’ money already in hand) plus the value of in-force business (VIF, today’s value of profits from policies already sold). Each year EV grows by the unwind (one year’s discount released), the new business sold (VNB) and how experience compared with assumptions example.

That growth, the EV operating profit, divided by opening EV is the operating return on EV: Kaveri’s 700 ÷ 5,300 = 13.2%. It plays the part ROE plays elsewhere (Part 10). The market’s view is price to EV (P/EV): if Kaveri’s shares were worth ₹12,000 cr in all, P/EV would be 2.0 example, a premium paid for the business it has not sold yet. Like P/B (Part 14), it is only as good as the assumptions behind the book.

Insurers: general

Combined ratio and the float

A general insurer (motor, health, fire) sells mostly one-year cover. Its test is the combined ratio: claims plus costs, divided by premium. Godavari General earns ₹1,000 cr of premium, pays ₹730 cr of claims (a 73% loss ratio) and ₹300 cr of commission and expenses (30%), so its combined ratio is 103%: it loses ₹30 cr on the insurance itself example.

It is still profitable, because premiums arrive before claims are paid. The money held in between, the float, here ₹2,000 cr, is invested; at 7.5% it earns ₹150 cr example. A combined ratio above 100% is survivable while investment income covers it, and dangerous when claims run long or yields fall. Below 100%, the insurer is in effect paid to hold its float.

Go deeper: definitions differ

Companies divide claims by earned premium and expenses by earned or written premium, and some exclude certain items. Before comparing two insurers’ combined ratios, check that both use the same definition, and read several years: one bad catastrophe year can move it a lot.

So what

Questions before any verdict

  1. Lender: what does its money cost, and how fast does that cost move? Cost of funds, its trend, and how much of the funding is short-term.
  2. Lender: what falls due before the money comes back? Read the maturity ladder, the cash buffer and the LCR, not just profit.
  3. Life insurer: how much was sold, how profitably, and how fast does EV grow? APE, VNB margin, operating return on EV, and P/EV for the price.
  4. General insurer: does the insurance itself make money? Combined ratio over several years, and how much of profit is float income.

Lenders and insurers live on rates and timing at home. The next sector part turns to companies that live on the exchange rate: Part 31, “Exporters: IT services and pharma”, covers constant-currency growth, the rupee, deal wins and USFDA inspections.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A lender lives on the gap between what its money costs and what it earns, and dies on the gap between when its money falls due and when it comes back; an insurer is judged by what its promises will be worth, not this year’s profit.
