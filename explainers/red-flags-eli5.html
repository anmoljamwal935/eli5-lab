MONETARY ARCHITECTURE / 28 MINELI5

Red flags and forensic checks

Accounts rarely blow up without warning. The warnings are patterns: profit that never turns into cash, customers who owe more every year, costs that slip onto the balance sheet. Each pattern is a question to ask, never a verdict.

The idea

A flag is a question, not a verdict

A red flag is a pattern that has often come before trouble. It is not proof of trouble, and most flags have innocent explanations: a new sales channel, a big project, a one-time clean-up. Even the healthy teaching company shows two small ones. In FY26 Ramu’s receivables grew 18.4% against sales growth of 11.1%, and its inventory grew 17.0% example. Part 12 showed why: the cash cycle lengthened by four days, which is a reason to watch, not to worry.

A flag earns attention by its size, its persistence and its company. One mild gap in one year is a note. A large gap, several years running, with other flags beside it, is a pattern. The right response is to ask the company, read the notes, and compare with peers, never to conclude from one number.

Profit without cash

Profit that never arrives as cash

Profit is an accounting number; cash is what reaches the bank. Part 5 showed they differ and Part 13 measured the gap as CFO ÷ PAT, cash from operations over profit after tax. Ramu’s FY26 ratio is 58 ÷ 50 = 1.16 example. Ramu Chai B, our fudged twin, reports a higher profit, 55, but only 15 of operating cash: 15 ÷ 55 = 0.27 example.

Look for CFO well below PAT for two or more years. Part 13’s rule of thumb is a ratio below 0.8. One weak year can be plain timing, such as a big order shipped in the last week of the year. Several years in a row mean profit is being booked but not collected, or spent on something that does not pass through the P&L. Ask: where did the gap go: receivables, inventory, an advance to someone?

Receivables and stock

Customers owe more, stock piles up

Part 12 turned balances into days. If receivables grow much faster than sales, customers are paying later, or sales are being booked early or on easy terms. Ramu’s receivable days went from 31 to 33 (45 ÷ 500 × 365). Ramu B’s went from 31 to 44.5 (61 ÷ 500 × 365): receivables up 60.5% while sales rose 11.1% example.

Inventory piling up has two readings. Unsold goods may later need writing down, which hits profit. Or the company may be stocking ahead of a price rise or a festival season. Ramu’s inventory days went from 76 to 80 example, a mild rise; the test is whether it keeps climbing while sales do not.

Innocent explanations are common: a shift to channels that pay slowly (modern trade, exports), a large customer given longer terms, an acquisition, a year-end timing quirk, or a weaker rupee that inflates overseas balances when they are translated into rupees. Ask for the ageing table: how much is overdue, and for how long?

Capitalised costs

Costs moved onto the balance sheet

Part 6 showed that a cost recorded as an asset reaches profit only slowly, through depreciation or amortisation. Some spending genuinely creates an asset; some should be expensed at once. The international standard IAS 38, which India’s Ind AS 38 follows, says internally generated brands are not recognised as intangible assets. So when Ramu B capitalises ₹20 cr of “brand development” example, only ₹2 cr is amortised (over ten years) and ₹18 cr sits on the balance sheet. Reported profit before tax is 73; expensed, it would be 55, below Ramu’s 67.

The cash still left the company: it shows up under investing, not in CFO, so CFO looks better than the true cost of running the business. Ask: what exactly was capitalised, who did the work, over how many years is it amortised, and what do peers do?

Long useful lives work the same way. Spreading an asset’s cost over more years lowers each year’s depreciation. As a one-off illustration that does not change Ramu’s accounts, stretching the life of Ramu’s machines by half would cut depreciation from 12 to 8 and lift profit before tax from 67 to 71 example.

Compare a company’s depreciation rates and asset lives with its peers’ and with its own past. A change in life is disclosed in the notes; a company that changes it in a weak year deserves a question.

Outside the business

Profit that comes from elsewhere

Part 3 separated what the business earns from other income (interest on spare cash, dividends, gains) and exceptional items (one-offs shown separately). Ramu’s other income is 4 of its 67 profit before tax, 6% example. In a one-off lookalike with the same 67, 27 comes from other income: 40% of profit would then rest on treasury returns, not on selling tea.

Recurring “exceptional” items are the other half. A one-off that appears every year is a cost of doing business under another name, and it flatters the profit shown above it. Count how many of the last five years carried one, and read what it was.

Hidden bills

Bills that are not on the balance sheet

A contingent liability is a possible obligation, such as a disputed tax demand, a lawsuit or a guarantee, that is not on the balance sheet but described in a note. Most are disputes the company expects to win; some are lost. What matters is the size against profit and equity, whether it is growing, and how many past claims were actually paid. Ramu’s accounts carry none; this ₹30 cr dispute is a one-off illustration example.

A ₹30 cr claim is 60% of a year’s profit but 12% of equity, so it would hurt without sinking the company. Ask: how much has the company provided, and what happened to the last claims of this kind?

Who signs, who pledges

Auditors, pledges and related parties

Part 19, “Governance and who owns the company”, is built alongside this part and goes deep. Three flags belong on this list. The auditor: a resignation before the term ends, repeated changes, a qualified opinion (the auditor disagrees on something specific), or an emphasis of matter drawing attention to an uncertainty. Promoter pledges: the owners borrowing against their own shares; a rising share of their holding pledged means they may be short of cash elsewhere. Related-party loans or purchases: money moving between the company and the owners’ other businesses.

Ramu pledges 0.55 cr of its promoters’ 5.5 cr shares, 10%. Ramu B’s pledge rose to 2.2 cr, 40%, its previous auditor resigned two years before its term ended, and it advanced ₹30 cr, 7% of total assets, to a supplier owned by the promoter’s brother example. Each can be innocent; together they are a pattern. A key audit matter (KAM) is different: it marks an area of heavy judgement, and most large companies’ reports carry one.

Cash, dilution, projects

Rich in cash, yet still borrowing

High cash and high borrowings together is odd: why pay 10% on a loan while the cash earns 5%? A one-off company with ₹200 cr of cash and ₹180 cr of borrowings pays ₹18 cr of interest and earns ₹10 cr example. Sometimes the cash is not really free: it is trapped abroad, pledged, or an overdraft that is netted against it. Sometimes it is a warning that the cash is not all there.

Frequent equity dilution, new shares issued year after year, spreads the same profit over more shares; Part 21 covers each tool. The flag is repeated raising while the accounts show profits. Capital work in progress stuck for years (Part 4) is spending on an asset not yet in use: it earns nothing and is not depreciated. A balance that sits for years may be a stalled project or costs parked that should be expensed.

Tax and timing

A low tax rate and late results

The effective tax rate is tax divided by profit before tax. Ramu’s is 17 ÷ 67 = 25.4% example, close to the 25.17% rate TCPL’s own tax note uses as the Indian rate public. A rate far below normal means either a legitimate shield (tax-exempt income, losses brought forward, a concession) or profit that is not what it seems. Ask for the reconciliation note, which lists every item between the standard rate and the actual one.

Delayed results and frequent restatements are the softest signals. Listed companies must file results by exchange deadlines (Part 2 shows where the filings sit). A late filing, a restated prior year or a changed accounting policy may be a clean-up or a sign the numbers do not hold together. One occurrence is a question; a habit is a pattern.

Screening scores. Some researchers combine several of these ratios into one number. The best known is the Beneish M-score (Messod Beneish, 1999), which blends eight ratios from the financial statements, among them receivables against sales and the gap between accruals and cash, into a probability-style score of earnings manipulation. Such scores are aids, not proof: they were built on one country’s data, they can misfire on fast-growing or acquisitive companies, and they cannot read a note. Use one to decide where to look first, then read the accounts yourself.

    TCPL: cash, receivables and stock

    Tata Consumer Products, consolidated, year to 31 March 2026 public. This runs the checklist on the audited statements to show how to ask questions, not to accuse: nothing printed here suggests anything improper. Cash. CFO was ₹2,421.85 cr against profit for the year of ₹1,546.80 cr, a CFO ÷ PAT of 1.57 (FY25: 1.60), healthy, as Part 13 found; it includes a ₹145.19 cr inflow from working capital. Receivables are the flag worth asking about: trade receivables rose from ₹869.79 cr to ₹1,148.33 cr, +32.0%, while revenue from operations rose 15.2%. On year-end balances, receivable days went from 18.0 to 20.7. Inventories fell from ₹3,599.91 cr to ₹3,526.76 cr.

    What does the report say? Its own ratios are flat: the management discussion shows “debtors’ turnover” of 18 days in both years, and the audited ratio note shows trade-receivables turnover of 20.11 against 19.95. Both use average receivables. The year-end jump looks larger because FY25’s closing balance was low against its average (the printed ratio implies a FY24 close near ₹896 cr, my arithmetic). The report offers no explanation of the FY26 rise itself. It does say that its e-commerce channel “grew 62%+ led by quick commerce” and that it expanded in regional modern trade, channels that can carry different payment terms, but it never connects them to receivables; that link is my guess, not the report’s.

    Of the ₹263.19 cr rise in gross receivables, ₹107.35 cr is not yet due (+17%) and ₹165.71 cr is up to six months past due (+74%); older balances fell by ₹9.87 cr, and the allowance for credit impairment fell from ₹63.51 cr to ₹48.16 cr. Receivables due from a related party were ₹46.24 cr (FY25: ₹17.08 cr). Stock is not piling up: on materials and purchases, inventory days went from 130.5 to 108.6 (my arithmetic, 365 days).

    Contingent liabilities. What I would ask management: why year-end receivables rose 32% against 15% sales growth, and why the part not yet due and the part under six months past due both grew; what the ₹46.24 cr due from a related party is; what sits behind an asset write down in both years; how the ₹645 cr of tax claims have fared in past years; and when the Yeshwantpur land will be developed or sold.     Go deeper: what the UK goodwill KAM asks

    Goodwill is what was paid above the value of assets bought (Part 4); it is tested each year against the cash the unit is expected to earn. For UK and Europe the note uses a 10.15% pre-tax discount rate and 1.44% long-term growth, and concludes that no reasonable change in the assumptions would make recoverable value fall below carrying value. A KAM tells you the auditor spent time on a judgement, not that the judgement is wrong.

    So what

    Five habits for any set of accounts

    1. Compare profit with cash, over years. CFO well below profit for two years is the first flag to chase.
    2. Compare balances with sales. Receivables, inventory and capital work in progress should not outgrow the business without a reason.
    3. Read the notes. Capitalised costs, contingent liabilities, related parties and tax reconciliations live there.
    4. Count flags, then read them. Several small flags together say more than one big one; each is a question.
    5. Ask before you conclude. Most flags have an innocent reason. Part 37 will put the whole checklist on one company, start to finish.

    The next part, Part 21, “Corporate actions and dilution”, asks what bonus issues, splits, buybacks, rights issues, QIPs, ESOPs and demergers do to a shareholder’s stake, and which of them cut earnings per share.

    AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
    A red flag is a pattern worth a question, not proof of a problem: compare profit with cash, balances with sales, read the notes, and ask before you conclude.
