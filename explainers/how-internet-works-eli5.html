How the internet actually works

A photo your friend sends from London can light up your phone in New York in about an eighth of a second. It does not fly there in one piece: it is chopped into about 1,400 numbered pieces, turned into light, and rebuilt at the other end.

The journey

One photo, 130 milliseconds

Sophie is in London. You are in New York. She taps send on a photo in your group chat, and your phone lights up about 130 milliseconds later. A millisecond is one thousandth of a second, so this is roughly one eighth of a second — faster than a blink, which takes about 300 milliseconds.

The video slows this trip down so we can follow it stop by stop. These times are a tidy illustration, not a stopwatch reading from one real send: your own trip changes with distance, busy networks and your phone. But the order of the stops is real, and every stop below is one this page will unpack.

The building block

First, the photo becomes numbers

A phone cannot send a picture as a picture. It stores the photo as a long list of numbers: how bright and what colour each tiny dot (called a pixel) is. Written in the simple counting system computers use, built only from 1 and 0, that list becomes millions of ones and zeros. Each single 1 or 0 is called a bit; eight bits make one byte, the usual unit for counting file size.

Before anything leaves, the phone also locks the list. In plain words, it scrambles the numbers with a secret key so that anyone who grabs them on the way sees only gibberish. Only the phones in the chat hold the key to unscramble them. This scrambling is called encryption, and when only the sender's and receivers' phones can open it — not even the company in the middle — it is called end-to-end encryption.

The problem

Too big to send in one go

Our running example is a photo file of about 2 million bytes. Networks do not move a file that size in one lump. There is a usual largest chunk, about 1,500 bytes including its labels, that the equipment on the path will accept. So the phone chops the locked photo into small pieces called packets. At about 1,460 usable bytes per packet, 2 million bytes needs about 1,400 packets — the video's number.

Each packet carries two extra things. An address: where it is going, written as numbers (an IP address — IP is just the internet's shared rulebook for addresses). And its own number: packet 1, packet 2, and so on, saying where this piece belongs in the full photo. Remember those numbers. They become the heroes near the end of the trip.

The mechanism

First by air, then by light

The first short hop is the only wireless part near Sophie. Her phone sends the packets as radio waves — invisible waves in the air, the same family as light but with much longer waves — to a cell tower down the street. In the video this takes about 4 milliseconds. Radio is handy because no wire reaches a moving phone, but it is also the slowest, most easily disturbed part of the whole trip.

The tower then changes the signal's form. The packets become tiny pulses of light flashing inside thin glass threads called optical fibres, buried in cables under London's streets. Light in glass can carry far more, far faster, than radio through air, so almost everything after the first hop travels this way.

The mechanism

The server that cannot read it

About 10 milliseconds in, the packets reach a computer owned by the chat app — a server, which just means a computer that waits for requests and responds to them. Because the photo is locked end to end, the server sees only scrambled numbers. It cannot open the photo. What it does know is the group's member list: who is in this chat.

So the server becomes a post office that cannot read the letters. It takes Sophie's locked packets and sends a copy onward to every member's phone — including yours in New York. One honest limit: this blindness is only true for apps built with end-to-end encryption, such as WhatsApp, Signal and iMessage. WhatsApp's own help centre says even WhatsApp cannot read such chats. Some other apps lock the photo only part of the way and could open it on their server, so check your app.

The mechanism

Nobody knows the whole route

After the server, there is no single planned road to New York. The packets hop from box to box. Each box is a router: a special computer whose only job is to look at a packet's address and hand it to the next box in roughly the right direction. A router keeps a list — which neighbour is a good next step for which addresses — and picks from that list in a fraction of a millisecond.

The video says each router finds "the fastest path it can". That is a kind summary, but not exact: no router sees or chooses the whole path, and the choice is "a good next hop", not a guaranteed fastest whole route. Different packets from the same photo can even take different roads. Keep that in mind — it explains the mess at arrival.

The mechanism

Across the ocean in glass

At about 20 milliseconds the light leaves Cornwall, on England's south-west coast, and enters a cable lying on the Atlantic floor. TeleGeography, the firm that maps these cables, says that for most of its journey such a cable is about as wide as a garden hose — roughly 17 to 21 millimetres across in deep water. Inside it, the glass threads carrying the light are about as thin as a human hair. Cables like this carry about 99% of the data that moves between continents (an industry estimate; satellites handle well under 1% of United States international capacity, by the regulator's figures TeleGeography quotes).

Light weakens as it travels, so every 50 to 80 kilometres a device in the cable, called a repeater, boosts it back to full strength — the video said 50 to 100; the industry guide KIS-ORCA gives 50 to 80. A 6,000-kilometre crossing therefore passes roughly 75 to 120 repeaters. And the crossing itself is quick for a simple reason: light in glass travels at about 200,000 kilometres per second, about two thirds of its speed in empty space. Divide 6,000 kilometres by that speed and you get about 30 milliseconds — exactly the video's ocean leg, ashore on Long Island at about 50 milliseconds.

The surprise

Arrival is a mess — by design

The packets do not arrive neatly. Because different packets can take different roads, they show up out of order: packet 5 before packet 2, say. And some never show up at all. In the video, three of the 1,400 are missing. This is not a failure of the system; the system expects it, because cheap, busy networks drop pieces now and then.

Now the numbers earn their keep. Your phone lines the packets up by number, sees exactly which numbers are missing, and sends back a short "send those again" message. The missing pieces make the trip a second time. This give-and-take — number every piece, confirm what arrived, resend what did not — is the heart of TCP, the internet's main delivery rulebook (today's standard is RFC 9293); newer apps often use a modern cousin of it with the same numbering idea. Only when every packet is present does your phone unlock the full list and rebuild the photo. That is also why the tidy 130 milliseconds is optimistic: one real resend needs a signal back across the ocean plus a fresh crossing, adding about 60 milliseconds on this route.

Try it

How long should the ocean take?

No app can beat light. Here the fastest possible trip (the dashed line) is pure light travel at about 200,000 kilometres per second, with nothing lost and no waiting anywhere. Add the everyday waiting — phones thinking, servers copying, routers choosing — and then lose a few packets and watch the resend bill arrive. Photo size changes how many packets there are; on this page it does not change light's travel time.

Fixing lost packets is charged as one extra round trip: the "missing" signal travels back, then the resent packets travel forward. Real networks sometimes notice losses sooner, so treat this as an honest estimate, not an exact bill.

The surprise

1858: sixteen hours for 98 words

The same ocean was crossed by wire long before the internet. In August 1858 the first telegraph cable across the Atlantic carried a 98-word message of congratulations from Queen Victoria to United States President James Buchanan. The words were tapped out by hand in code, letter by letter, and the message took about 16 hours to get through. The cable failed a few weeks later, but the idea never died.

Put the two trips side by side. Sixteen hours is 57,600,000 milliseconds. Today's photo — millions of times more information than 98 words — crosses in about 130 milliseconds on the video's telling. That makes the modern trip roughly 440,000 times faster in time alone, before you even count how much more it carries. So what

Three things this explains about your day

  • Why far-away calls always lag a little. Light in glass needs about 30 milliseconds to cross an ocean, and your voice must go there and back. No better app can remove that floor; distance is physics, not bad software. Try the lab above with your own city pair.
  • Why one cut cable can slow a country. About 99% of between-continent data rides on a few hundred undersea cables, each about as thick as a garden hose. When one is cut by an anchor or fishing gear, traffic squeezes onto the remaining cables until a repair ship arrives.
  • Why the lock matters, and its limit. In an end-to-end encrypted chat, the server that carries your photo cannot read it — only the phones in the chat can. But that protection depends on your app: if it does not offer end-to-end encryption, the company in the middle may be able to open what passes through.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
The internet is not one road or a cloud in the sky: it is millions of numbered pieces of light, handed from box to box, and rebuilt by their numbers at the other end.
