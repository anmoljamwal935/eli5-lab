The economy around the company

A company’s profit depends on forces it does not control: the price of its raw materials, the monsoon, inflation, interest rates and the rupee. Measure how much each one moves the accounts, and whether the company can pass it on, and you know what kind of business you own.

The problem

Same company, different weather

Ramu Chai does not set the price of tea, the RBI’s policy rate, the rupee or the rain. Each of them still lands on a line of its accounts. Take the FY26 P&L example and ask, slice by slice, what outside force could move it. Tea follows the crop, and the crop follows the weather. Wages and rent follow general inflation. The interest bill follows the rates banks charge, which follow the repo rate. Tax follows policy. And demand, how many cups are sold at all, moves the whole bar.

A company’s own decisions (Parts 16 to 21) matter, but so does this list. Reading a business means knowing which of these forces it is exposed to, how much, and whether it can pass the cost on.

Where things stood

The economy at the end of FY26

The three readings most often quoted, as close to 31 March 2026 as the official tables allow public. The policy repo rate, the rate at which the RBI lends to banks overnight, was 5.25%: the Monetary Policy Committee held it there on 6 February 2026, and the RBI’s weekly statistical table shows 5.25% on 27 March and 3 April, against 6.25% a year earlier. CPI inflation for March 2026 was 3.40% (food 3.87%), in MoSPI’s release of 13 April, confirmed as final in its 12 May release.

The rupee: a US dollar cost ₹94.60 on 27 March 2026, against ₹85.58 on 28 March 2025, on FBIL reference rates in the same RBI table. That is 10.5% more rupees for a dollar, a weaker rupee. We could not find a reference rate dated 31 March 2026 in the RBI’s weekly tables; the next reading is ₹93.21 on 3 April. Inflation is measured on a new CPI series (base 2024 = 100) from January 2026. The government kept the RBI’s target at 4%, with a band of 2% to 6%, for April 2026 to March 2031 (RBI).

Go deeper: how the repo rate and inflation fit together

The RBI raises the repo rate to cool spending when inflation runs high, and cuts it when inflation is low and growth needs help. Banks’ lending and deposit rates follow, with a lag. The site’s explainer How money actually works covers how the RBI and banks create and price money. The figures here are frozen at FY2026: the repo rate and the rupee have moved since, so check the RBI before using them.

Commodities

Tea up 20%: absorb or pass on?

Tea is ₹0.90 of each ₹5.00 cup (Part 16), ₹90 cr on 100 crore cups example. Suppose a weak monsoon pushes the tea Ramu buys up 20%. Each cup now costs ₹0.18 more, ₹18 cr a year. Ramu has two choices, and the cup shows both.

Absorb it: keep the price at ₹5.00. Every rupee of extra cost comes out of EBITDA, because the fixed costs are already paid: EBITDA falls from ₹85 cr to ₹67 cr, −21%. A 20% rise in one input, 3.6% of the price, takes a fifth of EBITDA. That is Part 8’s operating leverage working on costs. Pass it on: raise the cup to ₹5.18, +3.6%, and EBITDA stays at ₹85 cr, if customers keep buying.

The monsoon

A weak monsoon can hit twice

Most of India’s farm output still depends on the June-to-September rains. A poor season can reach a consumer company twice. On the cost side, crops such as tea, sugar and milk fodder cost more. On the demand side, farm households earn less and buy less. TCPL’s own report says of tea that “as an agricultural crop, the supply/demand balance may change quickly based on the changes in weather conditions” (Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, page 255) public.

Put both on Ramu example: tea +20%, not passed on, and 5% fewer cups. Each lost cup now gives up ₹2.07 of contribution, not ₹2.25, because tea already squeezed it. EBITDA falls from ₹85 cr to ₹56.65 cr, −33%. The 5% fall in cups is our assumption; we have not tied it to a real rainfall figure.

Pricing power

Passing it on needs pricing power

Raising the price works only if customers stay. How many leave depends on pricing power: a strong brand, a daily habit, few close substitutes (Part 17’s moats). The chart passes on more and more of the ₹0.18 under three assumptions example: cups hold; each 1% rise in price loses 1% of cups; each 1% loses 2%.

With loyal customers, passing on all of it restores EBITDA to ₹85 cr. With price-sensitive ones, the cups lost cancel most of the gain: ₹68.8 cr, barely above absorbing it. A company that can pass costs on protects its margin through a commodity cycle; one that cannot, takes the hit. Prices also tend to follow costs with a lag, so even a strong brand can lose margin for a few quarters.

Go deeper: the working

Passing on a share s of ₹0.18 sets the price at 5 + 0.18s, a rise of 3.6s%. Cups are 100 × (1 − e × 3.6s%), where e is the cups lost per 1% of price (0, 1 or 2). EBITDA = cups × (price − ₹1.85 − ₹1.08) − 140. At s = 100%: e = 1 gives 96.4 × 2.25 − 140 = 76.9; e = 2 gives 92.8 × 2.25 − 140 = 68.8.

Inflation

Inflation lifts the numbers, not the business

Inflation is the rise in prices in general, measured by the change in the consumer price index (CPI). It was 3.40% in March 2026 public. Suppose every price and cost Ramu faces rises 4% example: the cup, tea, milk, wages, rent. Sales become ₹520 cr and EBITDA ₹88.4 cr, +4%. Nothing real has changed: the same cups, bought with rupees that each buy 4% less. That is the difference between nominal and real growth, which How India turns rupees into real GDP explains for the whole economy.

What hurts is a relative move: costs rising faster than the price. If the cup rises 4% but every cost rises 6%, EBITDA falls to ₹80.1 cr, and in today’s money to ₹77.0 cr. The tea shock is the extreme case: one cost up 20% while general inflation is near 3%. When a company reports sales growth, ask how much is price and how much is volume (Part 32 covers this for consumer companies).

Interest rates

For Ramu, rates bite less than tea

When the RBI raises the repo rate, banks’ lending rates follow, and a borrower on a floating rate pays more (Part 11 showed 10% → 12%). Here, a one-point repo-led rise example: Ramu’s ₹100 cr of borrowings move from 10% to 11%, finance costs from ₹10 cr to ₹11 cr, and profit before tax from ₹67 cr to ₹66 cr. Profit after tax goes from ₹50 cr to ₹49.25 cr.

Drawn next to the other shocks, the direct hit is small, because Ramu borrows only ₹100 cr against ₹85 cr of EBITDA. Rates matter more in two other ways. They move demand: dearer loans slow purchases of cars, homes and machines, much more than of tea. And they move what future profit is worth today, for every company, borrower or not. That is the next part. A heavily indebted company feels all three at once.

Go deeper: assumptions

All ₹100 cr is assumed to reprice at once; in reality only floating-rate debt does, and with a lag. Interest earned on Ramu’s ₹40 cr of cash (₹4 cr) is held unchanged; if it also earned one point more, profit before tax would recover ₹0.4 cr. Tax is Ramu’s 17 ÷ 67 of profit before tax.

The rupee

Same dollars, more rupees

Ramu sells only in India and, in this series, buys in rupees, so the rupee does not touch its accounts directly example. A company with sales abroad is different. Take US sales of $10 cr. At ₹85.58 a dollar they are ₹855.8 cr in the rupee accounts; at ₹94.60 they are ₹946.0 cr, 10.5% more, with no extra cup sold. Those two rates are the RBI’s readings near the end of FY25 and FY26 public.

This is why companies report constant-currency growth: the growth at last year’s exchange rates, which strips the rupee out. A weaker rupee lifts the reported sales and profit of exporters and overseas subsidiaries, and raises the cost of anything imported. Some of the effect sits outside profit: overseas assets are re-valued in rupees through other comprehensive income. Part 31 covers exporters, where this matters most.

Cyclical or defensive

How far demand swings in a slowdown

The economy also moves how much people buy. People keep drinking tea, using salt and taking medicines in a bad year: those businesses are defensive. A car, a flat, a steel beam or a machine can be put off: those are cyclical, and their volumes swing with the economy and with interest rates. Give two businesses Ramu’s exact cost structure example: ₹2.25 of contribution a unit and ₹140 cr of fixed costs. In a slowdown the defensive one sells 3% fewer cups; its cyclical twin 20% fewer units.

Same costs, same operating leverage: the difference is only the demand. The defensive business loses 8% of EBITDA; the cyclical twin loses 53%. That is why cyclical earnings are judged across a whole cycle, not at the peak, and why their P/E misleads at the top and the bottom (Part 7’s trap). Part 33 covers cyclicals.

Try it

Shock Ramu’s year yourself

Start from Ramu’s FY26 accounts example and move four dials. Assumptions: only tea moves among the costs; the price rises by the share of the tea change you pass on (a tea fall is passed on as a price cut); cups sold is your own setting, so price and volume are not linked here; fixed cash costs (₹140 cr), D&A (₹12 cr) and interest on cash (₹4 cr) stay put; the borrowing rate applies to all ₹100 cr of debt; tax is 17 ÷ 67 of profit before tax, and none on a loss. The dashed marks are FY26 as reported.

Show the working

Try, in order: the two monsoon presets, then “Weak monsoon, passed on” and lower the cups: at −8% EBITDA is back at ₹67 cr and the price rise has bought nothing. Compare “Rate +1 point” with a 5% tea move. Finally set tea to +50%, pass none of it on, cups to −15% and the rate to 14%: profit before tax turns negative.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL: tea costs and the rupee

Tata Consumer Products, FY2026, year to 31 March 2026 public. Its AGM presentation (10 June 2026, slide 7) puts the International business, international tea and US coffee, at ₹4,885 cr: up 16% in rupees, but only 9% in constant currency. The difference is the rupee. Our reading of what that means in rupees, using TCPL’s rounded percentages:

Across the whole group the same effect is smaller: revenue of ₹20,290 cr grew 15%, or 13% in constant currency (IAR page 223). The weaker rupee also lifted the rupee value of TCPL’s overseas businesses: “exchange differences on translation of foreign operations” added ₹908.17 cr to other comprehensive income in FY26, against ₹246.25 cr in FY25 (page 415). That is outside the ₹1,546.80 cr profit for the year.

Tea costs, in TCPL’s words. The same choice as in our lab, absorb or pass on, made in real life:

“The tea business was impacted in H1 with significant inflation in tea cost carried forward from last year, which was mitigated through calibrated price increases, thereby protecting the margins and bringing the margins to normative levels in H2 aided by tapering of tea cost inflation.”BOARD’S REPORT · IAR PAGE 179
“The tea business recorded growth driven by a favourable mix and higher volumes, despite softer demand conditions and price reductions implemented to pass on the benefits of tapering of tea cost.”BOARD’S REPORT · IAR PAGE 177

So TCPL first passed a tea rise on, in steps, and then passed part of the fall back. Its standalone EBITDA margin rose 140 basis points to 12.5% “on account of tapering of tea cost inflation in India” (page 223). Abroad, International margins fell, “primarily driven by an increase in coffee costs and the impact of U.S. tariffs” (page 177): a different commodity, and a policy shock.

Go deeper: how much does a 5% currency move change TCPL’s profit?

The risk note (Note 36, page 467) gives one sensitivity: a 5% rise in the foreign currencies TCPL is exposed to would change profit before tax by +₹5.46 cr and pre-tax equity by −₹20.79 cr (the hedges lose value). That covers only foreign-currency financial assets, liabilities and hedge contracts on the books at year-end. It does not include the much larger effect of translating the overseas subsidiaries’ sales and profit into rupees, which shows up in the International growth above. The same note says commodity prices for “tea, salt, pulses and coffee” are managed through “active sourcing, distribution of source of supply, private purchases and alternate blending strategies” (page 468), with futures and options used only for US coffee. FY25 International revenue of about ₹4,211 cr is our estimate from the 16%; TCPL’s slide gives only the growth rate.

So what

Questions about the world outside

  1. Which forces touch which lines? Inputs, borrowing, currency, demand, policy. A tea company and a steel company answer differently.
  2. How big is each exposure? Tea is 18% of Ramu’s cup price. A 20% rise in it adds only 3.6% to the cost of a cup, yet takes a fifth of EBITDA.
  3. Can it pass costs on, and how fast? Read the management discussion for price increases, lags and what happened to volumes.
  4. Defensive or cyclical? If demand can be postponed, judge profit over a whole cycle, not one year.
  5. Reported or constant currency? Separate what the business did from what the rupee did.

One force reaches every company, borrower or not: interest rates change what a rupee of profit in ten years is worth today. Part 23, “Discounting and the discount rate”, starts Stage 5 with exactly that.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Every company sits in an economy it does not control. Find the lines each outside force moves, size the hit, and ask whether the company can pass it on: that, more than the forecast, tells you how its profit will behave.
