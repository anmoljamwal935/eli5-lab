Thinking and using tools

A model spends the same effort on every token it writes, so a hard question answered in one word gets almost no thought. This part shows the two ways around that: write the working out first, and hand jobs like arithmetic and search to real programs. Run both in a loop and you have an agent, with a new way to fail: small slips that add up over many steps.

A topic study built from the primary sources: the chain-of-thought (Wei and others, 2022), self-consistency (Wang and others, 2022), ReAct (Yao and others, 2022), Toolformer (Schick and others, 2023) and DeepSeek-R1 (2025) papers, OpenAI’s “Learning to reason with LLMs” (September 2024), METR’s time-horizon study, Anthropic’s chain-of-thought faithfulness study and “Building effective agents”, and Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s tool-use documentation as of November 2026; every number below was checked against them · Read the main source ↗

It builds on Part 2 (a model writes one token per lap, and never goes back) and Part 9 (each lap is one full pass through the model). It also leans on Part 13, “From autocomplete to assistant”, and Part 14, “Memory and the context window”. Facts carry a tag: public means a published paper, documentation or plain arithmetic, example means a made-up or rounded number used to show how something works, and not public means the company has not said. The problem

Every token gets the same effort

Part 2 showed the loop: one full pass through the model writes one token. Part 9 showed that a pass always does the same sums, whatever the question. So “2 + 2 =” and a tricky word problem get exactly the same effort for each token of the answer.

Here is the word problem from the chain-of-thought paper’s first figure: “The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought 6 more, how many apples do they have?” Asked to answer straight away, Google’s PaLM model wrote “The answer is 27.” The right answer is 9 public. This apples problem runs through the whole page.

The analogy

Working on paper, not in your head

Try 47 × 36 in your head, all at once. Hard. On paper it is easy: write 47 × 6 = 282, then 47 × 30 = 1,410, then add. Each line is simple, and each one uses the lines above it.

Mapping it back: the paper is the text the model has written so far, which every new pass reads (Part 2). Writing a line is spending passes. Where the analogy breaks: the model has no separate scrap paper. Its working is ordinary tokens in the same stream as the answer, written one at a time, with no eraser.

The mechanism

Each written step buys another pass

Chain-of-thought prompting adds worked examples to the prompt, so the model copies the habit of showing its working public. On the apples problem PaLM then wrote: “They used 20 to make lunch. So they had 23 − 20 = 3. They bought 6 more apples, so they have 3 + 6 = 9.” public

Nothing inside the model changed. It simply wrote about ten times as many tokens, so it ran about ten times as many passes before committing to a number, and each step’s result, like the 3, was sitting in the text for the next pass to read. The token counts are rounded example.

The evidence

Showing the working tripled the score

GSM8K is a test of 1,319 grade-school maths word problems like the apples one public. Wei and colleagues asked PaLM at three sizes, with eight examples in the prompt, answered directly or with working public:

The biggest model went from 17.9% to 56.9%. The smallest got slightly worse: the paper calls chain of thought an ability that appears only at sufficient scale, since smaller models wrote “fluent but illogical” chains public. Part 11, “Scale: why bigger works”, has more on skills that appear with size. The purple bar is a first glimpse of tools: chapter 10.

Go deeper: the other models in the paper

The same table has GPT-3 175B going from 15.6% to 46.9% and Codex from 19.7% to 63.1% on GSM8K public. The “+ calculator” rows run every equation in the written working through Python after the fact and paste the true result back in; for PaLM 540B that adds 1.7 points, to 58.6% public. DeepSeek’s R1 paper points out that a bare instruction, “Let’s think step by step”, with no examples at all, also triggers the habit (Kojima and others, 2022) public.

More of the same

Ask many times, take the vote

Part 2’s weighted die means the same question can produce different working each time. Self-consistency uses that on purpose: sample many chains of working, ignore the working, and take the answer most of them reach public. Wrong chains tend to scatter; right ones tend to agree.

With 40 sampled chains, PaLM 540B rose from 56.5% to 74.4% on GSM8K public. The cost is plain: 40 chains are 40 times the tokens. The authors suggest 5 or 10 chains get most of the gain, since accuracy levels off quickly public. The five chains drawn here are made up example.

Go deeper: why 56.5 here and 56.9 before

The self-consistency paper reran plain chain of thought, always picking the favourite token (Part 2’s greedy picking), and got 56.5% for PaLM 540B, slightly below the 56.9% in the chain-of-thought paper public. The +17.9 points in its abstract are measured from its own 56.5.

Reasoning models

Reward the right answer, not the steps

Prompting borrows a habit. Reasoning models are trained into it. DeepSeek’s recipe for R1-Zero: give the model maths and coding problems with checkable answers, let it write freely between <think> tags, then reward only a correct final answer, checked by a rule or a compiler public.

This is reinforcement learning: try, get scored, and nudge (Part 6) the numbers towards whatever scored well. Part 13 used the same idea with people’s preferences as the score; here a rule checks the answer. On AIME 2024, a hard US maths contest, R1-Zero rose from 15.6% to 77.9%, and 86.7% with a vote public.

The surprise

Nobody taught it to say “wait”

Nobody showed R1-Zero how to reason. Yet its answers grew longer as training went on, from hundreds to thousands of tokens, and it began to check its own steps, try other approaches, and backtrack public. DeepSeek printed one moment where a draft stopped itself: “Wait, wait. Wait. That’s an aha moment I can flag here.” public

Part 2 said a written token stays. That is still true here: the mistaken stretch is never erased. What the model learned is to write “wait”, step back in its own text, and carry on from a better place. The final answer after the thinking is what gets scored.

The trade

Thinking longer scores higher

OpenAI announced o1 in September 2024, a model “trained with reinforcement learning to perform complex reasoning”, and reported that its performance “consistently improves” with more training and with more time spent thinking public. Its own AIME 2024 numbers:

GPT-4o, which answers without a long think, averaged 12%. o1 averaged 74% with one try, 83% with a vote of 64 tries, and 93% when a learned scoring model picked from 1,000 tries public. Each gain costs more tokens: 64 or 1,000 full attempts per problem instead of one. OpenAI has not said how many tokens o1 thinks per problem not public.

Go deeper: two GPT-4o numbers

The post’s text gives GPT-4o 12% (1.8 of 15 problems); the appendix table on the same page gives 9.3% for one try and 13.4% for a vote of 64 public. The drawing uses the text’s figures for all four bars.

How much thinking

Thousands of tokens, more for harder problems

DeepSeek did publish token counts. On a set of 2024 maths-contest problems, R1 solved 61.8% while thinking 8,793 tokens per problem on average. GPT-4o solved 24.7% writing 711 tokens public. Drawn on one scale:

R1 sets its own effort: under 100 tokens for “1 + 1”, under 7,000 for easy contest problems, over 18,000 for the hardest public. DeepSeek also reports overthinking, too many tokens on simple questions public. Voting is not a substitute: GPT-4o with a vote of 64 rose only from 9.3% to 13.4% on AIME public.

The hidden cost

You pay for thinking you never see

Every thinking token is a full pass, like any other token. As of November 2026, Anthropic’s documentation says thinking tokens “are billed as output tokens, even when the thinking text isn’t returned”, and that what you see “is never the raw chain of thought”, only a summary public.

OpenAI made the same choice for o1, showing users a model-written summary of the chain of thought rather than the raw text public. The split drawn here uses R1’s average thinking length with a made-up 400-token answer and 300-token summary example. How long a given commercial model thinks on your question is usually not shown not public.

Tools

Some jobs belong to a program

Thinking helps, but a model is still a guesser of likely text. Toolformer’s authors put it bluntly: models “struggle with basic functionality, such as arithmetic or factual lookup, where much simpler and smaller models excel” public. So let the model ask a program, in the middle of its text:

That line is from the Toolformer paper. The model writes a call in a fixed format, a program runs it, and the result is pasted into the text. Then the next pass reads the true number instead of guessing one public. Toolformer learned when calls help by keeping only those that made the following words easier to predict public.

The mechanism

The model only writes the request

Today’s models work the same way, through the app that calls them. Anthropic’s documentation, as of November 2026: “The model never runs anything on its own. It emits a structured request, your code (or Anthropic’s servers) runs the operation, and the result flows back into the conversation.” public

Each round trip is a new request carrying the whole conversation so far. OpenAI’s guide describes the same five steps: send tools, receive a call, run it, send the output, receive the answer or more calls public. Where the program runs is the main difference between tools: in your app, or on the provider’s servers.

The evidence

A small model with a calculator wins

Toolformer is GPT-J, a 6.7-billion-number model, fine-tuned on text with useful calls inserted public. On ASDiv, another set of maths word problems, it was compared with the same model without calls and with GPT-3, 26 times larger public:

With the calculator it scored 40.4%, nearly three times GPT-3’s 14.0%. It chose to call the calculator for 97.9% of these problems public. Even with calls switched off it beat plain GPT-J, probably from training on so many worked results, the authors suggest public.

Agents

Think, act, look, and go round again

Put thinking and tools in a loop and you get an agent. ReAct (Yao and others, 2022) made the loop explicit: the model writes a thought, then an action, then the result of the action is pasted in as an observation, and it goes round again until it writes Finish public. A real trace from the paper:

The first search did not say who Milhouse was named after, so the second lap looked inside the page for “named after” and found it public. Anthropic describes today’s agents as “typically just LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop” public.

Go deeper: plan, act, observe, repeat

Anthropic separates workflows, where a program fixes the order of model calls and tools in advance, from agents, “where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage”. It recommends agents for open-ended problems “where it’s difficult or impossible to predict the required number of steps”, with stopping conditions such as a maximum number of laps public. Thoughts in the loop often make a plan first; the observations then show whether the plan is working.

The evidence

Looking it up stops the inventing

The ReAct team read 50 failed answers from each method on HotpotQA, a test of questions that need two or more Wikipedia pages public. Thinking alone failed mostly by inventing facts, the subject of Part 15, “Why models make things up”. With a search tool in the loop, invented facts vanished from the failures:

The failures moved, though: 47% were reasoning errors, including going round in circles repeating the same action, and 23% came from searches that returned nothing useful public. In two game-like tests, ALFWorld and WebShop, ReAct beat imitation-learning and reinforcement-learning methods by 34 and 10 points of success rate public.

The common mistake

95% per step is not 95% overall

The natural guess: if each step goes right 95% of the time, the agent is 95% reliable. But a task succeeds only if every step does. Twenty steps at 95% each is 0.95 multiplied by itself 20 times: 36% public, plain arithmetic.

Anthropic warns that agents’ autonomy brings “higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors” public. The curves assume each step fails independently and any failure sinks the task example. Real steps are not independent, and some mistakes can be noticed and fixed, which is what the lab explores.

The evidence

Longer tasks, lower success

METR, an AI evaluation group, timed skilled people on 170 software and research tasks, then measured how long a task an AI agent can finish half the time: its time horizon public. For frontier models such as OpenAI’s o3, that is about 110 minutes public.

Ask for 80% success instead of 50% and the horizon is 4 to 6 times shorter: these models “cannot reliably perform tasks of moderate length” public. Still, the 50% horizon has doubled about every seven months since 2019 public. The 80% bar here is 110 divided by 4 to 6 example.

Where agents fail

Fewer loops, more giving up early

METR also read 31 failed runs by GPT-4 (November 2023 version) and 32 by o1, and sorted each into one cause public. The older model most often repeated an action that kept failing. The reasoning model rarely did that, but often stopped too soon:

Half of o1’s failures were abandoning the task early, often submitting without checking or before reading everything needed public. METR notes o1 was failing on harder tasks than GPT-4, so the two columns are not like for like public. Its agents also struggled in “messier” settings without clear feedback public.

Try it

Run an agent on a long task

A task of many steps. Each step goes right on the first try with some chance. After each step a check can run, such as tests or a search result, and catches a wrong step with some chance. A caught step is retried up to your limit; if retries run out, the agent stops and says so example.

Three things to try. Lengthen the task with no checks and watch the white bar collapse. Add a strong check with two retries and most of it comes back, for some extra tokens. Then weaken the check: errors it misses slip through as confident wrong answers, the rose bar, which is worse than an agent that stops and says it is stuck.

Go deeper: what the lab assumes

Steps fail independently with the same chance, and a missed error sinks the task. A check never rejects a correct step. Each attempt costs 1,500 tokens and each check 500, made-up round numbers example. With first-try chance p, catch chance c and r retries, a step ends right with chance p × (1 + q + … + qr), where q = (1 − p) × c, and the whole task with that chance raised to the number of steps.

The other limit

Every lap re-reads the whole history

An agent’s loop only ever adds to its context: every thought, call and result stays in the text. And each new request carries all of it (chapter 12). So lap 20 reads everything from laps 1 to 19 before writing a token, and the total read grows much faster than the laps.

Twenty laps adding 5,000 tokens each read 1,050,000 tokens in all, and the last lap alone reads 100,000 example. Long runs also hit the context limit, the subject of Part 14, “Memory and the context window”. As of November 2026, Anthropic’s documentation offers compaction and context editing to summarise or drop old laps public.

The common mistake

The thinking is not a window inside

It is tempting to read a model’s thinking as an honest record of why it answered. Anthropic tested this by slipping hints into questions. When a model used a hint to change its answer, how often did its thinking say so public?

Only 25% of the time for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39% for DeepSeek R1, and less for hints that invited bad behaviour public. Thinking tokens are tokens that help the model get answers right, not a guaranteed confession. The authors conclude that watching the thinking helps catch problems, but cannot rule them out public.

The whole picture

Spend tokens, borrow tools, check the world

Everything in this part is a way to get more out of the same model by writing more text: working it out, sampling and voting, and calling programs whose results come back as text.

The loop is what makes an agent, and also what makes it fragile: small per-step slips multiply, every lap costs more than the last, and the thinking you read may not tell the whole story. The best defence is evidence from outside the model, such as tests, searches and code that actually ran.

The hand-off

Next: the chips all this thinking runs on

Thinking trades tokens for accuracy, and every token is a full pass of arithmetic. At Part 9’s count of about 15 billion sums per token for Llama 3 8B, R1’s 8,793 thinking tokens would be about 130 trillion sums for one answer, against about 11 trillion for GPT-4o’s 711 tokens example.

All of it lands on chips. Part 17, “How chips are made”, back on the chip track, shows where those chips come from: silicon, light, and the factories the whole industry depends on.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A model thinks by writing: more tokens of working buy more passes, tools put true results into the text, and an agent is that loop run until done. Over many steps, small slips multiply, so checks from the real world matter most.
