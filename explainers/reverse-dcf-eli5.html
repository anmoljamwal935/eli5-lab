Reverse DCF

A DCF turns a growth forecast into a value. A reverse DCF runs the same sums backwards: it starts from today’s price and finds the growth that price already assumes. Then the question becomes one you can check: is that growth believable?

The problem

The forecast is the weakest link

Part 24 valued Ramu at ₹76.7 a share example. The market price is ₹100. The gap comes almost entirely from one guess: growth of 12% a year for FY27–FY31, 8% for FY32–FY36 and 5% for ever after. Change the guess and the value moves a lot. Nobody knows Ramu’s next ten years.

So the honest question is not “what is it worth?”, which needs a forecast you cannot check. It is “what does ₹100 already assume?”, which needs only the price and the same model.

The idea

Read the same curve backwards

Hold everything in Part 24’s model fixed except one growth rate for FY27–FY36. Each rate gives one value a share, so the model draws a curve. A DCF reads it forward: choose growth, get value. A reverse DCF reads it backward: start at the price, go across to the curve, and down to the growth example.

One steady rate of 10.2% a year gives the same ₹76.7 as Part 24’s 12%-then-8% path. To reach ₹100 the rate must be 15.3% a year for ten years, then 5% for ever. That is the implied growth: not a forecast, but the growth the buyer at ₹100 is paying for.

The catch

Growth is paid for with reinvestment

Growth is not free. To grow, Ramu must put part of its after-tax operating profit (NOPAT) back into machines, cafés and stock. Part 24’s rule: if new money earns 20% example, growing NOPAT by g needs g ÷ 20% of it reinvested. What is left is free cash flow to the firm (FCFF).

FCFF = NOPAT × (1 − growth ÷ 20%) · FY26 NOPAT = EBIT 73 × (1 − 25%) = 54.75

Faster growth means bigger profits later but less cash now. At 15.3% growth, FY27’s FCFF is only ₹14.8 cr of ₹63.1 cr NOPAT. At 20% every rupee is reinvested. That trade-off is why the curve rises steadily rather than shooting up.

How to solve it

Guess, check, and halve the range

There is no neat formula that turns ₹100 back into a growth rate, but a computer, or a patient person, can search. Start with a range that must contain the answer, say 0% to 20%. Try the middle, value the share, and keep the half where ₹100 still lies example.

This is called bisection. Each try halves the range, so twenty tries pin the rate to within a hundred-thousandth of a percentage point. It works because value only ever rises with growth in this model. The lab below runs the same search every time you move a slider.

Go deeper: when growth adds no value

Value rises with growth here because new money earns 20%, more than the 11.6% it costs. If new money earned exactly the WACC, extra growth would add nothing, because each rupee reinvested would be worth just one rupee. If it earned less, growth would destroy value, and a higher price could not be explained by growth at all. A reverse DCF quietly assumes the first case; check that the business really earns more than its cost of capital (Part 10).

What 15.3% means

Ten years, four times the profit

At 15.3% a year, NOPAT grows from ₹54.75 cr in FY26 to ₹227.4 cr in FY36, 4.2 times as much. Part 24’s path reaches ₹141.8 cr. And more of the value now sits far away: 85% of the enterprise value (EV) comes from the years after FY36, against 68% in Part 24 example.

Go deeper: the sums at 15.3%

Present value of FCFF for FY27–FY36 at 11.6%: ₹154.5 cr. FCFF in FY37 = FY36 NOPAT × 1.05 × (1 − 5% ÷ 20%) = ₹179.08 cr. Terminal value at FY36 = 179.08 ÷ (11.6% − 5%) = ₹2,713.4 cr, worth ₹905.5 cr today. EV = 154.5 + 905.5 = ₹1,060 cr. Less net debt of ₹60 cr gives equity of ₹1,000 cr, or ₹100 on 10 crore shares.

Is it believable?

Against Ramu’s own record

Now compare the 15.3% with what Ramu has done. From FY22 to FY26 its sales grew 11.0% a year and its profit after tax 13.6% (Part 9) example. The price asks for faster growth than either, for twice as long.

Part of the 13.6% came from a margin that rose from 9.1% to 10% of sales, which cannot repeat for ever. A second check uses the market: Ramu’s ₹300 cr of packaged tea is 2.4% of a ₹12,500 cr regional market growing 8% a year (Part 17, “Industry structure and moats”). If packaged tea grows with NOPAT, Ramu needs about 4.6% of that market by FY36.

Neither check says ₹100 is wrong. They say what you must believe to pay it: roughly double the market share, without the margin slipping, while still earning 20% on new money.

Another story

Same price, a different shape

One equation can only solve for one unknown. Keep Part 24’s 8% for FY32–FY36 and 5% after, and solve only for FY27–FY31: the price then needs 21.9% a year for five years example. That is faster than the 20% that new money earns.

Growing faster than the return on new money means reinvesting more than all of the profit: 110% of NOPAT. FCFF turns negative, from −₹6.4 cr in FY27 to −₹14.1 cr in FY31, and the gap must be raised from lenders or shareholders. Both paths give ₹100; they are different businesses.

The common mistake

Implied growth depends on the rate

It is tempting to read 15.3% as “the market’s forecast”. It is not. It is the growth that fits ₹100 if investors discount at 11.6%, expect 5% after FY36, and believe new money earns 20%. Change the discount rate by one point and the implied growth moves about four or five example.

At a WACC of 10% the same price implies only 8.8% growth; at 13% it implies 21.7%. A reverse DCF does not remove judgement. It moves it to places you can argue about: the discount rate (Part 23), the return on new money, and whether the business can grow that fast.

Every number on this page comes from one simple model with a constant 20% return on new money and two growth stages. A real reverse DCF should also test margins, the length of the fast-growth period and the cost of capital.

So what

Questions to ask of any price

  1. What growth is in the price? Solve for it with a model you understand, and write the number down.
  2. Has the company ever grown that fast? Compare with its own record over several years, not one good year.
  3. Is there room? Turn the growth into market share, and ask who would lose it.
  4. Does new money earn more than it costs? If not, growth cannot justify any price.
  5. How much hangs on the discount rate? Move it a point each way before you conclude anything.

A reverse DCF asks what one price assumes. Part 26, “Relative valuation and P/E bands”, asks the other everyday question: is this price high or low against similar companies, and against the company’s own history?

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Don’t start by guessing what a share is worth: ask what growth today’s price already assumes, then check that number against the company’s record, its market and its returns.
