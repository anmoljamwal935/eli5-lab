Consumer companies

A soap maker and a supermarket chain both sell everyday things, so their sales are easy to see and hard to read. Split growth into more units, higher prices and a richer mix; for a retailer, into old stores and new ones. Then see why the market pays so much for steady demand.

The problem

Same growth, two different years

Krishna’s sales rose from ₹1,000 cr to ₹1,087.7 cr in FY26, up 8.8% example. A twin company grew by exactly as much. But Krishna sold more packs, and more of its dearer ones; the twin sold the same number of packs and simply charged more for each.

The headline is identical; the business is not. Volume is demand: more homes using the product. Price is pricing power, and it often just passes on costlier palm oil or packaging, which can reverse. Mix is customers trading up. Analysts of consumer companies ask first: how much of the growth was volume?

The building block

Sales are an area: packs × price

Draw sales as a rectangle: its width is the packs sold, its height the average price of a pack. Krishna sold 90 crore packs in FY25 at an average ₹11.11 example: ₹1,000 cr. In FY26 the rectangle grew a little wider (93.6 crore packs, +4%) and a little taller (₹11.62).

sales growth = (1 + volume growth) × (1 + average-price growth) − 1 = 1.04 × 1.0458 − 1 = 8.8%

Two things follow. Growth multiplies, so 4% volume and 4.6% price give 8.8%, not 8.6%. And the thin strip on top is two things at once: the price of each pack, and which packs people bought. The next panel pulls those apart.

Mix

Trading up lifts the average price

Krishna sells a regular pack at ₹10 and a premium pack at ₹20 example. In FY26 regular packs rose 2% (80 to 81.6 crore) and premium packs 20% (10 to 12 crore). Even at FY25’s prices, the average pack is now worth ₹11.28, not ₹11.11. That gain is mix.

So the 4.6% rise in the average price is 1.5% mix (1,056 ÷ 1,040) and 3% price (Krishna raised both packs by 3%, so 1,087.7 ÷ 1,056). Mix can run the other way: when budgets are tight, buyers shift to small packs and cheaper variants, and the average falls even if no price changed.

Go deeper: the three effects in rupees

Volume effect = FY25 average price × extra packs = 11.11 × 3.6 = ₹40 cr. Mix effect = FY26 packs at FY25 prices − FY26 packs at FY25’s average = 1,056 − 1,040 = ₹16 cr. Price effect = FY26 sales − FY26 packs at FY25 prices = 1,087.68 − 1,056 = ₹31.68 cr. The three add to ₹87.68 cr. The order matters a little (price measured on new volumes); companies choose their own method and rarely print it, so compare a company with itself over time.

Where price hides

Price can hide in the grams

Indian shoppers buy many products at fixed price points: the ₹5 or ₹10 pack. To raise prices without changing the sticker, companies change the pack. Shrink a ₹10 pack from 100 g to 90 g and the price per kilo rises 11% example, while the pack count stays the same.

This is why good disclosures count volume in tonnes or litres, not in packs, and why “grammage” is a price lever. It works in reverse too: when GST rates or commodity costs fall, a company may keep the ₹10 price and add grams, a price cut that a pack count never shows.

Rural and urban

Two Indias, two speeds of demand

About a third of Krishna’s packs go to villages and small towns example: 30 crore of 90. In FY26 rural packs rose 8% and urban 2%, so rural, a third of the volume, supplied two-thirds of the 3.6 crore extra packs. After a weak monsoon, rural falls 4% instead and the total does not grow at all.

Rural demand rides on the monsoon, crop prices, farm wages and food inflation, so it swings more; urban demand rides on jobs and salaries, and increasingly shifts to supermarkets and quick-commerce apps. Companies rarely print rural and urban growth as numbers. They describe it in the management discussion, so read the words for direction and the volume figure for size.

The common mistake

Slower sales, stronger demand

Suppose that next year commodity costs fall and Krishna cuts prices 3%, while regular packs rise 8% and premium 10% example. Sales growth slows from 8.8% to 5.1%. A headline reader sees a slowdown. In fact Krishna sold 8.2% more packs, twice FY26’s volume growth.

The reverse trap is worse: in an inflation year a company can post double-digit sales growth with flat or falling volumes, and that growth vanishes when costs fall. HUL’s Home Care business did the first thing in FY26: high-single-digit volume growth, but only 4% sales growth after price cuts public (the HUL panel below).

Why the P/E runs high

Steady demand, high returns, long runway

Part 26, “Relative valuation and P/E bands”, showed that a P/E is a DCF in shorthand. In the simplest version, for a company growing at a steady rate for ever, P/E on next year’s earnings = (1 − growth ÷ return on new money) ÷ (discount rate − growth). Consumer leaders score well on all three inputs.

Return on new money: a strong brand needs little capital to grow (HUL’s printed return on capital employed is 110.9% public), so almost all profit can be paid out while growth continues. Discount rate: people keep buying soap and tea in a slowdown, so profit is steady and investors accept a lower return. Runway: more homes, more categories, more premium packs. At a 10.5% discount rate example, HUL’s P/E of 45.4 is the price of about 8.5% growth for ever.

Go deeper: why a zero-growth company gets the same P/E either way

At zero growth both curves start at 1 ÷ 10.5% = 9.5×: if a company does not grow, the return it would earn on new money does not matter, because it invests none. Growth adds value only when new money earns more than the discount rate; at a 20% return it adds some, at 100% it adds a lot. The formula is the steady-state case of Part 24’s DCF, “DCF, step by step”, and it breaks when growth reaches the discount rate.

The catch

A high P/E has far to fall

The same curve is steep at the top. Drop the growth belief from 8.5% to 7.5% and the justified P/E falls from 45.4 to 30.8 example, a third off the price with no change in this year’s profit. HUL shows the gentler real version public: EPS rose from ₹37.77 to ₹45.25 over four years, the share price went nowhere, and the P/E fell.

EPS grew about 4.6% a year from FY22 to FY26, slower than the growth a P/E above 50 had assumed, so the multiple shrank as fast as earnings grew (Part 26’s re-rating, in reverse). A great company bought at a price that assumes too much can still be a poor investment for years.

Go deeper: which EPS?

These are HUL’s consolidated EPS from continuing operations, as printed in its long-term track record (p. 18), and the BSE closing prices it prints (p. 19). FY26 total EPS was ₹64.01, because it includes a ₹4,611 cr accounting gain on demerging the ice-cream business into Kwality Wall’s (India) Ltd on 1 December 2025 (p. 443). On that figure the P/E would look like 32.1: Part 7’s one-off trap.

Retail

A retailer grows two ways

Sabarmati Mart runs 100 supermarkets of 25,000 sq ft each, 2.5 million sq ft, with FY25 sales of ₹8,000 cr example. In FY26 its old stores sell 8% more, the like-for-like (LFL) or same-store growth. It also opens 20 stores, which trade for half the year on average at 75% of a mature store’s rate.

Sales rise 15.5% to ₹9,240 cr: 8 points from LFL and 7.5 from the ₹600 cr the new stores (the thin pale bar) add. The two halves mean different things. LFL is demand and execution in stores the market already knows. Store additions are capital spent on land, buildings and stock; they pay off only if the new stores mature, so watch how many open each year and how fast they reach the old stores’ sales.

Retail productivity

Sales per square foot, and which feet

The retailer’s productivity number is sales per square foot of selling area. Sabarmati’s FY25 figure is ₹32,000 (₹8,000 cr ÷ 2.5 million sq ft) example. For FY26 the answer depends on which floor area goes underneath.

Same year, same chain: −3.75%, +5% or +8%. A fast-expanding chain looks less productive on year-end area because its newest stores are counted in full but traded for only part of the year, and are still ramping up. So check which area a company divides by, compare a chain with itself, and read the figure next to LFL and store additions.

So what

Questions before buying a consumer company

  1. How much of the growth was volume? Split sales into volume, price and mix, and look at several years.
  2. Was the price real pricing power? Or costs passed on that will reverse, or grams quietly taken out?
  3. Where is demand coming from? Rural or urban, old channels or new ones, mass or premium.
  4. For a retailer: old stores or new ones? LFL, store additions, and sales per square foot on a stated area.
  5. What does the P/E assume? Turn it into years of growth, and ask what happens if growth is one point lower.

Consumer companies are prized because their demand barely moves with the economy. Part 33, “Cyclicals”, turns to cement, steel and car makers, whose volumes and prices swing hard with the cycle, so that a low P/E can be the moment to sell and a high one the moment to buy.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Sales are packs times price: split every year’s growth into volume, price and mix, and every retailer’s into old stores and new ones, then ask whether the high P/E of steady demand still matches the growth it assumes.
