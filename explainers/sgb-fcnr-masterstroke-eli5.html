Gold bonds, dollar swaps and the RBI “masterstroke”

With gold bonds, the government promised to repay in gold, and it lost money when gold soared. The 2026 dollar swap is the same kind of bet on the US dollar, which the RBI can actually influence. A video calls it a masterstroke. Here is how both bets work, and which of the video's claims hold up.

The question

Should 20% move from shares to bonds?

The video retells a talk with a worried industrialist friend. His fears: bond yields, unrest, foreign selling, and costly dollar deposits. He is offered 11–12% on company bonds and wants to shift 15–25% of his shares there. Top-rated company bonds were paying far less (fund-house tracker, 25 Sep 2026), so 11–12% means a weaker borrower or a privately negotiated deal. The video admits big lenders can negotiate.

The building block

Money is a raw material too

The video's first answer: when every factory owner borrows to expand at once, the price of money rises. In the year to March 2026, bank loans grew faster than deposits (16% vs 13.4%). Part of the reason, the video says, is that savers moved money from deposits into shares and SIPs. Rates that are high because demand is strong are different from the early 1990s, when rates were high because inflation was high and growth was weak.

The video's example

Power plants looked crazy, then paid off

The video's example: plants built with huge borrowing around 2008–10 looked reckless then, but they turned a power-short India into one that now meets peak demand. Official data show shortages almost gone (Ministry of Power, PIB). The video also says those plants were all privately built, and that the same plant would cost 10× today. Both are figures cited in the video that I did not verify.

What actually happens

The swap: dollars in, rupees out, reversed later

The video uses this example. An NRI deposits $1,000 for 3–5 years. The bank sells those dollars to the RBI and gets rupees at that day's rate (the video uses ₹95). At maturity it buys the same dollars back at the same rate (RBI circular, 8 June 2026). The bank lends the rupees in between and never pays to hedge against the rupee falling.

The real numbers

Far bigger than the video says

The video gives the total as “₹15 lakh crore” in one place and “₹1.5 lakh crore” in another. RBI data: $132.98 billion of these deposits by 31 August 2026 (RBI, 21 Sep 2026), about ₹12.6 lakh crore at ₹95. Savers earned about 6.25–6.75% in dollars (bank rates, Aug 2026), not the friend's 11–12%. The RBI's free hedge is what let banks pay that much.

The comparison

Gold bonds: a bet that gold wouldn't soar

Sovereign Gold Bonds took your rupees at that day's gold price and promised to repay the price of the same grams years later, plus 2.5–2.75% interest. The first issue sold at ₹2,684 a gram and paid back ₹6,132 at maturity in 2023. One gram redeemed in September 2026 paid ₹15,384. For buyers it was a masterstroke; the video's host says he urged everyone to buy, and retirees later thanked him. For the government it was what the video calls a short on gold: it owed rising grams it didn't hold. 146.96 tonnes were sold, and 130 tonnes were still outstanding in March 2025 (minister's reply to Parliament).

The surprise

Gold didn't return 2–3% a year

The video says gold's long-run average return is 2–3% a year and that the last two years were a freak. At 3% a year, the 2015 gram would be worth ₹3,690 today. It actually paid ₹15,384, about 17.6% a year, using the two RBI prices above. The last two years were indeed extreme: the RBI's price went from ₹7,196 in September 2024 to ₹15,384, about 2.1×. That is “doubled”, not “tripled”.

The common mistake

The swap pays only if dollars are spent

The video says the RBI “shorted” the dollar at ₹95: if the rupee strengthens to ₹60 by 2031, the RBI books a profit. That is true only if the RBI has sold the dollars in the meantime, for example to defend the rupee. If it simply keeps them in reserves, it hands back the same dollars and the exchange rate doesn't matter. The RBI did spend some: reserves fell by $51 billion in four weeks (Business Today, Oct 2026).

The RBI took on this bet by offering banks a free hedge. A market forward contract would have priced in the gap between Indian and US interest rates. The video's “masterstroke” profit therefore depends on two things: how many swap dollars the RBI spends, and where the rupee is when the swaps mature in 2029–31.

What actually happened

2026: the rupee's defence, in order

The video describes a chain of moves after the swap: the rupee dipped and climbed again, so the RBI opened a dollar window for oil companies, raised the CRR, and cracked down on gold and silver. The real order is different. The gold curbs came before the swap (World Gold Council). The oil-company window came in October (Business Today). I found no CRR hike: a fund-house tracker shows the CRR lower, at 3.00%.

Checking the data

The external gap widened in 2026

The video says balance-of-payments data come out every Friday, and they show the trade gap closing while capital inflows stay positive. The Friday data are actually forex reserves. Balance-of-payments figures are quarterly. The latest quarter (RBI, April–June 2026) shows a wider current-account deficit, which is mostly the trade gap. Net capital inflows did stay positive, though smaller, and foreign portfolio money left.

The claim

“Global trade no longer runs on dollars”

The video says de-dollarisation has already happened and the dollar will collapse by 2031. The most recent global survey, from April 2025, found the dollar on one side of 89% of all currency trades (BIS Triennial Survey). Each trade has two currencies, so the shares add up to 200%. The collapse is a prediction; the “already happened” part is wrong.

The video's reasons

Why the video expects more dollars

The video's reasons for a stronger rupee: weapons sales (Make in India), refined fuel exports, and limits on gold imports. Defence exports beat the video's guess, at ₹38,424 crore in FY26 (Defence Ministry via ETV Bharat). The claim that India is the EU's third-largest arms supplier is unverified. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio did visit Jamnagar to ask Reliance to refine Venezuelan oil (Al Jazeera). But India is not the only country that can refine it: US Gulf Coast refiners such as Valero run it too (PGJ).

The analogy

“India today is China in 2008”

The video's big analogy: in 2008 China was the world's third-largest economy, and its stock market rose while every other market fell. India is about to be third, and an AI-bubble crash in the US would help it the same way. The market part is backwards. Shanghai shares lost 65% in 2008, the worst in Asia, even though China's economy grew about 9% (NUS East Asian Institute). India is third by purchasing power but sixth in dollar terms in the IMF's October 2026 numbers (summary). The video says fifth.

So what

Three questions to ask next time

  1. Is that yield normal for this borrower? Compare an 11–12% offer with AAA (7.8%) and AA (8.56%) bonds. The extra few percent pays you for extra risk.
  2. Has the RBI actually spent the swap dollars? Watch the weekly reserves (Fridays) and the RBI's forward book. Only dollars that have been sold turn the swap into the bet the video describes.
  3. Is a claim data or a forecast? The swap's terms and the gold-bond prices can be checked. “The rupee peaks at 97” and “India is China in 2008” cannot be checked yet.

Gold bonds and the swap are opposite bets. Gold bonds cost the government when gold rose. The swap pays the RBI only if it sold the dollars and the rupee later strengthens. Nothing here is investment advice.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A promise to repay in something you don't hold is a bet on its price. Gold bonds lost that bet when gold soared. The dollar swap wins only on the dollars the RBI actually spends.
