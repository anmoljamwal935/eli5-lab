Screening for ideas

A screen is a set of lines drawn on ratios you already know. It turns hundreds of companies into a short list worth reading. It cannot tell a bargain from a company that is cheap for a reason, and a backtest that only sees today’s survivors flatters every screen.

The problem

Too many companies, one reader

Reading one company properly means its annual report, its filings and its business: days of work, as Part 37 will show. Our made-up market has 30 companies, from ₹180 cr of sales to ₹2,600 cr example. A real exchange lists far more. Nobody reads them all.

A screen is a cheap first pass. It asks every company the same few questions in numbers, drops the ones that fail, and leaves a short list to read. It saves time. It does not do the reading.

The building block

A filter is a line on one ratio

Take ROCE, profit before interest and tax divided by all the capital, equity plus debt (Part 10). Put every company on one axis and draw a line at 15%. Those at or above it go on; those below drop out example.

Every filter works this way: one ratio, one direction, one line. The line is a choice, not a fact. Nothing magic happens at 15%; a company at 14.9% is not worse than one at 15.1%.

How filters stack

Five lines, one short list

A screen joins filters with AND: a company must pass every one. This page’s default asks five questions from earlier parts example. Each filter is applied to what the last one left, so the list only shrinks.

Thirty become 12. The order changes the bars in between, not the end: AND does not care which question is asked first. Each extra filter cuts the list again, so five moderate lines can be stricter than one harsh one.

Go deeper: what a screen cannot hold

Banks and NBFCs are left out: debt is their raw material, and ROCE and debt ÷ equity mean little for them (Part 29). A loss-maker has no meaningful P/E or cash ratio (Part 35); this page counts “not meaningful” as a fail, never as a good score. Screener websites, such as Screener.in, Tickertape or Trendlyne, let you write filters like these on real data. They are named here as tools only: no figure on this page comes from them, and their definitions can differ from the series’ (Part 15).

Where to draw the line

Move one line, the list changes

Hold four filters still and slide the ROCE line example. With no ROCE line at all, 13 companies pass; at 15%, 12; above 26%, only one. Near the default, a point either way swaps companies in and out.

Eight companies miss the default screen by a single filter: Bijli Cables and Zafran Spices on cash, Dhoop Pumps on debt, Jharokha Windows on ROCE, Pankha Fans and Lekhni Pens on growth, and Gulmohar Pharma and Rangoli Paints on price. A near miss deserves a look. A screen is a sieve, not a verdict.

Value traps

Cheap for a reason

A value trap looks cheap on the screen and stays cheap, or gets cheaper, because the numbers are about to change or cannot be trusted. Three kinds pass screens easily example.

  1. A cyclical at the peak. Record profit makes the P/E low just when profit is about to fall (Part 33). Pathar’s P/E is 8 on this year’s EPS and 32 on its six-year average.
  2. A falling business. The market is shrinking, so a low P/E prices a profit that will not last. One good year can lift it over a growth line.
  3. Shaky governance. Pledged promoter shares, money lent to the promoter’s family, an auditor who leaves early (Part 19, Part 20). Most of this is not in any ratio.

Each trap’s reason sits outside the screen’s columns: the cycle, the market, the people. That is why a stricter line rarely removes a trap; reading does.

Survivorship bias

The backtest that only sees survivors

A backtest runs a screen on old data and checks what its picks did since. If the database holds only companies listed today, the ones that went bust have vanished from it. Say a screen run five years ago picked ten companies example. Seven are still listed; three were delisted, worth little or nothing.

Tested on today’s list, the screen’s picks averaged +30% over five years. Counting all ten, they averaged −8%. The dead companies were often the cheapest-looking ones, so the bias flatters cheap screens most. Ask any backtest: does its data include companies that no longer exist?

Go deeper: the arithmetic

Survivors: (120 + 60 + 45 + 30 + 10 − 20 − 35) ÷ 7 = 210 ÷ 7 = +30%. All ten: (210 − 100 − 100 − 90) ÷ 10 = −80 ÷ 10 = −8%. Equal amounts in each pick, no dividends, all figures example. A delisted share is not always worth zero (a takeover also delists a company), so a fair backtest records what holders actually got.

    Go deeper: the assumptions behind the 30

    Every company has 10 crore shares. ROCE is EBIT ÷ (equity + borrowings) at the year end; debt ÷ equity is borrowings ÷ equity; growth is one year’s sales growth, FY26 over FY25; cash is CFO ÷ profit after tax; P/E is price ÷ EPS. Each ratio is judged as shown, rounded (ROCE, growth and P/E to one decimal, the others to two). A loss-maker’s P/E and cash ratio are “not meaningful” and fail. “Average EPS” is the six-year average for the four cyclicals (Pathar, Lohapur, Neem, Kagaz), known only with hindsight, as Part 33 warned. Pledges are the share of promoter holdings pledged. Traps are set by the page’s story, not computed: that is the point.

    The common mistake

    A pass list is not a buy list

    On the default screen 12 companies pass. Seven are traps example. Not one of the seven fails a filter; their problems are in the cycle, the market or the boardroom.

    Switching on the two extra checks removes four of the seven, the pledge and the cyclicals. Three remain: a governance problem the numbers do not show and two shrinking businesses. Only reading finds those. Treat every name on a screen as a question to answer, and the screen as the start of the work.

    The ROCE fail is a definition at work. TCPL bought companies, and the price paid above their book value sits in its capital as goodwill and other intangibles. On all capital the return is 8.55%; on the capital left once those are taken out, 34.3%. Neither is “the” ROCE. A screen that uses one will drop TCPL; a screen that uses the other will keep it.

    The P/E fail is the market’s view: 65 times FY26 earnings is a price that already expects a lot (Part 25 asks how much). A cheap-only screen would never show TCPL, and a quality-only screen with no price line would show it straight away. Neither tells you whether it is worth buying. That takes the full reading, which is the next part.

    So what

    Questions before trusting a screen

    1. Which definitions? ROCE with or without goodwill, one year’s growth or five, reported or adjusted EPS, and which price date (Part 15).
    2. Why is it cheap? Check the cycle, the market and the governance of every low-P/E passer before anything else.
    3. What just missed? Read the near misses; the line is a rule of thumb.
    4. Who is missing from the data? Any backtest of a screen must include the companies that died.

    A screen finds candidates. Part 37, “One company, start to finish”, takes one, TCPL, through everything in this series and ends in a one-page thesis. Part 38 then covers the portfolio, taxes and costs.

    AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
    A screen is a few lines drawn on ratios: it shortens the list, but the traps pass because their reason is outside the columns, and a backtest that forgets the dead flatters every screen. The screen asks the questions; reading answers them.
