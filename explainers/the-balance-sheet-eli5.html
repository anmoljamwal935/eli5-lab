The balance sheet

A balance sheet is a photograph of a company on one date: what it owns, what it owes, and what is left for its shareholders. The two sides always match, and you can watch why.

The snapshot

A photograph, not a film

A P&L is a film: it adds up a whole year of sales and costs, and Part 3 covers it. A balance sheet is a photograph of one instant, here the close of 31 March. Ramu Chai’s two photographs, a year apart, differ by whatever the year changed.

The one equation

Assets = liabilities + equity

On 31 March 2026 Ramu owns ₹400 cr of things example. It owes ₹150 cr to lenders, suppliers and others. What is left, ₹250 cr, belongs to shareholders: that is equity. One total, two views: what the company has, and who paid for it.

In India the layout is set by Schedule III of the Companies Act 2013, Division II for companies reporting under Ind AS public. Assets come first, non-current before current; then equity; then liabilities. TCPL’s statement, later on this page, follows it line by line.

Why it balances

Every event is written down twice

Equity is defined as assets minus liabilities, so the sides match by construction. And every real event changes two things by the same amount: buy a machine and cash falls as the machine appears. One pan never moves alone; a mismatch means a missing entry, not a real gap.

Left side: long-lived

Machines, work in progress, goodwill

Non-current assets will not turn into cash within twelve months or one operating cycle public. Ramu’s ₹220 cr of plant and machinery (PP&E) is shown after depreciation. Its ₹25 cr of capital work in progress (CWIP) is machines still being built; they move into PP&E once ready example.

Goodwill is what a buyer pays above the fair value of the assets it can name. If Ramu paid ₹30 cr for a masala brand whose nameable assets are worth ₹20 cr, ₹10 cr is goodwill example. Ramu has none today; TCPL has a lot.

Go deeper: what happens to goodwill after the deal

Goodwill is not amortised. TCPL tests it for impairment every year, by cash-generating unit public. Brands and other intangibles with a finite life are amortised over their lives, the story of Part 6 on accounting choices.

Left side: short-lived

Stock, customers who owe, cash

Current assets should turn into cash within twelve months or one operating cycle public. Ramu holds ₹55 cr of stock (inventory), ₹45 cr that customers owe it (trade receivables), ₹40 cr of cash and ₹15 cr of other current assets example. How long stock and dues sit is Part 12.

Right side: owners

Equity: share capital plus reserves

Share capital is face value times shares: ₹10 × 10 cr shares = ₹100 cr, a printed number and not the share price. Other equity, or reserves, is profit kept plus any premium paid above face value: ₹150 cr. Together, ₹250 cr example. In consolidated accounts, non-controlling interest adds the share of subsidiaries that outsiders own.

At ₹100 a share the market values Ramu at ₹1,000 cr, four times its book equity. That gap is the market’s view, not the accounts’; Part 14 covers P/B.

Profit flows in

Profit becomes reserves; dividends leave

Equity was ₹218 cr on 31 March 2025. Add FY26 profit after tax of ₹50 cr, the last line of the P&L. Subtract the FY25 dividend paid in FY26, ₹1.80 a share or ₹18 cr: 218 + 50 − 18 = 250. Only reserves move; share capital stays at ₹100 cr example.

Go deeper: the dividend that has not left yet

Ramu proposes ₹2 a share (₹20 cr) for FY26, to be paid in FY27 example. It is not subtracted yet: a final dividend recommended by the board needs shareholders’ approval at the AGM, as TCPL’s note on share capital says public. So Ramu’s FY26 balance sheet carries no dividend payable.

Right side: owed

Loans, suppliers and provisions

Ramu owes banks ₹100 cr, of which ₹70 cr is long-term and ₹30 cr falls due within a year. It owes suppliers ₹35 cr for tea and packaging bought on credit (trade payables). The last ₹15 cr is provisions and other liabilities example. A provision is a cost expected to arrive whose amount or timing is not yet fixed. Part 11 turns debt into ratios.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL’s balance sheet, same shape

Tata Consumer Products’ consolidated balance sheet at 31 March 2026 public uses the same layout. On one scale, goodwill alone is 34% of the ₹34,453 cr total, and with other intangibles 55%. Plant and work in progress are under 8%. Ramu is machine-heavy; TCPL is brand-heavy.

Goodwill rose ₹553 cr in FY26 with no new deal: ₹569.50 cr from translating overseas goodwill into rupees, less a ₹16.02 cr impairment in Bangladesh public. The big step came in FY25, when goodwill added through business combinations was ₹863.75 cr and the group paid ₹1,809 cr for subsidiaries public; its subsidiaries now include Capital Foods (75% owned) and Organic India (100%). Outsiders’ 25% of Capital Foods is most of the ₹1,401 cr of non-controlling interest.

Now find it in a real report

Cash against borrowings: where ₹2,978 cr comes from

The AGM presentation says net cash was ₹2,978 cr public. The balance sheet has no line with that name; it comes from the company’s own “adjusted net (cash)/debt” in its capital-management note: cash and bank deposits, plus current investments and inter-corporate loans, less all borrowings. It adds up to ₹2,977.49 cr; the presentation’s ₹2,978 cr is that figure rounded up.

Cash and bank deposits of ₹3,396.51 cr are the balance sheet’s cash and cash equivalents (₹3,046.57 cr) plus other bank balances (₹373.92 cr), less ₹23.98 cr in the unclaimed dividend account. Current investments are liquid mutual funds (₹868.20 cr); inter-corporate loans are ₹247.28 cr non-current plus ₹585.81 cr current deposits public. Lease liabilities of ₹699.20 cr are not counted as borrowings in this measure.

Go deeper: cash that is also debt

₹1,595.17 cr of the ₹1,633.78 cr of bank overdrafts is part of a cash-pooling arrangement, matched by an equal amount inside cash and cash equivalents public. Strip that pair out and borrowings are about ₹525 cr and cash about ₹1,451 cr, my arithmetic from the notes. Net cash does not change, but gross cash and gross debt both look bigger than they are.

The balance sheet alone, with no adjustments, shows cash and equivalents of ₹3,046.57 cr against borrowings of ₹2,120.31 cr: ₹926.26 cr more cash than debt.

Real figures are frozen at FY2026: TCPL’s audited Consolidated Financial Statements in its Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, and its AGM presentation of June 2026. Educational, not a recommendation.

So what

Three questions for any balance sheet

  1. What is it made of? Machines, goodwill, stock or cash decide how a company behaves.
  2. Who paid for it? Owners’ equity and lenders’ money carry very different obligations.
  3. What falls due within a year? Compare cash and stock with short-term loans and payables.

Ramu earned ₹50 cr in FY26, yet its cash went from ₹41 cr to ₹40 cr. A balance sheet shows where the money is parked, not how it moved. Part 5, “Cash flow, and how the three connect”, follows the money.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A balance sheet is a photograph of one date, and it always balances because every event moves two things by the same amount: what a company owns equals what it owes plus what is left for its owners.
