Margin of safety and scenarios

A valuation is a range, not a number. Test two guesses at once, tell a bull, a base and a bear story, weight them by how likely you think each is, and then buy only well below the result, because the biggest risk in any model is your own error.

The problem

₹76.7 is one point in a range

Part 24’s answer looks precise, to a tenth of a rupee. But every input was a judgement. Change two of them within reason and the value runs from ₹57.7 to ₹106.5; tell a gloomier or a sunnier story and it runs from ₹44.1 to ₹99.6; Part 26’s P/E band gives ₹92 to ₹138 example. Each method gives a range, and the ranges overlap.

So the useful questions are: how wide is the range, where does the price sit in it, and how far below it would you need to buy to be safe if you are wrong? The rest of this part answers them in that order.

Sensitivity

Move two guesses at once

Two things stand out. The discount rate matters more than the growth: one point of WACC either way moves the base case to ₹94.5 or ₹63.7, while two points of growth either way only move it to ₹72.6 or ₹81.0. And no single change reaches ₹100, but two modest ones together do: a WACC of 10.6% with 14% growth gives ₹100.3, the corner Part 24 found.

Scenarios

Three stories, not three tweaks

A grid moves inputs independently, but in real life they move together. A business that wins customers grows faster and earns more on the money it puts in; one that stumbles grows slower, earns less, and looks riskier to lenders and owners. A scenario is a consistent story that sets every input at once example.

Weighting

Weight each story by its chance

Give each story a probability that adds up to 100%: here 25% bull, 50% base, 25% bear example. Multiply each value by its probability and add. Drawn as blocks whose widths are the chances and heights the values, the weighted value is simply their average height.

Weighted value = 25% × 99.6 + 50% × 76.7 + 25% × 44.1 = ₹74.3

At ₹100 the price is 35% above that. Notice where the bull block ends: ₹99.6, just under the price. On these stories, a buyer at ₹100 is paying for slightly more than the best case.

The catch

Probabilities are judgements too

Nobody hands you the 25 / 50 / 25. It is an opinion, so show how much it matters. Keep the base case at 50% and slide the other half from bull to bear: the weighted value falls in a straight line from ₹88.1 to ₹60.4 example.

Two lessons. First, the weights move the answer about as much as the WACC did, so write them down and be ready to defend them. Second, a weighted value can never leave the range of its stories: since even the bull case is below ₹100, no set of probabilities makes Ramu worth ₹100 on these three cases. To justify the price you need a better story, not different weights.

Margin of safety

Buy well below your value

Benjamin Graham’s phrase margin of safety means the gap between what you think a share is worth and what you pay. Pick the margin first, then work out the price: a 30% margin on the weighted ₹74.3 means paying no more than ₹52.0; on the base case alone, ₹53.7 example.

Buy price = value × (1 − margin) = 74.26 × (1 − 30%) = ₹52.0

How big a margin? The wider your range, the bigger it should be. A steady business with a narrow range might need 20%; a young or cyclical one with a wide range, 40% or more. The margin is a rule you set before you look at the price, so that the price cannot talk you out of it.

Why it works

It protects you against your own errors

The margin is not there because the market is wrong. It is there because you might be. Suppose you buy, and then one of the three stories comes true example:

Bought at ₹100, even the bull case barely breaks even. Bought at ₹52.0, the bear case still hurts, −15.2%, but far less, and the base case earns 47.5%. A margin does not remove mistakes; it makes them survivable. It helps less the further the truth falls below your bear case, which is why the bear case should be genuinely bad.

The common mistake

A fall in price is not a margin

“It is down 30% from its high, so I have a 30% margin” is the most common slip. A margin is measured against your value, not against an old price. If Ramu fell from ₹100 to ₹70, it would sit only ₹4.3 below the weighted ₹74.3: a margin of 5.7%, nowhere near 30% example.

The reverse also holds: a share that has doubled can still trade below its value, if the value rose faster. The old price is an anchor, and a margin of safety only works if you ignore it.

Go deeper: two more slips

Double caution. If your base case is already gloomy, demanding a big margin on top can mean you never buy anything good. Be honest in the scenarios, then cautious in the margin, not cautious twice. Fake precision. A weighted value of ₹74.26 from 25 / 50 / 25 is no more accurate than one from 30 / 45 / 25; quote it to the nearest rupee or so, and the margin absorbs the rest.

So what

Questions before you buy

  1. What is the range? Run a grid on the two inputs that matter most, usually the discount rate and growth.
  2. What are the stories? Write a bull, base and bear case in words, and let each set every input.
  3. Whose probabilities? Yours. Write them down, and see how far the value moves if you are wrong about them.
  4. Where is the price? Above the bull case, you are paying for a story you have not written.
  5. What margin, set in advance? Wider range, wider margin, measured against your value, never against an old price.

That completes Stage 5, valuation. Everything so far has used one kind of business: one that sells things, earns on its capital and borrows a little. Part 29, “Banks”, starts the sector stage, where debt is the raw material and the usual ratios stop working.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A value is a range: test the guesses that matter, weight honest bull, base and bear stories, and buy only well below the result, because the margin of safety is there to protect you from your own mistakes.
