How chips are made

Every part of this series has run on chips: billions of switches on a slice of silicon. This last part shows how they get there. Sand becomes a perfect crystal, the crystal is sliced into wafers, and light prints the switches and wires, layer by layer, in factories that cost tens of billions of dollars. Then it shows why some chips come out dead, why big AI chips cost so much, and how they are packaged with their memory.

A topic study built from the primary sources: ASML’s product pages and 2025 annual report, TSMC’s 2025 annual report, 2024 business overview and press releases, Intel’s and Samsung’s process announcements, the IRDS 2022 More Moore roadmap tables, the Semiconductor Industry Association’s 2025 polysilicon filing, SK hynix’s HBM3E release, NVIDIA’s Hopper and Blackwell pages, and Bogdanov and colleagues’ review of yield models; every number below was checked against them, as of November 2026 · Read the main source ↗

It builds on Part 3 (a chip is billions of switches flipped by electricity, with wires stacked above them), Part 5 (GPUs and their stacked HBM memory), Part 8 (why that memory sits right beside the chip) and Part 10 (the AI chips themselves). The problem

Eighty billion switches, all of them working

NVIDIA’s H100, the GPU Part 12 wired together by the thousand, is one piece of silicon 814 mm² across, carrying 80 billion transistors public. Here it is to scale, with one square millimetre lit up:

That is about 98 million switches in every square millimetre public. Placing them one a second would take about 2,500 years. And one bad switch in the wrong place can ruin the whole chip. So the switches cannot be built one at a time. They are printed, all at once, with light.

The analogy

A photograph, not a drawing

Think of an old darkroom. Light shines through a negative, a lens shrinks the image, and wherever light lands, the film changes. Chipmaking does the same. The negative is a mask carrying one layer of the chip’s design. The film is a light-sensitive coating on the wafer, called photoresist.

ASML’s EUV machines shrink the mask’s pattern by a factor of four on the way to the wafer public, so the mask can be drawn larger than the chip. Where the analogy breaks: a photo is one exposure. A chip needs dozens of them, one per layer, each lined up on top of the last to within a few nanometres.

The building block

From sand to one perfect crystal

It starts with silica, the main ingredient of sand. It is refined into crude silicon, then purified into polysilicon. For chips the polysilicon must be at least 99.999999999% pure: “eleven nines”, or one stray atom in a hundred billion public.

The polysilicon is melted and a single crystal is slowly pulled from the melt, every atom lined up with its neighbours. For 300 mm wafers the crystal, called an ingot, is about 7 feet long and weighs about 600 pounds public: roughly 2.1 m and 270 kg. Diamond saws slice it into wafers, which are then polished public.

Go deeper: who makes the raw material

The Semiconductor Industry Association’s 2025 filing to the US government lists five main makers of chip-grade polysilicon (Wacker, Hemlock, Tokuyama, Sumco and OCI) and five main wafer makers (Shin-Etsu Handotai, Sumco, GlobalWafers, SK Siltron and Siltronic). Chips use only about 2.4% of the world’s polysilicon; solar panels use the rest, at a lower purity of 6 to 10 nines public. The exact purity a given wafer maker reaches is not public.

The building block

A round wafer, square chips

The 300 mm wafer is the size most used in chipmaking today public, and TSMC alone shipped 15.0 million of them, counted as 12-inch equivalents, in 2025 public. Each wafer is printed with a grid of identical chips, called dies. Square dies do not tile a circle:

Dividing the wafer’s area by the die’s area overestimates, because the rim is wasted, and the bigger the die, the more it wastes example. These counts assume square dies, no gaps for the saw, and a 3 mm unusable edge example. The lab below counts the same way.

The mechanism

One layer: coat, expose, wash, etch

Printing one layer is a short recipe, the same every time public. Lay down a thin film of the material this layer needs. Coat it with resist. Shine light through the mask. Wash away the resist the light changed. Etch away the film where it is uncovered. Strip off the rest of the resist:

The light never touches the film itself. It only decides which parts of the resist survive, and the resist decides which parts of the film survive. Other steps slot into the same loop: firing charged atoms (ions) into the bare silicon to set where it conducts, which is Part 3’s doping, and polishing each layer flat public.

The mechanism

Then do it again, dozens of times

Part 3 showed a chip’s structure: switches in one layer at the bottom, wiring stacked above like floors in a building. Every floor is one more trip around the loop, lined up on the floor below. The finest layers come first:

ASML puts it plainly: chips are made of dozens of layers, each chip goes through this process hundreds of times, and the whole job can take more than three months public. The wires get thicker and further apart towards the top, where, as Part 3 showed, they cross the whole chip and bring in power.

The catch

Smaller lines need shorter light

Light blurs. The narrowest line a lens can print is set by the wavelength of its light, so each big shrink has needed shorter light. ASML’s own history runs from 436 nm blue light from mercury lamps to 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet public. Drawn to one scale:

Deep ultraviolet (DUV) comes from lasers: krypton fluoride at 248 nm and argon fluoride at 193 nm. Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) at 13.5 nm is, in ASML’s words, “almost x-ray range” public, fourteen times shorter than 193 nm. It is so different that it needs a whole new machine, two panels down.

The rule

Three levers: light, lens, and tricks

ASML gives the rule: smallest printable line = k1 × wavelength ÷ NA public. NA, the numerical aperture, measures how wide a cone of light the lens gathers. k1 covers every clever trick of the process, and ASML puts its physical limit at 0.25 public. Here are its three current kinds of machine:

Each faint bar is wavelength ÷ NA. The solid bar is what ASML says the machine prints: 38 nm or less for its NXT:2100i DUV machine, 13 nm for EUV, 8 nm for High-NA EUV public. Divide one by the other and you get k1: the DUV machine is already at 0.27, almost on the limit example. DUV has no room left to squeeze. Only shorter light, or a wider lens, goes further.

Go deeper: how a lens gets an NA above 1

In air, NA can never reach 1. ASML’s DUV “immersion” machines put a thin layer of water between the last lens and the wafer, which bends the light more steeply, and their NXT:2100i reaches an NA of 1.35 public. EUV cannot use water or even lenses, so it uses mirrors, at an NA of 0.33; the High-NA machines raise that to 0.55 public. Shrinking k1 needs tricks in the mask and the resist, many of them designed by computer simulation, which ASML calls computational lithography public.

The workaround

Print twice to halve the spacing

Before EUV, chipmakers beat the limit by printing one layer in several passes. Print every other line, etch, then print the lines in between, shifted by half a step. Two passes give half the spacing, called the pitch:

It works, at a price: more masks, more passes, more chances for a misaligned line. The IRDS roadmap lists self-aligned quadruple patterning on 193 nm machines as one way to print the tightest wires of 2022’s chips public. ASML’s case for EUV is exactly this: one exposure per layer instead of several, fewer masks, fewer steps and better yield public.

The machine

EUV: light made by blasting tin

No lamp or laser makes 13.5 nm light. An EUV machine makes it from tin. Molten droplets fall past a powerful laser, which fires up to 50,000 times a second. Two pulses hit each droplet: one flattens it, the next vaporises it into a plasma that glows with EUV light public.

EUV light is absorbed by everything, even air, so the whole light path is in a high vacuum, and glass lenses are out: multilayer mirrors steer the light instead public. Even the mask is a mirror. ASML’s NXE:3800E exposes at least 220 wafers an hour public.

Go deeper: High-NA EUV

ASML’s next platform, EXE, raises the NA from 0.33 to 0.55 and prints 8 nm instead of 13 nm public. It shipped its first EXE:5200B in April 2025, rated at 175 wafers an hour, and expects EXE to start supporting high-volume manufacturing in 2027 public. The IRDS roadmap pencils High-NA in for the tightest wires from 2028 public.

The bottleneck

Only one company makes EUV machines

ASML, in the Netherlands, calls EUV “a technology entirely unique to ASML” public. Its annual report names Canon and Nikon as competitors for DUV machines only public. Here is every lithography machine ASML recognised in its 2025 sales, one dot each:

48 EUV machines (44 NXE and 4 High-NA EXE) and 279 DUV machines public. ASML says its NXE:3800E supports high-volume manufacturing of 2 nm logic public: the newest processes run through that left-hand group. ASML in turn relies on one supplier, Carl Zeiss SMT, for its lenses and mirrors public. ASML does not publish the price of an EUV machine not public.

The common mistake

“3 nm” is a name, not a size

The natural guess is that a “3 nm” chip has 3 nm parts. It does not. The industry roadmap, the IRDS, labels its 2022 node “3nm” and its 2025 node “2nm”, and lists their real dimensions public. Drawn to one scale:

Nothing on a “3 nm” chip is 3 nm. The gate, the part that switches, is 16 nm long, and gates sit 48 nm apart public. Intel says it outright: its node numbers “do not represent the actual dimension of any physical feature on a transistor or structure” public. The names now mean “one generation better”, which Part 3 called a label for a generation.

The switch, reshaped

From flat, to fins, to sheets

Part 3’s transistor is a channel of silicon with a gate that turns it on and off. As it shrank, a gate sitting only on top lost its grip, and current leaked. So the channel was turned on its side into a fin, with the gate wrapped over three sides, then sliced into stacked nanosheets with the gate all around:

Intel began making FinFETs in 2011 public. Samsung started the first gate-all-around production in June 2022, on 3 nm, claiming up to 45% less power than its 5 nm public. TSMC’s N2, its first nanosheet process, entered volume production in late 2025, and Intel’s 18A, with its RibbonFET sheets, went into production in Arizona the same year public.

Go deeper: power from underneath

The next change moves the power wires. Today power and signals share the floors above the switches. Intel’s 18A adds PowerVia, which delivers power from the back of the wafer, “eliminating the need for power routing on the front side”, in Intel’s words public. TSMC’s version, Super Power Rail on its A16 process, is scheduled for volume production in the second half of 2026 public. The IRDS roadmap expects it from 2025 on public.

The hidden cost

Every wafer has a few specks

However clean the factory, a few defects land on every wafer: a speck of dust, a flaw in a film. Wherever one lands on a die, that die is usually dead. Here are the same 71 random defects on two wafers, one cut into small dies and one into big ones example:

Same wafer, same specks. Most small dies dodge them, but a big die is a big target, and more than half of the big ones are hit. The fraction of dies that work is the yield. Foundries do not publish their defect rates: TSMC says only that N2 entered production “with good yield” not public.

The formula

Yield falls fast as dies grow

The classic model treats defects as raindrops landing at random. If a die has area A and the process leaves D0 defects per square centimetre, a die expects A × D0 hits, and the chance of none is the Poisson yield, e−A·D0 public.

Real defects cluster, so some dies get several and others none, and the plain Poisson model is too gloomy for big dies. In 1964 Murphy proposed a version that allows for this, ((1 − e−A·D0) ÷ A·D0)², which predicts a little more public. The curves use 0.1 defects per cm² example.

Try it

Cut a wafer, count the good dies

Pick a die size, a wafer size, a defect rate and a price for the finished wafer. The lab tiles the wafer with square dies, keeps a 3 mm edge free and counts the whole ones. It then works out the good dies with both models, and scatters random defects on the drawing example. Foundries do not publish wafer prices, so the price is your guess not public.

Try the presets in order. A giant die on a young process loses most of its dies; the same die on a mature process keeps most of them. A small chip barely notices the same defect rate. On a 200 mm wafer, older factories’ size, the rim wastes a far bigger share. The drawing is one random wafer, so its count wobbles around the models’ average.

Go deeper: what the lab assumes

Square dies placed edge to edge, with no lanes for the saw; the lab tries four positions of the grid and keeps the one that fits most whole dies. Any defect on a die kills it, though real chips often carry spare parts to survive one. Defects are spread evenly over the wafer, and one defect is one speck. Real processes count defects per layer, and the IRDS roadmap sets its targets in yet another model, the Bose–Einstein model, which multiplies one factor per critical layer public. Foundries’ real defect rates and wafer prices are not public.

The surprise

Eight times the area, seventeen times the price

Put the lab’s default numbers side by side: a 100 mm² phone-size chip and an 814 mm² AI die, at 0.1 defects per cm² and $20,000 a wafer example. The big die is 8.1 times the area. Each working one costs far more than 8.1 times as much:

Two things stack up. Fewer big dies fit, because the rim wastes more, and fewer of those work, because each is a bigger target. That is why a big AI chip is so expensive to make, and why a young process, with more defects, hurts the biggest chips most example.

The ceiling

No chip can outgrow the mask

One exposure prints one field, the area of one mask image. TSMC puts that field at about 830 mm² public, and a single die cannot be larger. The H100’s 814 mm² nearly fills it. So the next generation went sideways:

NVIDIA’s Blackwell GPUs use “two reticle-limited dies” joined by a 10 TB/s link so they work as one GPU, with 208 billion transistors in all public (the reticle is the mask). Two smaller dies also yield far better than one impossible giant would, which is the lab’s lesson. Their exact areas are not public.

The package

CoWoS: chips on a silicon floor

Part 8 showed why AI chips keep their HBM memory right beside them. Making that work takes thousands of wires between the chips, far finer than a circuit board can carry. TSMC’s answer is CoWoS, chip on wafer on substrate: the GPU and its memory stacks sit side by side on a slab of silicon, the interposer, that is just wiring public.

The interposer is made with the same light-and-etch loop as a chip, so its wires can be very fine and very many. The full H100 die has room for six HBM stacks; the H100 SXM5 product uses five, for 80 GB public. Part 8’s 3,350 GB/s runs through these wires.

The package, growing

The silicon floor keeps getting bigger

An interposer is not one exposure, so it can grow past the mask limit. TSMC measures it in reticles of about 830 mm². Its silicon interposers grew from 1 reticle to 3.3 over the past decade, and TSMC now produces 5.5-reticle CoWoS public. Drawn to one scale:

Next is a 14-reticle CoWoS, which TSMC says can hold about 10 large compute dies and 20 HBM stacks, slated for production in 2028 public. The biggest, which TSMC calls CoWoS-L, build the floor from small silicon bridges set into a cheaper wiring layer public. The AI chip of Part 10 is now a package of many chips.

The memory, stacked

Twelve memory chips, drilled straight through

Each HBM stack is several memory chips piled up and wired vertically. Thousands of tiny holes run through every chip and are filled with metal, linking each chip to the ones above and below: through-silicon vias, or TSVs public.

SK hynix’s 12-layer HBM3E, in production since September 2024, stacks twelve 3 GB chips for 36 GB. To keep it the same height as the 8-layer version, it made each chip 40% thinner public. Every one of those chips is a die from a wafer, with its own yield, and one bad layer can spoil a stack.

The industry

A few companies, one long chain

Each step in this part is done by a handful of companies, often one. From the sources on this page public:

TSMC, in Taiwan, makes chips other companies design: in 2025 it served 534 customers, its 3 nm processes earned 24% of its wafer revenue, and it runs fabs in Taiwan, China, Japan and Arizona public. Samsung makes memory and other firms’ chips. Intel makes its own chips and others’, with 18A at Fab 52 in Arizona public. Market shares are left out: no primary source here states them.

The bill

Factories priced in tens of billions

All of this happens in a fab, short for fabrication plant: a building of dust-free rooms full of these machines. Their cost is now announced in tens of billions of dollars, for a few fabs at a time:

In March 2025 TSMC announced it intends to invest an additional $100 billion in the US, bringing the total to $165 billion; the addition covers three new fabs, two advanced packaging plants and an R&D centre public. Intel has said it is investing more than $32 billion in two new fabs and an upgrade in Arizona public. These are announced plans, spent over many years.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A chip is printed, not built: light through a stencil, dozens of layers deep. The smaller the lines, the shorter the light and the rarer the machines; the bigger the chip, the more of them die on the wafer.
