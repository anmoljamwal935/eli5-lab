MONETARY ARCHITECTURE / SERIES MAPELI5

Fundamental analysis, from scratch

You already know how the stock market works. This series teaches the next skill: reading a company's accounts, judging the business behind them, and working out what it is worth, all in an Indian context. Each part builds on the one before, and every part has a lab you can play with.

The plan

Three tracks, one question: what is it worth?

Valuing a company takes three skills. Reading the numbers: the profit and loss, balance sheet and cash flow, and the ratios built from them. Judging the business: how it earns, what protects it, who runs it, and what can go wrong. Price and value: what the market charges, and what the business is really worth. The series switches between them as each needs the others.

Read along the line from left to right. A filled dot is published; a hollow one is coming. The numbers at the top are the seven stages.

Two companies

One made-up company, one real one

Ramu Chai Ltd is a made-up tea company with clean, consistent numbers: ₹500 crore of sales, ₹50 crore of profit, 10 crore shares at ₹100 example. Every lab starts from them, so you meet the same figures in every part. Tata Consumer Products is the real one. Most parts end by finding the same lines in its audited FY2026 annual report public. Sector parts bring in other real companies where Tata Consumer has nothing to show.

Real figures are frozen at FY2026 (the year to 31 March 2026). This series is educational, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

How to read it

The same colours mean the same things

Every diagram in every part uses one colour key, so after a part or two you can read the drawings without a legend.

Money coming inSales, and cash flowing into the company.
Money going outCosts, capital spending, and cash flowing out.
What belongs to shareholdersProfit, earnings per share, equity, book value and dividends.
What is owedBorrowings, interest, and bills the company still has to pay.
The market's viewShare price, market cap, and multiples such as P/E.

Facts carry a tag. public means a company's filed accounts, a regulator's rule, or plain arithmetic. example means a made-up but consistent number, mostly Ramu Chai's. not public means the company has not said. Each part also has Go deeper boxes. They are optional: skip them on a first read and nothing later breaks.

The reading list

Every part, in order

Start at part 1. Each part recaps what it borrows from earlier ones in a line, so you can also jump to a stage you need.

    Why switch tracks?

    Each skill needs the others

    A P/E ratio (part 7) means little until you know where the profit under it comes from (part 3). A cheap-looking price is only cheap if the business behind it is sound (parts 16–20). And a valuation (parts 23–28) is only as good as the numbers and judgements you feed it. So the order follows the questions you would naturally ask, not one subject at a time.

    The bonus study, Satyam, decoded, reads India's best-known accounting fraud through the red flags from part 20. It is written to be read once those flags are familiar.

    AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
    A share's price is what the market says today. Its value is what the business will earn over the years. Fundamental analysis is the habit of checking one against the other.
