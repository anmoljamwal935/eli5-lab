The P&L, line by line

A profit and loss statement is a year told as subtraction: start with what customers paid, take away each cost in a fixed order, and see what is left for the owners. Learn the order once and you can read any Indian company’s P&L.

Built from Ramu Chai Ltd’s example accounts and Tata Consumer Products’ audited FY2026 consolidated statements, facts checked against them · Read the source ↗

Part 1 set a share’s price against the value of the business. Part 2 showed where a company’s statements live. This part reads the first of them. Ramu Chai Ltd is a made-up tea company, and its numbers carry example. Tata Consumer Products (TCPL) is real, and its audited figures carry public; they are frozen at FY2026, the year to 31 March 2026. Educational, not a recommendation. Go deeper boxes are optional.

The big picture

From ₹500 crore of sales to ₹50 crore

Ramu Chai sold ₹500 crore of tea in FY26 example. Each line of the P&L takes a bite, in a fixed order, until ₹50 crore is left. Teal brings money in, coral takes it out, rose is interest, gold is profit. The rest of this page walks down these steps.

The top line

Revenue is cups times price

Revenue from operations is what customers paid for the company’s main business. Ramu sold 100 crore cups at ₹5 each: ₹500 crore example. Interest earned on spare cash is not sales, so it sits on a separate line further down. Part 16 covers volume and price in depth.

The running costs

Three costs before wear and finance

Indian statements list costs by what they are, not by department. Ramu’s: materials ₹250 crore (tea, milk, sugar, packaging), employee benefits ₹60 crore, other expenses ₹105 crore (rent, ads, power, freight) example. ₹85 crore is left. Part 8 turns these into margins.

Go deeper: “materials” in a real statement

A company that buys tea in bulk and also resells finished goods shows three lines: cost of materials consumed, purchases of stock-in-trade, and changes in inventories. TCPL’s FY2026 figures, in ₹ crore: 7,788.16 + 4,108.23 − 40.75 = 11,855.64 public. Sales minus materials is “gross profit”, which is not a reported line either; analysts compute it.

A line nobody reports

EBITDA: profit before wear and finance

EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Ind AS has no such line, so you will not find it in an audited statement; everyone computes it. Two roads reach Ramu’s ₹85 crore: subtract the costs from the top, or add back from the bottom example.

Because EBITDA is each company’s own sum, compare like with like. TCPL’s own figure and the one its statement gives differ; see the real report below.

Wear and tear

Depreciation spreads a big purchase

Picture one ₹120 crore plant that lasts ten years. The P&L does not charge ₹120 crore in the year it is bought; it charges ₹12 crore a year example. That is depreciation. For brands and software bought in a deal, the same spreading is called amortisation. EBITDA ₹85 crore minus ₹12 crore is EBIT, ₹73 crore. Part 6 covers the choices behind it.

Lines that hide surprises

Other income and finance costs

Below EBIT, two lines come from the balance sheet, not from tea. Ramu earns ₹4 crore of interest on ₹40 crore of cash (other income) and pays ₹10 crore on ₹100 crore of borrowings (finance costs), both at about 10% example. Other income is the one to question: a company can look profitable on money that has nothing to do with its business.

Lines that hide surprises

Exceptional items: the one-offs

Gains and charges a company calls unusual, such as selling land or writing down an asset, get their own line just above profit before tax. Ramu’s real FY26 has none. But suppose it sold an old plot for a ₹12 crore gain example: pre-tax profit reads ₹79 crore, then “falls” 15% next year, though the tea business never changed.

Ind AS has no “extraordinary” line, and “exceptional” is loosely defined, so each company decides what goes there. Some “one-offs” return year after year: read the note behind the line.

The bottom line

Tax, profit after tax, and EPS

Tax takes about a quarter of Ramu’s ₹67 crore: ₹17 crore, leaving profit after tax (PAT) of ₹50 crore example. Split among 10 crore shares, each share’s slice is ₹5, its EPS; Part 7 teaches EPS and P/E in full. In a group, PAT is also split between the parent’s owners and “non-controlling interests”, outsiders who own part of a subsidiary.

Go deeper: current and deferred tax

The tax line adds tax due now (current tax) to tax that accounting timing moves between years (deferred tax). TCPL’s FY2026 tax of ₹535.28 crore is 545.27 current plus −9.99 deferred public. Ramu’s ₹17 crore is simply 17 ÷ 67 of pre-tax profit, about 25%.

Drive Ramu’s P&L yourself

Now find it in a real report

Tata Consumer’s FY2026 P&L

Tata Consumer Products’ consolidated statement of profit and loss (Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, page 415, audited by Deloitte) is the same staircase, in ₹ crore public. The group has more lines than Ramu, so the picture is split in two: the top half on one scale, the bottom half magnified about ten times. Figures are rounded to the nearest crore, so a sum can be off by one.

  • Revenue ₹20,290.43 crore. Some press reports say ₹20,280 crore; the audited statement and its Note 21 say 20,290.43, and so does TCPL’s own results filing.
  • EBITDA is not on the statement. Revenue less materials, staff and other expenses gives ₹2,791.80 crore. TCPL’s management report quotes ₹2,815 crore. The ₹23 crore gap is a definition: the statement does not reconcile the two.
  • Other income ₹164.75 crore is about 7.5% of profit before exceptional items, and more than finance costs of ₹137.03 crore. It is mostly interest (₹101.38 crore), plus ₹28.55 crore of gains on investments, ₹11.83 crore of dividends and ₹22.99 crore of other non-operating income. TCPL reports net cash of ₹2,978 crore (management report, page 224), so the interest has a source.
  • Finance costs halved, from ₹290.20 crore to ₹137.03 crore. ₹40.96 crore of the new figure is interest on leases. The note shows the split, not the reason for the fall.
  • Exceptional items net to −₹20.06 crore, which hides ₹90.93 crore of gains against ₹110.99 crore of charges (next drawing).
  • Associates and joint ventures: −₹90.70 crore, a line Ramu does not have. Profit for the year is ₹1,546.80 crore, of which ₹1,542.30 crore is the owners’ and ₹4.50 crore non-controlling interests’. Basic EPS is ₹15.59.

Those five items: a fair-value gain on contingent consideration (₹55.57 crore, the price still owed for an earlier acquisition, remeasured), profit on selling non-core assets (₹35.36 crore), an asset write-down (₹69.06 crore), the one-time effect of India’s new Labour Codes (₹25.91 crore) and a goodwill impairment (₹16.02 crore) public. The previous year’s line also held a ₹120 crore fair-value gain against ₹125.11 crore of charges. Read the note, not just the total.

So what

Three questions for any P&L

  1. How much of the profit is not from the business? Compare other income with profit before tax.
  2. What is inside exceptional items, and does it come back? Open the note and add up gains and charges separately.
  3. Which cost grew faster than sales? Set each line’s growth beside revenue’s.

A P&L covers a year of flow. It does not say what the company owns and owes on the last day, or where the cash went. Part 4, “The balance sheet”, answers the first: what a company owns, what it owes, and what is left for its shareholders. Part 5 follows the cash.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A P&L is a staircase from sales down to profit, and the steps worth doubting are the ones that don’t come from selling anything: other income and exceptional items.
