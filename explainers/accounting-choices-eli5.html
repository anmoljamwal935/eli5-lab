Accounting choices that change profit

Two honest accountants can report different profits for the same year, with the same cash in the bank. This part shows the choices behind that, how much room they leave, and why companies publish an “adjusted” profit beside the reported one.

The puzzle

Same ₹20 cr, two different profits

Suppose Ramu spends ₹20 cr on a new blending line, on top of its FY26 example. Book it as a cost and profit after tax falls from ₹50 cr to ₹35 cr. Book it as an asset, spread over five years, and profit is ₹47.15 cr. The cash is the same: free cash flow, ₹13 cr in Part 5 (operations 58 less capex 45), becomes −₹7 cr either way.

Accrual, in one line

Match the cost to the years it helps

Part 5 showed that profit is not cash. The reason is the accrual basis: Ind AS 1 says financial statements, except the cash flow statement, are prepared on it public. A cost is charged when it is used up, not when it is paid. A machine that helps for five years is charged across five years (ignoring scrap value for now), while the cash leaves on day one.

Capitalise or expense

Does the spend help beyond this year?

To expense a spend is to charge it to the P&L at once. To capitalise it is to put it on the balance sheet as an asset and charge it gradually. The test is whether it creates a benefit lasting beyond the year. Repairs and research are expensed. A new line or acquired software is capitalised. Ind AS 38 expenses research and capitalises development only when feasibility, intent and resources are shown public.

Pre-tax, day one. The choice does not make a project better or worse; it decides which year’s profit carries the cost. Part 20 covers the red flag: costs capitalised that should have been expensed.

Depreciation

Straight line or written-down value

Depreciation is the yearly charge for a capitalised asset: cost less scrap value, spread over its useful life. Most assets keep a small scrap value; Schedule II says it should be at most 5% of cost public, so we use ₹1 cr. Straight line charges the same each year. Written-down value charges a fixed percentage of what is left, so the charge starts high and shrinks. Ind AS 16 asks only that the method match how the benefits are used up public.

Go deeper: the written-down rate

To reach a ₹1 cr scrap value from ₹20 cr in five years, the yearly rate is 1 − (1 ÷ 20)^(1/5) = 45.07% of the opening book value: 9.01, then 4.95, 2.72, 1.49 and 0.82 example. Both methods charge ₹19 cr in total. They differ only in timing. A written-down method front-loads the cost, so profit is lower early and higher later.

Useful life

A longer life, a smaller yearly charge

Useful life is an estimate. Spread the same ₹19 cr over 3 years and the charge is ₹6.33 cr a year; over 15, ₹1.27 cr. Schedule II lists lives for tangible assets: 15 years for general plant and machinery, 8 for continuous process plant, 6 for servers, 3 for laptops, 10 for furniture, 8 for most cars and 60 for RCC buildings public. A company may use another life but must disclose why. Schedule II fixes lives, not the method.

Lives are reviewed at least every year-end; a change is a change in estimate and applies from then on, so earlier years are not restated public. For intangibles, Schedule II hands the question to the accounting standards.

Acquired intangibles and goodwill

Brands wear out on paper, goodwill is tested

When a company buys a business it splits the price. What it can name and value goes in separately: machines, a brand, customer lists. The leftover premium is goodwill. A brand with a finite life is amortised (depreciation’s word for intangibles). Goodwill, and a brand judged to last indefinitely, are not amortised; instead they are tested for impairment every year, and a write-down is never reversed public. Part 4 introduced goodwill; this is its cost to profit. Illustrated here with a made-up ₹60 cr purchase that is not in Ramu’s accounts example.

Provisions and estimates

A provision books cost before cash

A provision is a liability of uncertain size, such as warranty claims, unpaid-customer losses or a legal claim. Ind AS 37 requires one when there is a present obligation from a past event, an outflow is probable and the amount can be reliably estimated public. Profit takes the hit now, cash only when the claim is paid. The estimate is reviewed each period, so a changed guess moves profit without moving cash. Part 20 covers abuse.

The bars are a what-if on Ramu’s profit before tax of ₹67 cr example: a different estimate, same business, same cash.

One-offs and exceptional items

“One-off” is a label, not a fact

Ind AS 1 requires material items of income or expense to be shown separately public; it has no “extraordinary” category, and each company decides what it calls exceptional (Part 3 listed TCPL’s). Moving a cost into that line changes the label, not the profit before tax. Whether it is truly one-off is for the reader: if a “one-off” appears year after year, treat it as a cost of doing business.

Reported vs adjusted EPS

Why companies publish an adjusted profit

EPS, earnings per share, is profit for the owners divided by shares; Part 7 covers it, and the P/E built on it, in full. Ind AS 33 defines basic and diluted EPS. A company may add other per-share amounts, but must state the basis and reconcile it to a reported line public. Companies publish an adjusted figure to show a business without a charge that does not recur or does not use cash. A careful reader asks what was left out, and whether gains were removed as readily as costs.

On Ramu’s example: ₹20 cr × (1 − 25%) = ₹15 cr, or ₹1.50 a share over 10 cr shares example. Reported EPS ₹3.50; leave the one-off out and it is ₹5.00. Both are true; they answer different questions.

Try it · one spend, five years

Choose how Ramu books the ₹20 cr

The one-off spend sits on top of Ramu’s FY26 example, held flat for five years: profit after tax ₹50 cr, free cash flow ₹13 cr, 10 cr shares. Tax expense is 25% of book profit; tax actually paid is set by tax law’s own rules, so it does not move with the book choice (the gap is deferred tax). Scrap value is 5% of the spend, sold for cash at the end of life. Set the spend to 0 to see Ramu’s FY26 as reported.

Gold is reported profit after tax; teal (coral when negative) is free cash flow after the spend, which is ₹13 cr a year before it. A life above five years is cut off at year five; the ledger shows the rest.

Go deeper: why the lab holds cash tax fixed

Tax law has its own depreciation rules, set apart from the books, so changing how Ramu books the spend changes the tax expense in the P&L (with the difference carried as deferred tax) but not the tax paid. Part 5 made the same point about tax paid versus tax expense. The lab leaves out any cash tax saving from deducting the spend, which would be the same in every setting example.

Go deeper: the two columns in the AGM deck

The AGM table has two EPS columns. Before exceptional items: basic ₹15.64, adjusted ₹17.35. As reported: basic ₹15.59, adjusted ₹17.30. The ₹1.71 gap is the same in both, and the exceptional items moved EPS by only ₹0.05, because the gains and costs largely cancel and the goodwill impairment carries no tax shield in the figures given public. The amortisation adjustment is the bigger one.

So what

Three questions for any profit figure

1. When did the cost reach profit? Was it expensed or capitalised, and does a peer treat it the same way? 2. What lives and estimates are behind it? Compare depreciation lives with Schedule II and peers, and watch for lives that lengthen. 3. What was adjusted, and is it adjusted every year? An adjusted figure that removes only costs deserves a second look. In every case the cash flow statement stays the same, so profit and cash should be read together.

These choices change when profit shows up, not what the business earned in total. Next, Part 7, “P/E: what it says and when it lies”, takes EPS, reported or adjusted, and divides the share price by it, and shows when that ratio misleads.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Accounting choices decide which year carries a cost, not whether the cash left; over an asset’s life the total is the same, so read profit beside cash and ask what was adjusted.
