ROE, ROCE and DuPont

Profit means little until you ask how much money it took to earn. ROE and ROCE are that question, and DuPont shows that one return can be reached by three very different roads.

The question

Same profit, different capital

Two tea shops each earn ₹10 cr a year example. Shop A needed ₹50 cr to get there. Shop B needed more. Both profits are equal, yet one owner is plainly better off. Press the buttons to change Shop B’s capital. This is a one-off pair of shops; it does not change Ramu’s accounts.

A return is profit divided by the money that earned it. It lets you compare a small shop with a giant, or this year with last.

Return on the owners’ money

ROE: profit per rupee of equity

ROE, return on equity, is profit after tax divided by shareholders’ equity. Ramu earned ₹50 cr on ₹250 cr of equity: 20% example. Each rupee the owners have in the business earned 20 paise. Profit is after interest and tax, because the owners are paid last.

ROE = profit after tax ÷ equity = 50 ÷ 250 = 20%

Equity here is the owners’ own share. In consolidated accounts that means equity attributable to the company’s shareholders, not the slice outsiders hold in subsidiaries (Part 4 covers non-controlling interest). The profit on top must match: profit attributable to owners.

Return on all the money

ROCE: operating profit per rupee employed

ROCE, return on capital employed, divides EBIT (earnings before interest and tax, Part 3) by capital employed: equity plus debt. Ramu’s EBIT is ₹73 cr and its capital employed is 250 + 100 = ₹350 cr: 20.9% example. EBIT is counted before interest because lenders’ money is in the denominator, so lenders’ pay belongs in the numerator.

ROCE = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt) = 73 ÷ 350 = 20.9%

ROCE ignores how the business is financed, so a company with debt and one without can be compared fairly. That is why it sits beside ROE on many Indian stock screens. Capital employed has other definitions (assets minus current liabilities is common); this series uses equity plus borrowings, and says so wherever it matters.

One more, briefly

ROA, and three denominators

ROA, return on assets, is profit divided by everything the company owns: ₹50 cr ÷ ₹400 cr = 12.5% example. It asks how hard the assets work, whoever paid for them. Same profit, three bases: assets, equity, and equity plus debt. The numerators differ too: ROCE uses EBIT.

Go deeper: why ROA has several versions

Some analysts use EBIT after tax over assets, or average assets. Here ROA is profit after tax over closing total assets, because that is the version DuPont splits cleanly (two panels on).

Which balance?

Closing or average balances

Equity changes during the year, so the denominator needs a choice. This series uses closing balances: the number printed on the balance sheet, so you can check it. Average (opening plus closing, halved) fits better when capital moved a lot in the year, as with a rights issue or acquisition. For Ramu, average equity is (218 + 250) ÷ 2 = 234 and ROE is 21.4% example. The canonical 20% uses closing equity.

When comparing companies, use the same basis for both. The real-report panels later show TCPL on both.

DuPont

ROE is margin × turnover × leverage

Multiply and cancel: profit ÷ sales, times sales ÷ assets, times assets ÷ equity, leaves profit ÷ equity. So ROE splits into net margin (how much of each sale is kept), asset turnover (sales per rupee of assets) and the equity multiplier (assets per rupee of equity). Ramu: 10% × 1.25 × 1.6 = 20% example.

50 ÷ 500 × 500 ÷ 400 × 400 ÷ 250 = 10% × 1.25 × 1.6 = 20%

Read it per rupee of owners’ money: ₹1 supports ₹1.60 of assets, which make ₹2.00 of sales, of which ₹0.20 is kept. A weak ROE can sit in any of the three.

THE SAME 20% THREE WAYS

The catch

Leverage lifts ROE and thins the cushion

The multiplier is borrowing in disguise: assets paid for by someone other than the owners. It lifts ROE, but a thin equity cushion is also what absorbs losses first. Take two firms with ₹100 cr of equity example. A 10% fall in asset values costs the first (multiplier 1.6) ₹16 cr. The second (10×) loses all its equity.

That is why the lender-like preset is flagged: it reaches 20% with a multiplier of 10. Part 11, “Debt: how much is too much”, covers how to judge it. Banks and other lenders are built this way; Part 29 covers them.

Why returns compound

A high return, kept, compounds

A business that earns 20% on equity and keeps 60% of profit (Ramu pays out 40% example) grows its equity by 20% × 60% = 12% a year, with no outside money. At 8% the same rule gives 4.8%. Same start, ₹250 cr; five years later ₹441 cr against ₹316 cr. One-off illustration, assuming new money earns the same return.

This links to Part 9, growth worth paying for: growth needs capital, and the return on that capital decides whether it is worth it. Whether a return is high enough depends on what the money costs; Part 23 compares ROCE with the cost of capital.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL: ROE and its three factors

Tata Consumer Products, consolidated, year to 31 March 2026 public. Profit attributable to owners was ₹1,542.30 cr; equity attributable to owners ₹21,787.53 cr. ROE on closing equity is 7.08%; on average equity, using FY25’s ₹20,001.08 cr, it is 7.38%.

closing: 1,542.30 ÷ 21,787.53 = 7.08% average equity: (21,787.53 + 20,001.08) ÷ 2 = 20,894.31 · 1,542.30 ÷ 20,894.31 = 7.38% DuPont: 1,542.30 ÷ 20,290.43 (7.60%) × 20,290.43 ÷ 34,452.96 (0.589) × 34,452.96 ÷ 21,787.53 (1.581) = 7.08%

Compared with Ramu, the margin and leverage are not far off; the turnover is the gap. TCPL’s ₹20,290 cr of sales sit on ₹34,453 cr of assets, 0.59 times, and 55% of those assets are goodwill and other intangibles. It also raised equity through a rights issue in FY25, ₹2,980.57 cr net of expenses public, which enlarged the equity base. A year earlier the same ratio, ₹1,278.47 cr over ₹20,001.08 cr, was 6.4%.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL’s ROCE, with and without acquisitions

EBIT here is EBITDA less depreciation and amortisation, the same construction as Ramu: ₹20,290.43 cr revenue less ₹11,855.64 cr materials, ₹1,661.00 cr employee costs and ₹3,981.99 cr other expenses is EBITDA of ₹2,791.80 cr; less ₹626.68 cr D&A is EBIT ₹2,165.12 cr public. That is before other income (₹164.75 cr) and before exceptional items (−₹20.06 cr). Capital employed is total equity ₹23,188.86 cr plus borrowings ₹2,120.31 cr (₹302.14 cr non-current plus ₹1,818.17 cr current): ₹25,309.17 cr.

ROCE = 2,165.12 ÷ 25,309.17 = 8.55% (average capital employed 24,274.06: 8.92%) without goodwill: 2,165.12 ÷ (25,309.17 − 11,883.90 = 13,425.27) = 16.13% without goodwill and other intangibles: 2,165.12 ÷ (13,425.27 − 7,111.76 = 6,313.51) = 34.29%

What each tells an investor. The 8.6% judges the whole bargain: what the business earns on every rupee put in, including the premium paid for acquired businesses such as Capital Foods and Organic India. It speaks to how well acquisitions have paid back so far. The 34.3% judges the underlying operating engine: factories, stock and dues from customers. It shows the business itself is not capital-hungry, but flatters, because it pretends the acquisitions were free. The truth for a buyer of the shares sits between, and each number alone misleads.

Go deeper: choices behind these numbers

Other intangibles of ₹7,111.76 cr are mostly brands and trademarks (₹6,882.03 cr); ₹103.56 cr is software and the rest customer, patent and distribution assets public. Goodwill rose in FY26 partly from translating overseas goodwill into rupees (₹569.50 cr), less a ₹16.02 cr impairment. Amortisation of other intangibles was ₹342.67 cr in FY26 and is already deducted in EBIT; adding it back would lift the last figure to 39.7%, my arithmetic.

A wider EBIT, profit before tax plus finance costs (₹2,172.78 cr + ₹137.03 cr = ₹2,309.81 cr), includes other income and exceptional items and gives 9.1% on closing capital. Lease liabilities (₹699.20 cr) are not in capital employed here; adding them gives 8.3%. Cash (₹3,046.57 cr) also sits in capital employed, which pulls ROCE down.

So what

Three questions for any return

  1. Which profit, which capital? Check numerator and denominator match, and whether closing or average is used.
  2. Which factor drives it? Margin, turnover or leverage: the same ROE can come from very different businesses.
  3. How much cushion sits behind it? A high ROE built on a high multiplier is borrowed strength.

Ramu earns 20% on its equity. Part 11, “Debt: how much is too much”, asks how much of that comes from borrowing, and what happens when interest rates rise.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Profit only means something against the money that earned it: ROE and ROCE divide one by the other, and DuPont shows the same return can come from a fat margin, fast turnover or heavy borrowing, which are very different risks.
