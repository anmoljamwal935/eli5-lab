Reading ratios together

Every ratio answers one question, and a company is a story that needs several. Put like peers side by side, add PEG, give each number a light, and the contradictions between ratios show you what to read next.

The problem

No ratio stands alone

Each ratio answers a single question. P/E: what does the market pay for a rupee of profit? Margin: how much of a sale is kept? ROE: how hard does the owners’ money work? None of them says whether the profit turns into cash, or whether the company survives a bad year. Take Ramu and a one-off rival, Company X, both at a P/E of 20 example. The P/E bars are identical. Everything else differs. X is made up for this page and changes no figure of Ramu’s.

A story needs several ratios, and each ratio needs something to be compared with. The rest of this part is about doing both.

Compared with what?

Compare like with like

A ratio is a number; a peer comparison makes it a judgement. It only works if the peers are really alike in three ways. Same industry: tea, steel and software keep different margins and need different amounts of capital. Same size band: a ₹300 cr firm and a ₹20,000 cr one face different costs and different risks. Same accounting basis: standalone or consolidated (Part 2), closing or average capital (Part 10), reported or adjusted EPS (Part 6), and the same year-end and price date.

Ramu, Pahadi Leaf (sales ₹800 cr) and Masala Brew (₹300 cr) are a fair set example: same business, same 10 crore shares, sales within a 3-to-1 band. TCPL’s consolidated sales of ₹20,290.43 cr public are 25 times Pahadi’s, and a group with overseas and food businesses. Part 26 covers peer multiples and historical P/E bands properly.

Price against growth

PEG: P/E divided by growth

A P/E of 35 is high next to a P/E of 15, unless the first company is growing much faster. PEG puts them on one line: P/E divided by EPS growth, with growth written as a plain number of percentage points. Ramu: 20 ÷ 13.6 = 1.47, which this series rounds to 1.5. Pahadi Leaf: 15 ÷ 8 = 1.9. Masala Brew: 35 ÷ 22 = 1.6 example. Growth here is the four-year EPS CAGR (Part 9).

PEG = P/E ÷ EPS growth (in % points) = 20 ÷ 13.6 = 1.5

Read it as the number of years of growth you are paying for. Pahadi has the lowest P/E and the highest PEG: cheap on one question, dear for the growth it has. A folk rule treats a PEG near 1 as fair and higher as demanding more faith; that is a starting question, not a law.

Where PEG breaks

PEG’s limits

PEG is one division, and the answer depends on which growth you divide by. Fix the P/E at Ramu’s 20 and move the growth example: at 13.6% the PEG is 1.5, at 2% it is 10, and at zero or negative growth it is meaningless.

  1. Which growth? Past CAGR, last year, or a forecast. Each gives a different PEG for the same price (Part 7 covers forward P/E).
  2. Will it last? A 22% four-year pace may not repeat; a small fade changes the PEG a lot.
  3. Is the growth cheap to get? PEG ignores the capital growth needs. Growth that eats cash (Part 9) is not the same as growth that returns it.
  4. Is it a rebound? After a bad year, EPS growth can look huge for a company that is merely recovering.
Go deeper: variants of PEG

Some analysts use a forecast growth rate (forward PEG), some add the dividend yield to the growth (so a payer is not penalised), and some use revenue growth. Whatever the variant, compare only like with like, and say which one you used. This series’ PEG is the plain version above.

Rules of thumb

Traffic lights are rules of thumb

A dashboard gives each ratio a light: ● good, ◆ watch or ▲ weak. The line between them is a judgement, and it moves with the industry. A debt-to-equity of 0.9 is a warning for an asset-light brand and ordinary for a firm that builds plants. For banks, debt is the raw material and the ratio means little (Part 29 covers them). The lines below are illustrations example, not industry norms.

On this page the rule is simple: a light is good when the number is on the right side of the line, watch when it is within 20% of the line on the wrong side, and weak beyond that. Every light carries a shape as well as a colour, so it reads without colour vision. The lab lets you move every line.

When they disagree

Contradictions are information

Pahadi’s P/E is the lowest of the three, yet its cash is the weakest. It reported ₹56 cr of profit but only ₹39.2 cr of operating cash, 70 paise per rupee; Ramu and Masala bring in more cash than profit example. When two ratios disagree, neither is wrong. Reading a ratio sheet

One label, several numbers

Screening sites and company factsheets print a ratio sheet like our dashboard. The same label can hide different numbers. Each of these three belongs to TCPL, the same company in the same year, and each is correct on its own definition public.

  1. Which basis? Standalone or consolidated, and which year or trailing four quarters (Part 2).
  2. Which price, and when? Every price ratio moves daily. Note the date.
  3. Which definition? ROCE, debt with or without leases, EPS reported or adjusted. Look for a definitions page.
  4. Which peers? A site’s industry group can be broad. Check the size and the business.
  5. What is blank? “Not meaningful”, zero or negative values usually mean a loss or a missing input, not a good score.
  6. How did it move? Read five years of each ratio, not one point, and check one number against the filing itself.

No site is named or endorsed here. Part 36 covers screening: filters, value traps and survivorship bias.

Read the dashboard as a story. The cash and the balance sheet are strong: operating cash of ₹2,421.85 cr is 1.57 times profit, there is net cash of ₹926 cr on the strict definition, and interest is covered 15.8 times. The returns are modest: ROE 7.08% and ROCE 8.55%, because ₹11,883.90 cr of goodwill and ₹7,111.76 cr of other intangibles from acquisitions sit in the capital base (Part 10). And the multiples are high: P/E 69.7 and EV/EBITDA 38.7. A multiple that high means the market expects a lot, in growth, quality or both. Part 25 asks what growth a price like that needs.

That is a description, not a verdict. A dashboard says what the numbers are; whether the price is right is a question for valuation, which comes later in the series.

Go deeper: choices behind the price ratios

P/B uses market cap over equity attributable to owners (₹21,787.53 cr), because the market cap belongs to them; EV adds the NCI back because EBITDA is consolidated in full. Leases (₹699.20 cr) are left out of EV, which would lift it slightly. The dividend yield uses the ₹10 per share recommended for FY26 (₹989.56 cr, paid in FY27); the dividend actually paid during FY26 was ₹8.25 per share. PEG here divides by only one year of EPS growth, a thin basis; a four-year CAGR, as for the peers, would need the earlier years’ EPS.

So what

Questions before any verdict

  1. Like with like? Same industry, size band, accounting basis, and price date.
  2. Which question does each ratio answer? Cover growth, profitability, safety, and cash and price before concluding anything.
  3. Where do two ratios disagree? That is the next thing to read, in the filings and in the business.
  4. Whose line is it? Thresholds are rules of thumb, and they move with the industry.

A dashboard shows what the numbers say. It cannot say why Masala Brew earns 26% on its equity, or how long it can. Part 16, “Business model and unit economics”, starts Stage 4: the business behind the numbers.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
No ratio stands alone: line up growth, profitability, safety, cash and price for like peers, let the lights point to where ratios disagree, and then go and find out why.
