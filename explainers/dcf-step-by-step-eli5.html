DCF, step by step

A discounted cash flow (DCF) values a business as the free cash it will produce in future, shrunk to what that cash is worth today. Four steps take Ramu Chai from a profit forecast to ₹76.7 a share, and show which guesses decide the answer.

The idea

A business is worth its future cash

A share is a slice of a business (Part 1). A DCF puts a number on the business: forecast the free cash it will produce each year, for as long as it exists, then shrink each rupee by how long you must wait for it. Ramu’s forecast cash grows every year example; the far years count for least today.

There are four steps, one panel each below: forecast the cash, discount it, add a terminal value for all the years after the forecast, then go from the whole firm to one share.

Step 1 · Forecast

Free cash is profit minus reinvestment

Start from NOPAT, net operating profit after tax: EBIT taxed as if the company had no debt. Ramu’s FY26 EBIT of 73 at 25% tax gives 54.75 example. To grow, part of it must go back in as capex and working capital, and how much depends on the return that new money earns (Part 10). Growing 12% at a 20% return needs 12 ÷ 20 = 60% of NOPAT reinvested. What is left is free cash flow to the firm (FCFF).

FCFF = NOPAT × (1 − growth ÷ return on new investment)
FY27: NOPAT 54.75 × 1.12 = 61.3 → 61.3 × (1 − 12% ÷ 20%) = 24.5

Slower growth needs less reinvestment, so free cash jumps when growth eases: FY32’s NOPAT is 1.7 times FY27’s, but its free cash is 2.5 times. The 20% is Ramu’s ROE and ROCE, both about 20% (Part 10), assumed to hold for new money too. That is an assumption, not a fact, and the lab lets you change it.

Go deeper: why EBIT, and where growth ÷ return comes from

Why EBIT, not profit after tax. This cash belongs to lenders and owners together. Interest is paid out of it, so it is not taken off here; the cost of both kinds of money is in the discount rate instead. Other income (Ramu’s ₹4 cr of interest on its cash) is left out too, because the cash itself is counted in Step 4, when net debt is subtracted. Counting both would count the cash twice.

Why growth ÷ return. To lift FY27’s NOPAT of 61.3 by 12% in FY28 Ramu needs 7.4 more profit a year. If each new rupee invested earns 20%, that takes 7.4 ÷ 20% = 36.8 of new investment, which is 60% of 61.3. The same logic sets reinvestment at 40% when growth is 8%, and 25% when it is 5%.

The forecast

Ten years at two speeds, then for ever

The plan example: 12% a year from FY27 to FY31, close to Ramu’s recent pace (sales grew 11.0% a year and profit 13.6% over FY22–26, Part 9); 8% from FY32 to FY36 as it matures; then 5% a year for ever. The last rate is kept below the long-run growth of the economy in rupee terms, because no company can outgrow its economy for ever. Reinvestment falls from 60% of NOPAT to 40%, then 25%.

Step 2 · Discount

Shrink each year’s cash to today

Part 23, “Discounting and the discount rate”, builds Ramu’s weighted average cost of capital (WACC): 11.6%, the return lenders and owners together expect for lending to and owning a business like this. Cash due in t years is divided by 1.116 multiplied by itself t times. FY27’s 24.5 is worth 22.0 today; FY36’s 85.1 only 28.4, a third of it example.

Present value = FCFF ÷ 1.116t · FY27: 24.5 ÷ 1.116 = 22.0 · FY36: 85.1 × 0.334 = 28.4

The ten years add up to 262.4 today. Growth makes the later cash bigger and discounting makes it smaller, and here they nearly cancel: each year adds between 22 and 32.

Go deeper: timing, and the other route

This page counts each year’s cash as if it arrived on 31 March, a full year after the last, and values the company on 31 March 2026. Many analysts assume cash arrives through the year, on average mid-year, which lifts the value by half a year of discounting, about 5.6% at 11.6%. Neither is wrong; say which you used.

FCFF goes with the WACC because both cover lenders and owners. The other route discounts the cash left for owners alone, after interest and debt repayments, at the cost of equity (12% for Ramu, Part 23). Done carefully the two land in the same place; mixing them, say owners’ cash at the WACC, does not.

Step 3 · Terminal value

Everything after FY36, in one number

The forecast stops; the business does not. If free cash grows at a steady rate g for ever, all of it is worth next year’s cash ÷ (WACC − g), valued one year before that cash arrives. FY37’s NOPAT is 141.8 × 1.05 = 148.9; at 5% growth only 25% is reinvested, leaving 111.65. At FY36 the rest of time is worth 1,691.6, and today 564.5 example.

Terminal value at FY36 = FCFF FY37 ÷ (WACC − g) = 111.65 ÷ (11.6% − 5%) = 1,691.6
Worth today = 1,691.6 ÷ 1.11610 = 1,691.6 × 0.334 = 564.5

The formula only works while the WACC is above g. As g creeps up to the WACC the answer runs off towards infinity, which is a warning about the formula, not a discovery about the company.

Go deeper: why the formula works, and exit multiples

A cash flow C next year, growing at g and discounted at r, is worth C ÷ (1 + r) + C(1 + g) ÷ (1 + r)² + … , a series that adds up to C ÷ (r − g) when r is above g.

Some analysts use an exit multiple instead, such as an EV/EBITDA at FY36 (Part 14). That hides the same growth and return assumptions inside a multiple. Ramu’s terminal value is 11.4 times FY37’s NOPAT; check that any exit multiple you pick implies something sensible.

The surprise

Two-thirds of the value comes after FY36

Put the pieces on one bar, to scale. The ten forecast years give 262.4 of the 826.9 total, 32%. The years after FY36 give 564.5: 68% of the value rests on what happens more than ten years from now example.

That is normal for a growing company, and it is why the terminal assumptions deserve the most scrutiny. Move terminal growth from 4% to 6% and the terminal share moves from 66% to 71%, and the value a share from ₹72.0 to ₹82.9.

Step 4 · Per share

From the whole firm to one share

The 826.9 is enterprise value (EV): what the whole business is worth to lenders and owners together (Part 14). The lenders’ claim comes off first. Net debt, borrowings of 100 less cash of 40, is 60, which leaves equity of 766.9 for the owners. Divide by 10 crore shares example.

Value a share = (EV − net debt) ÷ shares = (826.9 − 60) ÷ 10 = ₹76.7, against a price of ₹100

At ₹100 the market’s own EV is 1,060 (Part 14), 233 more than this forecast. So the market is pricing in more cash, or safer cash, than these assumptions give. Finding out which is the job of the next three parts.

Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

NOPAT starts at FY26’s 54.75 and grows at the chosen rate each year. Reinvestment each year is NOPAT × growth ÷ return on new investment, so a return below the growth rate makes free cash negative: the company has to raise money to grow. Cash is counted at each year end and discounted to 31 March 2026. The terminal value uses FY37’s cash at the terminal growth rate. Net debt (60) and shares (10 crore) stay fixed. The WACC slider never goes below 8% and terminal growth never above 7%, so the WACC is always above it.

Sensitivity

Small changes in WACC, big changes in value

The discount rate is an estimate too (Part 23). Everything else held at the base case, Ramu’s value a share runs from ₹108.7 at a WACC of 10% to ₹59.5 at 13% example. One point either side of 11.6% gives ₹94.5 and ₹63.7.

The terminal value takes most of the swing. It is the most distant cash, and the WACC appears in it twice: once in the discount factor and again in WACC − g. At 10% the terminal value is 75% of EV; at 13%, 63%.

Reconcile

Why 11.75 here, when Part 13 found 13

Part 13’s FY26 free cash flow was cash from operations 58 less capex 45 = 13. The DCF’s version for FY26 is NOPAT 54.75 less reinvestment of 43 (capex 45 less depreciation 12 = 33 of net capex, plus 10 into working capital) = 11.75 example. Both start from EBITDA of 85 and take off the same capex and working capital. The gap of 1.25 is tax, and behind the tax, interest.

  1. Part 13 takes off the tax actually paid, 17. Interest is deductible, so that bill is lower than it would be with no debt.
  2. The DCF takes off tax on EBIT, 73 × 25% = 18.25, as if Ramu had neither debt nor interest income. Net interest of 10 − 4 = 6 saves 25% × 6 = 1.5 of tax; the other −0.25 is because the 17 paid is a little more than 25% of profit before tax of 67 (16.75).
  3. The saving is not lost. The WACC uses the after-tax cost of debt, 7.5% rather than 10% (Part 23). Counting the saving in the cash as well would count it twice.

So 13 and 11.75 are both right, for different questions. A DCF uses the one its discount rate was built for.

Go deeper: FY26 against the forecast

In FY26 Ramu reinvested 43, 79% of NOPAT. The forecast assumes 60% for FY27, which needs the 20% return on new money to hold. If FY26’s rate were the norm at 12% growth, the return on new money would be nearer 12% ÷ 79% ≈ 15%, and the value a share ₹61.5 instead of ₹76.7. One year is a thin basis either way, which is why Part 13 reads five.

The common mistake

₹76.7 is not a verdict on ₹100

It is tempting to read “value ₹76.7, price ₹100” as “overpriced by 30%”. Every input above is a judgement. Move each one across a range a careful analyst could defend, one at a time, and the value moves a long way example.

No single range reaches ₹100, but two modest moves together do: a WACC of 10.6% with growth of 14% to FY31 gives ₹100.3. The gap between ₹76.7 and ₹100 says that the price assumes more growth, better returns on new money or a lower discount rate than this forecast. Whether those assumptions are right is the real question.

A DCF is only as good as its assumptions. Its answer looks precise, to a tenth of a rupee, but it inherits every guess about growth, returns and the discount rate, and two-thirds of it comes from years nobody can see. Use it to make those guesses explicit and testable, not as the answer. Part 28 turns this into scenarios and a margin of safety.

In FY26 TCPL spent less on capex (448.57) than it charged as D&A (626.68) and released working capital, so its free cash flow to the firm, 1,955.0, was larger than its NOPAT, 1,631.7: negative reinvestment, on Step 1’s formula. For a company that grows by buying, reinvestment has to include the acquisitions and be averaged over several years, and the return on new investment has to count the price paid: TCPL’s ROCE is 8.55% with goodwill in the base (Part 15).

So what

Questions before trusting any DCF

  1. What growth, for how long? Compare it with the company’s record and its industry (Parts 9 and 17).
  2. What does new money earn? Growth adds value only when that return beats the WACC. The lab’s “Growth that only earns its cost” shows growth adding almost nothing.
  3. How much is terminal value? If it is two-thirds or more, the answer rests on the far future: test terminal growth and the WACC.
  4. Which cash, which rate? Cash to the firm with the WACC, then net debt off at the end; cash to owners with the cost of equity. Never mix them.

A DCF asks what a set of assumptions makes the shares worth. Part 25, “Reverse DCF”, turns the question round: what growth does the ₹100 price already assume? On this model, about 15.3% a year for all ten years.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A DCF is free cash forecast, shrunk to today and split by the shares. Ramu’s ₹76.7 rests on growth, the return on new money, a discount rate and a terminal value that is two-thirds of the answer, so test the assumptions before trusting the number.
