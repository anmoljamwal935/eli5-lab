Corporate actions and dilution

Some corporate actions only cut the same company into more or bigger slices, and change nothing you own. Others add shares or take them away, and change your share of the profit. For those, the price paid decides whether you win or lose.

Built from SEBI’s own regulations as amended (Buy-back to 6 July 2026, Issue of Capital to 21 March 2026, Listing Obligations to 15 October 2026, Employee Benefits to 4 December 2025), Tata Consumer Products’ 2024 rights-issue letter of offer and its Integrated Annual Report 2025-26 · Read the source ↗

Already covered: Part 7 divides profit by the number of shares to get earnings per share (EPS), Part 14 reads book value and dividends, and Part 18 asks when a buyback beats the other uses of spare cash. This part shows what each corporate action does to the shares, the profit per share and your stake. Facts carry a tag: public is a figure from a filing or a SEBI regulation, example is a made-up number for our teaching company, Ramu Chai Ltd: 10 crore shares of ₹10 face value at ₹100, profit ₹50 cr, EPS ₹5.00, equity ₹250 cr. All amounts are ₹ crore (cr) unless a rupee sign says per share. Tax on buybacks and dividends is Part 38’s subject. Go deeper boxes are optional.

The question

Re-slicing, or changing your slice?

Picture Ramu’s ₹50 cr profit as one bar, cut into one slice per crore shares example. A bonus issue cuts the same bar into twenty thinner slices and gives each holder two for one: your profit is still ₹5 cr. A share sale to new investors adds an eleventh slice, so yours shrinks to ₹4.55 cr until the new money earns. A buyback removes half a slice, so yours grows to ₹5.26 cr.

So every action falls into one of two groups. Re-slicing (bonus issue, split, demerger) leaves your share of everything unchanged, however the price per share moves. Changing the slices (rights issue, QIP, employee options, buyback) moves money in or out at some price. Then the question is the one Part 18 asked of buybacks: is that price above or below what a share is really worth?

Who gets it

Record date and ex-date

A dividend, bonus shares, rights and demerger shares all go to the holders on the company’s list on one day, the record date. A listed company must tell the exchanges at least three working days ahead, excluding the day it tells them and the record date itself (SEBI Listing Regulations, regulation 42(2), as amended to 15 October 2026) public. For a scheme of arrangement such as a demerger the notice is seven working days.

A purchase only puts you on the list once it settles. Indian shares settle one working day after the trade (T+1), fully in place since 27 January 2023, according to SEBI’s August 2026 consultation paper public. So when the record date is a trading day, it is also the ex-date: buy that day and you miss out. On the ex-date the price drops by what was detached, the dividend or the value of the right, and after a bonus or split it simply resets to the new number of shares.

Bonus issue

A bonus moves reserves into share capital

In a 1:1 bonus Ramu gives one new share for every share held example. No cash moves. ₹100 cr is relabelled from reserves to share capital, so 20 crore shares now exist, the price settles near ₹50, EPS halves to ₹2.50 and book value per share falls from ₹25 to ₹12.50 (Part 14). Market cap stays ₹1,000 cr. A holder of 100 shares worth ₹10,000 now has 200 worth ₹10,000.

SEBI’s rules for listed companies (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements Regulations, 2018, as amended to 21 March 2026) public: a bonus can only come out of free reserves, the securities premium account or the capital redemption reserve, built from genuine profits or premium collected in cash, never from revaluing assets (regulation 294(3)); it cannot be issued in place of a dividend (294(4)); and once announced it cannot be withdrawn. If shareholders must approve it, it has to be done, meaning the new shares start trading, within two months of the board’s decision; if not, within fifteen days (regulation 295).

Stock split

A split cuts each share smaller

A split does the same to the share count by a different route. Ramu splits its ₹10 face value into two ₹5 shares example: 20 crore shares, price near ₹50, EPS ₹2.50, market cap ₹1,000 cr, exactly as with the bonus. But nothing is moved out of reserves: share capital stays ₹100 cr, now 20 crore shares of ₹5. Face value is only an accounting unit; it is not what a share is worth.

Why bother? A lower price per share can make the shares easier to trade in small amounts, and a bonus signals that the board is comfortable locking reserves into capital, which cannot be paid out as a dividend. Neither creates value. If a stock rises after a split, something else changed. Every past EPS and price you compare with has to be divided by the same factor, which the common-mistake panel shows.

Buyback

A buyback retires shares for cash

Ramu buys back 0.5 crore shares at ₹100, Part 18’s choice example: ₹50 cr of cash goes to the sellers, 9.5 crore shares remain, EPS rises to ₹5.26 and market cap falls to ₹950 cr while the price stays ₹100. Equity falls by the cash paid, from ₹250 cr to ₹200 cr. This ignores the interest the cash was earning; the lab lets you add it back. (Ramu ended FY26 with ₹40 cr of cash; Part 18’s ₹50 cr is a teaching sum.)

EPS always rises after a buyback, because the profit is divided by fewer shares, so EPS growth alone proves nothing. Holders who stay gain only if the price paid is below what a share is worth, and sellers take the difference when it is above (Part 18 draws both).

The limits, from SEBI’s Buy-back of Securities Regulations, 2018, as amended to 6 July 2026 public:

  1. Size. At most 25% of paid-up capital plus free reserves, on the standalone or consolidated accounts, whichever gives the lower amount; and at most 25% of the paid-up equity capital in shares bought in a year (regulation 4(i)). Up to 10% a board resolution is enough; above that shareholders must pass a special resolution (5(i)(b)). Ramu’s ₹50 cr is 20% of its ₹250 cr, so it needs the vote.
  2. Debt. Borrowings afterwards may be at most twice paid-up capital plus free reserves (4(ii)). Ramu’s ₹100 cr against ₹200 cr is 0.5 times.
  3. Routes. A tender offer buys from all holders in proportion, with 15% of the shares reserved for small shareholders, those holding no more than ₹2 lakh of shares on the record date (regulation 6). The open market route runs through book-building or through the stock exchange. The stock-exchange route was barred from 1 April 2025 and returns from 1 August 2026, capped below 15% of paid-up capital and free reserves (4(iv)); promoters cannot sell into it (SEBI board decision, 19 June 2026).
  4. Timing. A buyback must finish within a year of the resolution (5(ii)), and the company may not raise fresh capital for a year after the buyback period ends, except to meet existing obligations (24(i)(f)).

Tax: SEBI said it brought back the stock-exchange route “in light of the revision in taxation framework” for buybacks (same board decision). Part 38 covers the tax itself, so no rates are given here.

Rights issue

A rights issue: buy, sell or lose

Ramu offers 1 new share for every 10 held at ₹80, below the ₹100 price example. If everyone takes it up: 11 crore shares, ₹80 cr raised, market cap ₹1,080 cr, and a theoretical ex-rights price of ₹98.18. EPS falls to ₹4.55 before the new money earns anything. The discount is not a gift: you pay it to yourself. What matters is what you do with the right.

Theoretical ex-rights price = (10 × ₹100 + 1 × ₹80) ÷ 11 = ₹98.18 · a right is worth ₹98.18 − ₹80 = ₹18.18

Subscribe and you keep exactly your share of the company, with ₹800 more of your money in it. Sell the right and you are made whole in cash. Do nothing and you lose ₹182, because the discount now goes to whoever did subscribe. The right arrives in your demat account as a rights entitlement, and it can be sold on the exchange until a cut-off date during the issue (regulation 77A and the issue’s own timetable) public.

SEBI’s rules (Issue of Capital Regulations, as amended to 21 March 2026) public: the company fixes the issue price before it fixes the record date, and the price may not be below face value (regulation 73); once the record date is announced the issue cannot be withdrawn without a twelve-month listing ban (68(2)); and if less than 90% is subscribed the money is refunded, unless the issue is not for a capital project and the promoters take up their full share (86).

QIP

A QIP sells new shares to institutions

A qualified institutions placement (QIP) sells new shares privately to qualified institutional buyers such as mutual funds and insurers. You are not asked. Ramu sells 1 crore new shares at ₹95 example: ₹95 cr raised, 11 crore shares, EPS ₹4.55 before the new money earns, and today’s holders go from owning 100% of Ramu to 90.9%.

Because you cannot join in, the price is your only protection. SEBI sets a floor: the average of the weekly high and low of the closing prices over the two weeks before the relevant date, the day the board or its committee decides to open the issue; shareholders can approve a discount of up to 5% on it (regulations 171(b) and 176(1), as amended to 21 March 2026) public. At least 10% must go to mutual funds, and nothing to the promoters (179(2)).

The ten closes are example prices example: week 1 runs from ₹98 to ₹103 and week 2 from ₹97 to ₹102, so the four numbers average ₹100 and the lowest allowed price, with the discount, is ₹95. Selling at ₹95 something worth ₹100 costs today’s holders 45 paise a share. If Ramu were really worth ₹130, it would cost them much more; if the ₹95 cr earns well, they gain. The lab runs both.

Employee options

ESOPs: a small, steady dilution

An employee stock option (ESOP) is a right to buy a share later at a fixed exercise price. Ramu’s staff hold 0.2 crore options at ₹60 example. When they exercise, Ramu issues 0.2 crore new shares (2.0% more), receives ₹12 cr, and EPS falls to ₹4.90. Shares worth ₹99.22 each change hands for ₹60.

The ₹7.84 cr gap is real pay to employees, taken from today’s holders as 78 paise a share. Under SEBI’s Share Based Employee Benefits and Sweat Equity Regulations, 2021 (as amended to 4 December 2025) a company is free to set the exercise price (regulation 17), and options must wait at least one year before they vest (18(1)) public. Two things to check in a report: the share-based payment cost in employee expenses, and diluted EPS, which divides profit by the shares that would exist if options were exercised. Small each year, it compounds: 2% a year for five years is 10.4% more shares.

Demerger

A demerger splits one company in two

In a demerger a business moves into a new company and its shares go to the old company’s holders. Suppose Ramu’s cafés, ₹125 cr of the ₹500 cr sales (Part 16), become Ramu Cafés Ltd, with 1 new share for every 10 Ramu shares example. A holder of 100 Ramu shares ends up with 100 Ramu Chai and 10 Ramu Cafés shares.

That is re-slicing again: you own the same 1-in-10-crore share of both pieces, so on day one nothing you own changes, while Ramu Chai’s price drops by what left. What changes later is that each piece is priced on its own, which can close or open a gap like the holding-company discount in Part 27. A merger runs the other way: your company issues shares to another company’s holders, so judge it by what it gets for those shares.

Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

Ramu starts at 10 crore shares, profit ₹50 cr and a market price of ₹100. The company is worth your estimate times 10 crore shares; cash raised adds to that value rupee for rupee, and cash paid out takes away from it. The profit changes only by what the cash earns, at the rate you set (for a buyback, what the cash would have earned). Valuing new cash at what it cost is a simplification: whether a project is worth more than it costs is Part 18’s question. The rights issue assumes the market prices the right at its theoretical value. The lab applies three SEBI limits and says so when it does: a buyback stops at 25% of the shares or ₹62.5 cr, 25% of Ramu’s ₹250 cr of capital and reserves, treating all reserves as free; a rights price cannot go below the ₹10 face value; a QIP cannot go below ₹95, assuming a two-week average of ₹100. Options have no SEBI floor. A real split uses a round face value (₹5, ₹2, ₹1); the lab lets the split ratio vary freely.

The common mistake

Cheaper after a split is not cheaper

After a 1:2 split the printed price halves overnight, and a chart that is not adjusted shows a crash that never happened example. The adjusted line divides every earlier price by two, and it shows the truth: the year went up. The same goes for EPS: Ramu’s ₹5.00 becomes ₹2.50 after a 1:1 bonus, so the P/E is still 50 ÷ 2.50 = 20.

  1. Is the history adjusted? Any price or EPS series that crosses a bonus or split must be divided by the same factor. Check the dates against the company’s corporate actions.
  2. Is EPS growth real? EPS growth is roughly profit growth minus the growth in share count. A buyback adds to EPS growth without adding profit; issues and options take it away. Ramu’s share count stayed at 10 crore from FY22 to FY26 (Part 9), so its EPS and profit grew alike.
  3. Who got the price? For every issue or buyback, compare the price paid with your estimate of value. Selling shares cheap or buying them dear moves wealth between groups of holders.
  4. How often? One rights issue to fund a good deal is different from shares issued every year. Part 20 lists repeated dilution as a red flag.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL’s 2024 rights issue, from the filings

Tata Consumer Products (TCPL) public. On 19 January 2024 its board approved commercial paper of up to ₹3,500 cr as bridge funding for buying Capital Foods and Organic India, and a rights issue of up to ₹3,000 cr (outcome of board meeting). The abridged letter of offer names the use: up to ₹2,940 cr to repay the commercial paper that financed those purchases. The letter, dated 23 July 2024, sets the terms: up to 3,66,47,492 shares of ₹1 face value at ₹818 each (₹817 premium), up to ₹2,997.77 cr, 1 new share for every 26 held on the record date, Saturday 27 July 2024.

The record date fell on a Saturday. With T+1 settlement, a buyer on Thursday 25 July settled on Friday and was on the list; a buyer on Friday 26 July settled on Monday, too late. So 25 July was the last day to buy with the right, and the shares traded ex-rights from 26 July. BSE’s own closing prices show the step (bhavcopy, 25 July; 26 July):

A holder who did nothing owns 95.28 ÷ (95.28 + 3.66) = 96.3% of their old stake, diluted by 3.7%. A holder who subscribed kept their share. Whether the ₹2,980 cr was well spent is the acquisition question that Part 18 left open.

So what

Four questions for any corporate action

  1. Re-slice or new slices? A bonus, split or demerger changes the number on the screen, not what you own. Adjust the history and move on.
  2. Who pays what price? For a buyback, rights issue, QIP or options, set the price against your estimate of value. Below value is good for buyers of shares and bad for sellers, whoever they are.
  3. What will the money earn? New cash dilutes EPS until it earns; a buyback spends cash that was earning something. Ask what return management expects, and check later.
  4. Can you take part? In a rights issue you can protect yourself by subscribing or selling the right before the deadline. In a QIP or with options you cannot, so read the pricing and the diluted EPS.

Each action here was decided inside the company. Part 22, “The economy around the company”, looks outward: interest rates, inflation, the rupee, tea prices and the monsoon, and how they move Ramu’s costs and profit.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A bonus, split or demerger only re-slices the same company. A rights issue, QIP, options or buyback changes your slice of the profit, and the price paid against what a share is really worth decides whether that helps you or hurts you.
