Testing Einstein with clocks on a mountain

Gravity slows time, so a clock 2.7 km higher should gain about 25 billionths of a second a day. Checking that to the last digit takes trapped atoms, a laser beamed across Colorado, and a ruler made of light.

The experiment

Two clocks, 2.7 km apart in height

NIST put one ytterbium clock in its Boulder lab (1,650 m) and a twin on the summit of Mount Blue Sky (about 4,300 m). Einstein's general relativity says the higher clock runs faster: about 25 nanoseconds more every day. It's the biggest height gap yet tried with clocks this precise; earlier tests used 450 m (Tokyo Skytree) and about 1 km inside a mountain.

The usual picture

Gravity is mostly time running slow

Science centres show gravity as a ball rolling in a stretchy funnel. For slow things like us and apples, the bigger effect is different: clocks tick slower lower down, and objects fall toward the slower-ticking region.

This "time does most of the work" rule holds for slow objects in weak gravity, which covers everyday life on Earth: in that limit only the time part of spacetime's geometry steers motion (Carroll's GR notes, §4.1). Light and fast objects also feel curved space.

The open question

Where does a mass in two places bend?

Relativity has passed every clock test so far. But quantum mechanics lets a particle sit in two places at once, and relativity can't say how spacetime bends then. Somewhere one theory must give, so physicists hunt for the first crack.

The size of it

The effect hides in the 13th digit

The mountain clock runs fast by 2.885 parts in 1013 (the team's prediction, from gravity surveys at both sites). To test Einstein you then need several more digits: the best ytterbium clocks are accurate to 1.4 parts in 1018 (NIST, 2018): run since the Big Bang, off by less than a second.

The building block

Every clock counts something that swings

A grandfather clock counts a pendulum, a watch counts vibrating quartz, an atomic clock counts light that atoms respond to (not radioactivity; NIST's forerunner built the first one in 1949). The video puts caesium clocks at over 50 billion times more accurate than watch quartz. More swings per second slice the second more finely: ytterbium's light swings about 56,000 times faster than caesium's microwaves.

The mechanism

An atom answers only one exact colour

An electron jumps to a higher energy level only if a photon's frequency matches the gap. Sweep a laser's frequency, find where atoms respond, and keep the laser locked there by checking how many atoms jumped. Counting 9,192,631,770 caesium cycles has defined one second since 1967; it underpins UTC, the time on your phone.

Two different qualities

Steady is not the same as right

Every atom of one isotope has the same natural frequency, so ticks are consistent: that's stability. Whether you read the true frequency depends on how well the machine is built: that's accuracy. Caesium fountains reach about 1 part in 1016; NIST says under a second lost in 100 million years.

The first problem

Moving atoms read the wrong colour

Solid ytterbium blurs the signal (neighbouring atoms tug each other's electrons), so it's boiled into a vapour at about 500 °C. Those atoms fly at roughly 300 m/s, a millionth of light's speed, and the Doppler effect then smears the reading from the 7th digit on.

The fix, part 1

Slowing atoms down by shining light

Beams from every direction are tuned slightly below the atoms' colour. An atom only absorbs from a beam it's flying into (Doppler again), and each photon kicks it backwards. A blue laser cools to about a thousandth of a kelvin, a green one to about a millionth.

The fix, part 2

Trapping cold atoms in a crate of light

A laser bounced back on itself makes a standing wave: fixed bright and dark spots. Its electric field slightly stretches each neutral atom's charges, and the stretched atom is pulled to the bright spots, like a marble rolling to the bottom of a bowl. Pinned there, the atoms barely move.

The climb

Beaming a clock signal across the plains

A smaller copy of the whole clock went up the mountain. A cable wasn't practical (animals would chew it), so a 10 cm laser beam crosses tens of kilometres of air to a rooftop north of Denver (the video says about 80 km; the team's abstract says 69 km), spreads to about a metre, then must enter a fibre core 8 µm wide. The lab's signal makes the same trip back.

Why stay with light

Same blur, much longer timing error

Satellites already carry microwave clocks, and microwaves pass clouds. But to compare clocks you must pin down where each wave is in its cycle, and noise (a poor signal-to-noise ratio) only lets you pin that to a fraction of a cycle. A microwave cycle lasts tens of thousands of times longer, so the same fractional blur is tens of thousands of times more time.

The next target: space

Find each other in space and colour

A team at the University of Western Australia flew an optical clock in a helicopter. Beacon lasers let the ground and air terminals find each other, and a mirror steering 600 times a second fights turbulence. Then the two lasers must match colour within ~5 MHz, a few parts in 100 million (a car radio needs only a fraction of a percent), though they can start gigahertz apart. They got brief locks.

The readout problem

Light ticks too fast to count

Even with the link working, the clock's light delivers about 518 trillion cycles a second. The video's figure for a very fast transistor is about 10 billion switches a second, roughly 50,000 times too slow. The old workaround multiplied a microwave signal up in stages to optical, through long chains of locked oscillators that only a few labs could run.

The trick

Count the beat, not the wave

Strike two tuning forks, 440 Hz and 437 Hz, and the sound swells and fades 3 times a second: a beat at the difference. Overlap two lasers and you get a beat too, slow enough for electronics. Known frequency ± beat = unknown frequency.

The ruler

A comb of perfectly spaced colours

A beat needs a reference within tens of GHz, yet visible light spans ~400,000 GHz. The fix: one laser whose spectrum is a comb of evenly spaced teeth, so some tooth always lands near the clock's colour.

Every term is countable: f_rep is locked to a microwave reference, and the beat is slow. The video's formula leaves out f_CEO, the comb's offset from zero, and flags that on screen; the Nobel committee's background gives the full form.

The history: in 1994 Theodor Hänsch saw a pulsed titanium-sapphire laser at a trade show and guessed its spectrum could be such a ruler; his team then showed the teeth are evenly spaced to a few parts in 1017. In October 1999 John Hall's team in Boulder made the first comb wide enough to measure itself (Hänsch's Nobel lecture). Combs soon read the first optical clocks (on the mountain, comb pulses also carry the clock signal through the air), and Hall and Hänsch shared half the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Try it

Can your clock see a step up?

Assume Einstein is right: lifting a clock by a height h makes it tick faster by g·h/c² (g = 9.80 m/s², c = light speed). That's the dashed line. A clock can only see the shift if its own uncertainty, the teal bar, is smaller. That's why NIST's clock rooms carry survey markers stating their altitude.

The result so far

Einstein holds, to the digits checked

Over the link, the time offset between the clocks ramps steadily, as predicted. A few weeks later the team said: no evidence against Einstein, analysis ongoing. The best ground test so far, Tokyo Skytree in 2020, reached about 1 part in 10,000 of the effect; this team hopes for about 1 in 100,000.

Time-sensitive: the Mount Blue Sky results were not yet published when the video came out (Oct 2026). Prior results: Gravity Probe A and Galileo from Shinkai, Takamoto & Katori's review, which reports Skytree's α = (1.4 ± 9.1) × 10⁻⁵.
