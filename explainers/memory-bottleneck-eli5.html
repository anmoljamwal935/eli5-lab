Memory: the real bottleneck

Parts 5 and 7 left the same clue: an AI chip can do sums far faster than it can fetch the numbers to do them on. This part shows why moving a number costs more than multiplying it, how chips are built around that fact, and why it, more than raw arithmetic, now sets how fast AI answers.

The problem

The chip spends most of its time waiting

Part 5 ended on a puzzle. For one person chatting with an 8-billion-number model, an H100 could do the sums for a token in about 16 millionths of a second, but reading the model’s 16 GB takes about 4.8 thousandths public. The arithmetic finishes almost 295 times sooner. Then the chip waits.

The analogy

A fast cook, a far pantry

Picture a cook who can chop at superhuman speed. A few ingredients sit on the counter, a few more on a shelf, and everything else is in a pantry down a long corridor. However fast the hands, dinner is limited by trips to the pantry. Inside a chip, the cook is the arithmetic, the counter and shelf are small memories on the chip, and the pantry is the main memory beside it.

Where the analogy breaks: the pantry is not slow to walk to so much as narrow to carry from. Fetching one number takes a while, but the real limit is how many numbers per second the corridor can carry.

The building block

Every trip costs energy

Moving a number costs energy, and the further it travels the more it costs. On a 2014-era chip, a 16-bit multiply took about 1.1 picojoules. Reading 64 bits from a small nearby cache took 10, and from main memory 1,300 to 2,600: roughly a thousand times the sum public. Today’s numbers are all smaller, but the gap is the point.

Go deeper: where these numbers come from

They are Mark Horowitz’s “rough energy costs” for a 45 nm chip, from his 2014 ISSCC keynote (Figure 1.1.9) public. The memory figures are for a 64-bit read. He notes that even with better wiring a DRAM access would still cost about 10 pJ per bit, far more than a core operation, and that efficient designs need “roughly a thousand operations” per DRAM fetch.

The ladder

Small and near, or big and far

So chips keep a ladder of memories. The nearest are tiny: an H100 has 256 KB of registers and 256 KB of L1 cache in each of its 132 processing blocks, about 34 MB of each in total, and a 50 MB L2 cache shared by all public. The model itself lives in 80 GB of HBM beside the chip public. Near memory is fast but small, so most numbers must come from far away.

Why HBM

A very wide road next door

Part 5 met HBM: memory chips stacked and placed beside the GPU. Its trick is width. One HBM3 stack talks over 1,024 wires, each up to 6.4 billion bits a second, about 819 GB/s public. An H100 has five stacks and 10 memory controllers of 512 bits, 5,120 wires in all, for 3,350 GB/s public. That is over thirty times a laptop CPU’s 100 GB/s, and still the bottleneck.

The mechanism

Count the sums per byte fetched

The useful question is how many sums a task does for each byte it fetches. While writing one token for one person, each 16-bit number (2 bytes) is used for one multiply and one add, then not needed again: 1 sum per byte public. The H100 can do about 295 sums in the time one byte arrives (989 trillion sums a second divided by 3.35 trillion bytes) public. So it idles.

The picture

The roofline: two ceilings

Computer architects draw this as a roofline. Speed is capped by whichever is lower: the chip’s peak sums, or memory speed times sums per byte public. Below the ridge, about 295 sums per byte on an H100, a task is memory-bound: faster maths would not help. Above it, it is compute-bound. One chat sits far down the slope.

Go deeper: training and reading a prompt

Training and reading your prompt are different. A prompt of 1,000 tokens goes through the model in one pass, so each number fetched is used about 1,000 times, past the ridge example. Training works on huge batches the same way. The one-token-at-a-time writing of an answer is the part stuck on the memory slope.

Try it

Is the chip waiting or working?

One H100 writes the next token for several people at once. Each step reads the whole model from HBM once and does two sums per number per person. Whichever takes longer sets the step time, shown by the dashed line. The extra memory for each chat’s saved keys (Part 7) is left out example.

The fix

Serve many people with one read

If 64 people’s next tokens are worked out together, each number fetched is used 64 times. The read time stays the same while the sums grow, so tokens per second rise almost 64-fold. Around 295 people the sums catch up with the reading, and adding more stops helping example. This trick, batching, is the heart of the bonus study.

Another fix

Keep the work on the chip

Attention (Part 7) makes a grid of scores for every pair of words. The standard version in the FlashAttention paper writes that grid out to HBM, reads it back, writes the shares out and reads them back again public. FlashAttention instead works on tiles small enough to stay on the chip, so the grid never leaves it public. Same answer, fewer trips: its authors measured GPT-2 training about 3 times faster than standard versions public.

The trend

Maths grew faster than memory

The gap keeps widening. Across 20 years of server chips, peak sums per second grew about 3.0 times every two years, memory speed only 1.6 times, and links between chips 1.4 times public. Researchers call this the memory wall. Over ten years that is roughly 243 times more maths but only 10 times more memory speed.

The common mistake

More sums a second is not faster chat

It is natural to compare chips by their headline sums per second. For one person chatting, that number barely matters. Double the sums and the chat speed stays at about 209 tokens a second; double the memory speed and it doubles example. When you read a chip’s launch, look for memory bandwidth first.

So what

Next: the transformer, assembled

Moving numbers, not multiplying them, sets the speed of AI today. That is why chips stack memory beside the processor, why models shrink their numbers, and why one chip serves many people at once. Part 9, back on the LLM track, puts every piece from Parts 1, 2, 6 and 7 together into one full pass through a model, and counts where all those numbers live.

  • When a new chip launches, compare its memory bandwidth, not just its sums per second.
  • When a chatbot slows as more people join, ask whether it is waiting on memory or on maths.
  • When a model is offered in 4-bit form, expect it to answer faster, at some cost in accuracy.
AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A chip that could do a token’s sums in microseconds waits milliseconds for its numbers, so AI speed is set by how many bytes it can move, and every trick, from HBM to batching to 4-bit numbers, is a way to move fewer.
