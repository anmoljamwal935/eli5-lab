Debt: how much is too much

Borrowing is cheap, lifts the owners’ return and costs nothing extra to run, until a bad year arrives and the interest is still due. This part measures how much debt a company carries and how much room it has before the bill bites.

Built from the audited FY2026 consolidated statements of Tata Consumer Products (TCPL), as printed in its Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, and of Tata Steel, as printed in its Integrated Report & Annual Accounts 2025-26, plus the arithmetic of the ratios · Read the TCPL source ↗ · Read the Tata Steel source ↗

Already covered: Part 3 reads the P&L down to EBIT and finance costs, Part 4 shows borrowings and cash on the balance sheet, Part 5 shows interest leaving as cash, Part 8 shows how fixed costs magnify swings in profit, and Part 10 shows leverage lifting ROE. This part adds the risk side. Facts carry a tag: public is a figure from a company’s audited statements, example is a made-up number for our teaching company, Ramu Chai Ltd, a small tea business. All amounts are ₹ crore (cr). Part 12 covers working capital, Part 23 the cost of capital, Parts 29–30 how banks and NBFCs use debt (it works differently for them), and Part 33 cyclical businesses. Go deeper boxes are optional.

Definitions used across the series. Net debt = borrowings − cash and cash equivalents; lease liabilities are left out unless we say so. EBIT = EBITDA − depreciation and amortisation, so before other income and exceptional items. Interest cover = EBIT ÷ finance costs as printed. Companies publish their own versions, and we show where they differ.

money coming in: EBITDA, cash money going out: tax, extra interest belongs to shareholders: profit, equity what is owed: debt, interest, leases EBIT the market’s view

Why borrow

Debt is cheap money, and tax helps

A company can fund itself with the owners’ money (equity) or with a lender’s. Lenders are paid before owners and have a fixed claim, so they accept a smaller return than owners, who take the risk (Part 23 puts a number on that). Better still, interest is deducted before tax. Ramu’s ₹10 cr of interest example saves about ₹2.5 cr of tax at 25%, so it costs ₹7.5 cr. A dividend, paid from profit after tax, gets no such help. Borrowing also issues no new shares, so nobody’s ownership is diluted.

Ramu pays about 10% on ₹100 cr of borrowings example, which is 7.5% after tax. That is the price. The next panel shows what the owners get for paying it.

What debt buys the owners

Debt magnifies the owners’ return

Part 10 showed ROE = margin × turnover × equity multiplier, and called leverage borrowing in disguise. Here is the effect in money. Ramu earns 20.9% on all its capital before interest (ROCE, Part 10) but pays lenders only 10%, so the gap goes to the owners: ₹50 cr on ₹250 cr of equity is an ROE of 20.0% example. Fund the same business entirely with equity (₹350 cr, no interest, same tax rate) and profit is about ₹57.5 cr but ROE is only 16.4%. This is a one-off comparison; it does not change Ramu’s accounts.

The rule: borrowing lifts ROE only while the business earns more on its capital than the debt costs. When it earns less, the same arithmetic cuts ROE. That is why the next panel matters.

The price of the loan

Interest is due in bad years too

Owners can be paid nothing in a bad year. Lenders cannot: interest is a fixed promise, so it comes out of EBIT first and owners get what is left. Because Ramu’s costs are mostly fixed (Part 8), EBIT swings harder than EBITDA, and what is left for owners swings harder still. At EBITDA −80% EBIT is ₹5 cr, half the interest example. Ramu’s ₹4 cr of interest income would help a little, but cover (later in this part) ignores it.

Miss interest and you are in default: lenders can demand their money back and, in the end, take the company to insolvency. That asymmetry is the whole danger of debt. Good years hide it; one bad year shows it.

Measuring stick 1 and 2

Debt to equity, and net debt

Debt to equity (D/E) is borrowings ÷ equity: Ramu’s ₹100 cr ÷ ₹250 cr = 0.40 example. Equity is the cushion beneath the lenders. Net debt subtracts cash, because cash could repay debt tomorrow: ₹100 cr − ₹40 cr = ₹60 cr, or 0.24 of equity. If cash exceeds borrowings, net debt is negative, which is called net cash.

D/E = borrowings ÷ equity = 100 ÷ 250 = 0.40 · net debt = 100 − 40 = 60
Go deeper: other versions of the same idea

Some screens divide total liabilities, not just borrowings, by equity; that number is higher and mixes in supplier credit. Some count only long-term debt. Companies also add things (leases, bank deposits, investments). This series uses borrowings and cash and cash equivalents only, and flags any other definition. For consolidated accounts we use total equity, including the minority’s share, since lenders to the group lend against all of it.

Measuring stick 3

Net debt to EBITDA: years to repay

EBITDA is the cash-like profit the business throws off before interest, tax and replacing plant. Dividing net debt by it gives a rough count of years of EBITDA the debt equals: Ramu’s ₹60 cr ÷ ₹85 cr = 0.71×, about eight and a half months example. A common rule of thumb treats levels above about 3× as stretched. That is not a law: steady, contract-backed businesses can carry more, and swingy ones should carry less.

net debt ÷ EBITDA = 60 ÷ 85 = 0.71×

Treat it as a first filter. EBITDA ignores tax, capex and working capital, so it flatters; Part 13 turns to free cash flow, the cash left after those.

Measuring stick 4

Interest cover: can profit pay the bill

Interest cover is EBIT ÷ finance costs: how many times operating profit pays the interest. Ramu’s EBIT of ₹73 cr covers ₹10 cr of interest 7.3 times example. EBIT is used, not EBITDA, because depreciation is a real cost of keeping plants and shops running. Cover looks at the profit flow; net debt to EBITDA looks at the pile. You want both.

interest cover = EBIT ÷ finance costs = 73 ÷ 10 = 7.3×
Go deeper: what sits inside “finance costs”

Finance costs as printed can include more than loan interest. TCPL’s ₹137.03 cr includes ₹40.96 cr of interest on lease liabilities and ₹12.66 cr of interest on employee benefit plans (Note 26). Companies also compute their own “interest service coverage”, often net of interest income. This series divides EBIT by finance costs as printed, so the figures agree across parts.

When it is due

Short-term debt and refinancing risk

The same ₹100 cr of debt is safer or riskier depending on when it falls due. Ramu’s is ₹70 cr long-term and ₹30 cr short-term example. The ₹30 cr must be repaid or rolled over within a year; Ramu’s ₹40 cr of cash covers it 1.3 times. Refinancing risk is the chance that, when a loan falls due, lenders will not roll it over, or only at a much higher rate. Rolled over at 12% instead of 10%, the ₹30 cr costs ₹0.6 cr more a year example.

Short-term borrowing is often cheaper and flexible, which is why firms use it for working capital (Part 12 covers it). Funding a long-lived plant with short-term loans is the dangerous mismatch, and Part 30 shows what it did to NBFCs.

What the rate cycle does

Fixed versus floating, and an RBI hike

A fixed-rate loan keeps its rate until it is repaid. A floating-rate loan is priced off a benchmark that tends to follow the RBI’s policy repo rate, and resets on set dates, so a hike works through to the borrower’s interest bill after a lag. Suppose rates rise from 10% to 12%. Ramu’s finance costs rise from ₹10 cr to ₹12 cr if all its borrowings float, ₹11 cr if half do, and not at all if all are fixed (until the next refinancing) example.

Fixed rates feel safe, but the borrower cannot enjoy a cut either. Companies can also swap floating debt for fixed with a bank (a hedge). How fast and how fully a hike passes through depends on each loan’s terms. The lab below lets you set how much of Ramu’s debt floats.

Debt in disguise

Leases are debt-like (Ind AS 116)

Under Ind AS 116, a company that rents a building or truck for years records a right-of-use asset and a lease liability: the rent payments still to come, discounted to today. They are fixed promises, just like loan payments. A one-off example, not in Ramu’s accounts example: rent of ₹5 cr a year for ten years, discounted at 10%, is a liability of ₹30.7 cr. Added to borrowings it lifts D/E from 0.40 to 0.52. The cost moves too: EBITDA no longer deducts the rent; depreciation of the asset and interest on the liability do.

This series leaves lease liabilities out of debt unless stated, so ratios stay comparable from part to part. Some companies, Tata Steel among them, count them in their own net debt. Always check which one a ratio uses before comparing companies.

TCPL: effectively debt-free

Tata Consumer Products, consolidated, at 31 March 2026 public. Borrowings are ₹2,120.31 cr (₹302.14 cr non-current plus ₹1,818.17 cr current); cash and cash equivalents ₹3,046.57 cr. On the series definition, net debt is −₹926.26 cr: net cash of ₹926 cr. TCPL’s own figure, net cash ₹2,978 cr (₹2,977.49 cr), also counts bank deposits, current investments of ₹868.20 cr and inter-corporate loans of ₹833.09 cr, which Part 4 reconciled. Both are right; they answer different questions.

net debt = 2,120.31 − 3,046.57 = −926.26 · D/E = 2,120.31 ÷ 23,188.86 = 0.09 · net debt ÷ EBITDA = −926.26 ÷ 2,791.80 = −0.33×

With almost no debt, the equity multiplier (Part 10) lifts TCPL’s 7.08% ROE very little: leverage is not where its return comes from. Lease liabilities of ₹608.64 cr plus ₹90.56 cr, ₹699.20 cr in all, are not in these numbers. Adding them leaves net cash of ₹227 cr.

Go deeper: why 86% of TCPL’s borrowings are “current”

₹1,633.78 cr of the borrowings is a bank overdraft inside a group cash-pooling arrangement, with offsetting balances sitting in cash and cash equivalents (Note 15). Pooling inflates both sides, borrowings and cash, by about the same amount and leaves net debt alone. The cash flow statement nets it: cash of ₹1,412.79 cr plus the overdraft of ₹1,633.78 cr is the balance sheet’s ₹3,046.57 cr (p. 418). Read the notes before judging a big current-borrowings number.

TCPL: interest cover of 15.8×

EBIT is ₹2,165.12 cr: revenue ₹20,290.43 cr less ₹11,855.64 cr materials, ₹1,661.00 cr employee costs and ₹3,981.99 cr other expenses is EBITDA of ₹2,791.80 cr, less ₹626.68 cr D&A public. Finance costs are ₹137.03 cr. Interest cover is 2,165.12 ÷ 137.03 = 15.8×. A year earlier finance costs were ₹290.20 cr and cover, on the same construction, about 6.5× (my arithmetic).

FY26: 2,165.12 ÷ 137.03 = 15.80× · FY25: 1,878.61 ÷ 290.20 = 6.47×

Why finance costs fell. TCPL borrowed commercial paper (short-term company IOUs sold to investors) to fund acquisitions. Tata Steel: the opposite balance sheet

Tata Steel, consolidated, at 31 March 2026 public. Borrowings are ₹84,956.39 cr (₹63,753.67 cr non-current plus ₹21,202.72 cr current); cash and cash equivalents ₹8,884.95 cr. Net debt on the series definition is ₹76,071 cr. Tata Steel’s own net debt is ₹80,144 cr: it adds lease liabilities of ₹7,425.29 cr (so its gross debt is ₹92,382 cr) and deducts bank balances, current investments and currency hedges. Its reported EBITDA is ₹34,848 cr and its net debt to EBITDA is 2.3×, down from 3.2× a year earlier.

EBITDA = 232,139.94 − (74,468.93 + 18,159.14 + 989.62 + 25,998.98 + 79,139.21 − 968.38) = 34,352.44 EBIT = 34,352.44 − 11,954.51 = 22,397.93 · cover = 22,397.93 ÷ 7,167.06 = 3.13× · net debt ÷ EBITDA = 76,071.44 ÷ 34,352.44 = 2.21×

Why the numbers differ from Tata Steel’s own. The series EBITDA of ₹34,352 cr leaves out other income and the share of joint ventures; Tata Steel’s ₹34,848 cr (p. 29) uses its own definition (p. F33). Its reported interest service coverage of 4.72× divides EBIT by net finance charges, after interest income and dividends, so it is higher than our 3.13×. Neither is wrong. Pick one definition and use it for both companies. Rate sensitivity differs hugely too. By each company’s own disclosure, a 100 basis point rise in rates would add ₹389.14 cr to Tata Steel’s finance costs; TCPL’s 25 basis point figure of ₹1.03 cr scales to about ₹4 cr.

Same group, opposite balance sheets

Why a steelmaker carries debt

Both companies are Tata group companies. TCPL sells tea, salt and coffee, everyday products, and holds more cash than debt. A steelmaker is different in two ways. First, plants are huge: Tata Steel’s property, plant and equipment is ₹149,878 cr, with ₹27,510 cr more under construction, against total assets of ₹301,254 cr (p. F142). Debt is how such plants get built. Second, cash flows are cyclical: its EBITDA margin was 15.01% in FY26 against 11.81% in FY25, and profit before exceptional items rose from ₹9,268 cr to ₹17,001 cr (pp. F33, F144). Debt with a fixed interest bill sits on top of profit that swings. Part 33 covers cyclicals.

This is not a verdict. A low-debt balance sheet is not automatically better (it may mean slow growth or idle cash), and a high-debt one is not automatically worse. The questions are the ones in the next panel: how much, how soon, how steady is the cash that pays for it, and what is counted.

So what

Four questions for any company’s debt

  1. How big is the pile? Net debt to EBITDA and D/E, with cash netted off, and a note of what is counted (leases, overdrafts, deposits).
  2. Can profit pay the bill in a bad year? Interest cover, and how fast it would fall if EBITDA dropped 30%.
  3. When is it due, and at what kind of rate? The share due within a year, the cash against it, and how much floats.
  4. Why does this business need debt? Heavy plants and swingy cash flows call for more caution than steady, asset-light ones.

Ramu’s short-term borrowing of ₹30 cr funds part of its stock and customer dues. Part 12, “Working capital and the cash cycle”, asks how long a rupee stays stuck in them.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Debt is cheap and lifts the owners’ return, but the interest is due in the bad years too: measure the pile (net debt to EBITDA), the bill (interest cover), the timing and the rate type, and know what each company counts as debt.
