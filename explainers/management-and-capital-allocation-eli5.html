Management, capital allocation and concalls

A business earns cash; its managers decide what to do with it. Five choices, one test for each, and a record you can check: did they do what they said they would?

The question

What management really controls

Managers do not set the price of tea or the monsoon. They do decide how hard to chase sales, how much to spend on advertising and people, how much to borrow, and above all what to do with the cash the business produces. Here is where Ramu’s FY26 income went example, regrouped by who has more say. The split is my judgement, and every line is a mix: sourcing skill moves the materials bill, and tax is partly structure.

The ₹50 cr of profit is the one slice that is not spent. Part of it is paid out (Ramu proposes ₹20 cr, Part 14); the rest stays, along with depreciation that is not a cash cost (Part 13). Whatever stays is cash that someone has to put to work. That choice is called capital allocation, and over a decade it can matter as much as running the business well.

Five uses

Five things to do with spare cash

Suppose Ramu has ₹50 cr to decide on example. This is a teaching sum, not a line in Ramu’s FY26 accounts, which end with ₹40 cr of cash and ₹13 cr of free cash flow. A company has exactly five places to put it: reinvest in its own business, acquire another, pay a dividend, buy back its own shares, or repay debt. Each is the right answer sometimes. Each has one test.

The tests share a shape. Money that leaves the company has a price: what its owners could earn elsewhere, often called the cost of capital (Part 10 measured returns; Part 23 covers how to set the cost). A use passes if it earns more than that price, and fails if it earns less. The rest of this page applies that one idea five times.

The test

Return on the next rupee, not the average

Part 10’s ROCE is an average over all the capital in the business: Ramu’s is 20.9% example. The test for new cash is the return on incremental capital: the extra profit the new money adds, divided by the new money. The two can differ a lot, because the best opportunities get used first.

A rough check on Ramu’s own record: capex was ₹30, 38, 42 and 45 cr in FY23–FY26, ₹155 cr in all, and profit rose from ₹30 cr to ₹50 cr, a ₹20 cr gain: 20 ÷ 155 = 12.9% example. It ignores depreciation, working capital and what the base business did on its own, so treat it as a pointer, not a measurement. It says the next rupees earned less than the 20.9% average, though still more than the example 10% hurdle. A manager who quotes the average to justify a new project is answering a different question.

Use two

Acquisitions often overpay

An acquisition is reinvestment where you also pay the seller’s hopes. Ramu pays ₹50 cr for a rival earning ₹4 cr a year after tax: 8% on the price paid example. If owners could earn 10% elsewhere, a ₹4 cr profit stream is worth ₹4 ÷ 10% = ₹40 cr to them. Paying ₹50 cr is paying ₹10 cr, 25%, more than it is worth.

Why do buyers overpay so often? Rival bidders push the price up, the seller knows the business better, and the synergies that justify the premium (savings, cross-selling) are promises by the buyer’s own managers, who gain from being bigger. Studies of takeovers often find the buyers’ shareholders gain little, but I have not cited one here and the claim is general: the arithmetic above is what to test each deal with. Two checks make the test concrete: what return on the price paid does management say it expects, and what does the report show a few years later (the TCPL panel below shows why that second check is hard).

Use four

Buybacks help only below value

A buyback spends cash to retire shares, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice. Ramu buys back 0.5 cr of its 10 cr shares at ₹100: ₹50 cr, 5% of the company example. Before it acts, a share is worth ₹100 for the business (20 × EPS ₹5, Part 7) plus ₹5 for its slice of the spare cash: ₹105. If the cash buys shares at less than ₹105, the holders who stay gain; above ₹105 they lose. Sellers get the opposite.

Nobody is made richer by the buyback itself: it moves value between those who sell and those who stay, and the price decides who gets it. Spread evenly, with every holder selling the same fraction, it equals a dividend (the lab proves it below). It also lifts EPS, because the profit is divided by fewer shares, which makes it a tempting way to flatter a number. Part 21 covers how a buyback is actually run and taxed.

Use three

Dividends suit firms that can’t reinvest well

Paying out is the default when nothing inside the business beats what owners can do themselves. Ramu’s ₹5 a share, invested by its owners at 10%, becomes ₹5 × 1.110 = ₹12.97 in ten years example. Reinvesting the same ₹50 cr in the business wins only if it earns more than 10%. At exactly 10% the two tie.

That is Part 14’s payout and retention seen from the other side: a high payout is no virtue, and a low one is no sin. Ramu keeps 60% of profit because it expects to earn more than 10% on it. A firm that keeps most of its profit and earns 5% on it is quietly costing its owners money.

Use five

Repaying debt is a return too

Repaying debt earns the interest it saves, nearly risk-free. Ramu owes ₹100 cr at about 10% example. Repaying ₹50 cr removes ₹5 cr of interest a year; after tax at about 25% (Ramu’s tax is ₹17 cr on ₹67 cr of profit before tax) that is ₹3.75 cr, 7.5% on the ₹50 cr, and interest cover (Part 11) rises from 7.3× to 14.6× (EBIT 73 ÷ 5).

Against an example 10% hurdle, a certain 7.5% is a poor return, which is why a company with cheap or small debt rarely rushes to repay it; but it becomes the best use when the rate is high or the debt is a danger. Go deeper: where the formulas come from

With hurdle s and N years, an annuity of 1 a year invested at s grows to AF = ((1+s)N − 1) ÷ s. Dividend: ₹100 + ₹5 × (1+s)N. Reinvest or acquire at return r: ₹100 + ₹5 (the project, valued at cost) + ₹5 × r × AF. At r = s the three are equal, which is the whole point: the return only has to beat the hurdle. Buyback: the ₹1,000 cr business is shared by 10 − 50 ÷ price crore shares. The model keeps projects at cost, so a project earning less than the hurdle is flattered slightly; and a real project’s return fades with scale, which the “fade” situation shows.

The people

Track record: what the past cash shows

You judge managers by what they did with other people’s money. Four questions: what did earlier cash go into, and what did it earn? How long have the leaders been there, and do the same names keep making the promises? How have they treated outside shareholders when the promoters’ interests differed (Part 19 covers ownership, pledges and related-party deals)? And did they tell you the truth in a bad year? The first can be read straight from the accounts. Here is Ramu over five years example.

Over FY22–FY26 Ramu’s operations produced ₹231 cr of cash and it spent ₹175 cr on capex: 76% of its cash went back in, leaving ₹56 cr of free cash flow. In return, sales grew from ₹330 cr to ₹500 cr and ROE stayed near 20% (20.2% on FY25’s ₹218 cr of equity, 20.0% on FY26’s ₹250 cr). Heavy reinvestment with steady returns is the pattern of a manager who is putting cash to good use; the same reinvestment with falling returns would be the warning. Part 20 lists the other patterns that come before a blow-up.

Promises

Guidance versus delivery

Guidance is what management says it expects: growth, margins, a launch date. Anyone can say a number; the useful thing is whether it was met. So keep a promise tracker: write down each target with its date, and when the year closes, write down what happened. Ramu guided 10% sales growth for each of FY24, FY25 and FY26, and delivered 405 ÷ 365 = 11.0%, 450 ÷ 405 = 11.1% and 500 ÷ 450 = 11.1% example. Modest promise, kept every year, a little beaten.

Now compare it with a company that promises more and delivers the same. The toggle below swaps Ramu’s guidance for a one-off over-promiser, example not Ramu’s: it guided 20%, 18% and 15%, and delivered Ramu’s 11.0%, 11.1% and 11.1%. The tolerance stepper sets how close counts as “met”.

Read the tracker the way a lender reads a record: one miss is noise, a pattern is information. Hits and misses are not the only signal. A target that is always met by a hair may have been set to be easy (sandbagging); one missed every year while the explanation changes is the pattern to fear.

The concall

How to read an earnings call

Listed companies hold an earnings call (a “concall”) after each quarterly result, and publish a transcript and an investor presentation (slides) on their website. Both are management speaking, not audited statements. A call has two parts: prepared remarks, which are the frame management chose, and analysts’ questions, where the frame is tested. On TCPL’s Q4 FY26 call (8 May 2026), by my word count of the transcript, only about a quarter of the words were prepared remarks.

So read the questions first. What is listened for: volume against price (growth from selling more is sturdier than growth from raising prices), margins and what is said about their direction, capex plans (where cash will go next, and the returns expected), working capital (Part 12), and the questions management dodges. TCPL’s Q3 FY26 call (27 January 2026) shows the first one in one breath: India Foods revenue grew 19% on 16% volume growth, and packaged tea’s 3% revenue growth was 3% volume.

A dodge looks like this. On the Q4 call an analyst asked what margin to expect for FY27 given higher costs. The answer spoke of packaging and fuel costs and then said: “I wouldn’t lose sleep on trying to figure out margins at least in the next 2, 3 months. Longer term, your guess is as good as mine.” It added confidence in double-digit revenue growth and EBITDA growing ahead of it, but gave no margin level. A good analyst presses, and this one did: asked whether the 50–70 basis points of expansion indicated earlier still held, the CEO said “50 to 75, 80 bps is a given … We will deliver it” (and “50 to 75 for the full year will happen”). So the first answer was a sidestep, the second a dated promise to put in the tracker; the follow-up is where the information was. Source: Q4 FY26 transcript, pp. 6–7. I searched the Q1, Q3 and Q4 FY26 transcripts for “capex” and “capital expenditure” and found no mention, so the capex plan has to come from the annual report instead.

Claims and facts

Management’s number versus the audited one

Everything said on a call or shown on a slide is a claim. Audited statements are facts, checked by an independent auditor, though still shaped by accounting choices (Part 6). Two TCPL examples public. EBITDA is not printed in the audited statements at all. TCPL’s presentation gives ₹2,815 cr; building it from the statement’s lines (revenue less materials, employee and other expenses) gives ₹2,791.80 cr, 0.8% lower. Close, so the claim holds up. Adjusted EPS was ₹17.30 against the audited basic EPS of ₹15.59: an 11% gap, because the adjusted figure adds back the amortisation of the Capital Foods and Organic India brands (Part 6).

Neither is false. The rule is simple: when a number is in the audited statement, use it; when it is management’s own (adjusted, “underlying”, “like-for-like”), ask what was left out and who chose to leave it out. TCPL’s “21 days” of working capital is a third example, covered in Part 12: a definition the statements do not print.

Warning signs

Warning signs in management talk

Patterns worth noting in what managers say, none a verdict alone: a target that is always “next year” (or always two years away, below); a metric that changes just when the old one turns bad (revenue, then “order book”, then “engagement”); adjusted figures that grow while reported ones do not; blaming outside forces for every miss and crediting itself for every hit; capital raised again and again with the same story; and silence on questions that matter. Part 19 covers governance and ownership, and Part 20 covers red flags in the accounts themselves, such as receivables outrunning sales.

In this one-off example example a firm promises ₹1,000 cr of sales by FY26, then by FY27, then by FY28: the finish line recedes at exactly the speed the firm runs. Put each promise in the tracker with its date, and the pattern becomes visible in years, not quarters.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL: where the cash went, FY24–FY26

Tata Consumer Products, consolidated public. Two acquisitions anchor the period. Form AOC-1 lists Capital Foods Private Ltd, acquired on 1 February 2024 (75% held), and Organic India Private Ltd, acquired on 16 April 2024 (100% held, with its US arm). The audited FY25 cash flow shows “Acquisition of Subsidiaries” of ₹1,809.00 cr, nothing in FY26. How was it paid for? TCPL raised ₹2,995.83 cr of equity by a rights issue allotted in FY25, one new share for every 26 held at ₹818 a share, ₹2,980.57 cr after expenses, and the same cash flow shows ₹1,164.49 cr of commercial paper “for acquisition funding” repaid.

The acquisitions plus the commercial paper repaid (₹2,973.49 cr) are within ₹7 cr of the rights money, which suggests the story but is not proven by the statements, so I do not claim the two tie. It shows the cost: owners were asked for fresh capital, so the deals have to earn more than the cost of that equity (Part 21 covers rights issues). TCPL also invested ₹125.00 cr in a joint venture in FY25.

FY25 cash flow · acquisitions 1,809.00 + commercial paper repaid 1,164.49 = 2,973.49 · rights issue, net 2,980.57 (gross 2,995.83 − expenses 15.26)

Dividends kept rising through the period. Cash paid grew from ₹738.45 cr in FY25 (₹7.75 a share) to ₹816.34 cr in FY26 (₹8.25), and the board recommended ₹10 for FY26, ₹989.56 cr, payable in FY27 (Part 14).

What did the purchases leave on the balance sheet? Goodwill of ₹11,883.90 cr and other intangibles of ₹7,111.76 cr: ₹18,995.66 cr, 55% of total assets of ₹34,452.96 cr. That is built up over many deals, not only the two above, and the balance sheet does not say how much came from each. Part 10 showed the effect on ROCE: 8.6% on all capital employed, 16.1% without goodwill, 34.3% without goodwill and intangibles. Acquired capital drags the ratio down; whether the deals earn their cost over time is the open question.

Why not just compute the return on the ₹1,809 cr? Form AOC-1 gives each subsidiary’s own profit, but not the return on the price paid: the group’s accounting for the purchase (the brands and goodwill above, amortised at the group level) sits outside it, the Capital Foods deal sits in FY24 and not FY25, and “adjusted EPS” adds the amortisation back. The auditor flags the risk: Deloitte Haskins & Sells LLP’s key audit matter is the impairment of goodwill of the UK and Europe cash-generating unit, carried at ₹2,184.50 cr, where “competitive pressures and decline in black tea demand” raised the risk of impairment. A goodwill impairment of ₹16.02 cr was recognised in FY26.

Now find it in a real report

One dated promise, checked against the numbers

On TCPL’s Q1 FY26 earnings call on 23 July 2025, an analyst (Tejash Shah) asked: “So 16% EBITDA margin that we are potentially indicating by Q2, Q3 this year, where do we stand on that guidance now?” The managing director and CEO, Sunil D’Souza, answered: “So I do think by Q3, we should definitely be able to get to it.” The figure 16% is the analyst’s wording, and the CEO’s “it” accepts it; I could not verify from the transcripts who first said 16%. Source: tataconsumer.com, Q1 FY26 transcript, p. 12 of 16 public.

Against the record: TCPL’s Q3 FY26 press release (27 January 2026) gives EBITDA of ₹728 cr on revenue of ₹5,112 cr: 14.2%. On the Q4 call the CEO put Q4 at 14.6%, and the full year at 13.9%. By TCPL’s own figures the 16% was not reached in Q3, in Q4, or over the year. In fairness: on the Q4 call management pointed to high tea costs in the first half and elevated US coffee costs through the year, the CEO had said Q2 “would be a bridge”, and the Q3 release says the benefit of lower tea input costs was passed on to consumers, a pricing choice rather than a failure; EBITDA still grew 26% in Q3. But a promise dated “by Q3” that was not met belongs in the tracker as a miss, with the reasons beside it.

Q3 FY26 EBITDA margin = 728 ÷ 5,112 = 14.2% · 16% would have been 0.16 × 5,112 = 818, about ₹90 cr more EBITDA

A second, softer claim, from the Q3 call on 27 January 2026: “we have always said we will drive double digit topline and bottomline ahead of the topline. And I think we have roughly delivered that.” Check it against FY26. Revenue grew 15.2% (₹20,290.43 cr against ₹17,618.30 cr), so double digit is right. Profit before tax grew 22.3% and profit for the year 20.2%, ahead of revenue, so the “bottomline” half holds. EBITDA grew 12% on the call’s figure, behind revenue, so “EBITDA ahead of topline” does not.

What lifted the bottom line past EBITDA? Finance costs fell from ₹290.20 cr to ₹137.03 cr, ₹153 cr less, the interest saved after the rights issue repaid debt: 39% of the ₹396 cr rise in profit before tax. A reader who ignores capital allocation would credit operations for what a financing decision did. EPS rose less than profit, 19.4% (₹15.59 against ₹13.06), because the rights issue added shares. These are descriptions of what was said and what was reported, not a view on the shares.

So what

Three questions for any management

  1. What did the last ₹50 cr earn, against what it cost? Not the average ROCE: the return on the next rupee, after the price paid, against what owners could earn elsewhere.
  2. Is the price below value? For buybacks and acquisitions alike, the price paid is the whole game. A buyback above value moves wealth to the sellers.
  3. Did they do what they said? Keep the tracker: the target, the date, the result. Trust the pattern, not the latest promise.

Ramu’s managers look good on these tests: a 20% reinvestment and a modest promise kept three years running. But managers answer to owners, and owners are not all alike. Part 19, “Governance and who owns the company”, asks who owns the shares, what a pledge does when the price falls, whose side the board is on, and how much the auditor can be trusted.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Management has one decision only it can make: what to do with spare cash. Pass every choice through one test, return against what owners could earn elsewhere, and check the words against the record.
