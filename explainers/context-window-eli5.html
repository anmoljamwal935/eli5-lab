Memory and the context window

Part 13 ended with a well-mannered assistant that still sees only so much at once. Part 7 left a bill unpaid: every word compares with every earlier word, so reading more costs far more than more. This part fits the two together: how much text the model holds in view, what falls out as the chat grows, and why the middle of a long chat is the worst place to hide a fact.

The problem

Long chats forget their beginnings

An hour into a chat, ask about something from the first minutes and the model blanks — or worse, answers smoothly about something you never said. It is not distracted. The beginning of your chat is simply gone: not faded, not fuzzy, but no longer in the text it reads.

This is not forgetting the way people forget. Nothing decayed. The model reads a fixed amount of recent text before every reply, and your first minutes fell outside it. The rest of this part measures that frame.

The idea

It reads through a fixed frame

Before every reply the model re-reads the chat — Part 2’s document — but only up to a fixed budget of tokens. That budget is the context window: the maximum text it holds in view at one time. Everything inside the frame is read; everything outside might as well not exist.

The window is counted in tokens, Part 1’s word pieces, and it is shared by everything: your history, any documents you attach, and the reply being written. A 128,000-token window holding a whole novel still has to fit its own answer inside too.

The idea

One window, four tenants

Peek inside a full window and four tenants share it. First the system prompt — the standing instructions behind Part 2’s [system] marker. Then the conversation so far, newest last. Then any fetched pages (chapter 8 below). And last, empty space held for the reply being written. Shares vary by app; this split of one 128K window is sketched example.

The order matters: anything you attach or recall squeezes the room left for history. A window stuffed with documents is a window with a short memory of you — a trade the next chapters keep making.

The mechanism

Why the frame cannot just grow

Part 7 showed the price: each word scores every word before it, so doubling the text roughly quadruples the comparisons. At Llama 3’s full window of about 128,000 tokens that is some 8.2 billion comparisons for a single head in a single layer public. Ten times the view costs about a hundred times the arithmetic.

And the saved keys from Part 7 pile up too: one key and value per token, kept for the whole reply. A longer window is a bigger pile in memory. So every extra page in view is paid twice, in sums and in storage.

Go deeper: the memory bill

The KV cache from Part 7 grows with every token in view. For Llama 3.1’s 8-billion-number model that is about 128 kilobytes per token, or 16 gigabytes at its full 131,072-token window public. Part 8 is about why that memory is the scarcest thing on the chip.

The mechanism

New words push old words out

When the chat outgrows the window, something has to give, and it is always the oldest text. Each new token slides the frame forward by one: the newest words enter, the oldest fall out. The model never notices the loss — it only ever sees what is inside.

That is the whole mechanism of machine forgetting. No decay, no interference, no “tip of the tongue”: just a frame that fits the ending and drops the beginning, one token at a time.

The practice

Each step, ten times the view

Engineers have spent years buying a bigger view. GPT-4 launched at 8,192 tokens in March 2023; its Turbo follow-up jumped to 128,000 that November public. Gemini 1.5 crossed a million in February 2024 and two million by June public. Llama 3.1 made 128K standard for open weights public.

Each step is roughly ten times the last, and each costs roughly a hundred times the comparisons — paid for with the faster chips and leaner attention of Parts 8 through 12. A 128,000-token window holds about a 300-page novel example, and still answers one token at a time.

Go deeper: stretching without retraining

Researchers also stretch finished models without starting over: position-rescaling tricks (the YaRN family, 2023) remap the position numbers so old training covers longer text public. Mistral went the other way, pairing long inputs with sliding-window attention that only ever compares nearby words public. Same frame problem, opposite answers.

The practice

What does not fit gets fetched

Two workarounds carry what the frame cannot. Retrieval keeps the library outside and fetches only the few chunks each question needs into view. Summarization compresses the old chat into a short running note — “so far we decided…” — that fits where the full history would not.

Both are admissions that the window is finite. Retrieval bets the right pages can be found fast; summarization bets the details can be safely forgotten. When either bet loses, the model answers from a confident partial picture — a preview of Part 15.

The gotcha

Lost in the middle

Being in view is not the same as being used. Liu and colleagues hid one answer-bearing document among many and moved it through every position: accuracy sagged into a U, high at the start and the end, worst in the middle — dropping by tens of points public (Liu et al., 2023).

The test has a folk name: needle in a haystack. Newer long-window models have flattened the curve a lot — but flattening a U is not erasing it, and the middle of a long context remains the worst place to hide the one fact that matters.

Go deeper: why the middle loses

Researchers have found pieces of the answer. The first tokens anchor attention out of all proportion to their content — evicting them wrecks the model, so serving systems pin a few down permanently (attention sinks, Xiao et al., 2023) public (StreamingLLM). Recent tokens dominate because every word looks back at what just happened (Part 7). The middle gets neither advantage — and training rarely rewards digging there.

The gotcha

The window is not the memory

The advertised number is a capacity, not a promise. RULER, an independent benchmark, grades ten long-context models on tasks harder than finding a needle — and finds only four hold up at 32K, with almost all models falling below the bar before reaching their claimed lengths public (Hsieh et al., 2024).

And the whole visible chat is re-read — and billed — on every single reply. A long afternoon in view means paying for the afternoon with each answer. The frame is priced by what fills it, not by its size. Providers soften the bill by caching the prefix: Anthropic (August 2024) and OpenAI (October 2024) discount re-reading unchanged opening text by up to 90% and 50% respectively public.

The surprise

Between chats, nothing persists

Close the chat and the frame is discarded — words, keys, values, all of it. Every session starts empty: the model brings its trained numbers from Parts 1 to 13 and nothing else. When an app “remembers you”, it saved notes to disk and fetches them back into view (chapter 8 again) — memory as retrieval, not recall.

There is no contradiction with the lab: the frame is roomy, but it is rented by the chat. Fluency about “last time” is either notes reread — or Part 15.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
The model reads the ending through a fixed frame and the beginning falls out; and even inside the frame, the middle is where facts go missing. Memory here is a window, not a mind.
