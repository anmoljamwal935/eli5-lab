Cash flow, and how the three connect

A company can report a healthy profit and still hold less cash than a year ago. The cash flow statement shows where the cash really went, and it is the thread that ties the profit and loss account to the balance sheet.

Built from Ind AS 7 and Tata Consumer Products’ Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, facts checked against the audited statements · Read the source ↗

Part 3 built the P&L and Part 4 the balance sheet, using Ramu Chai Ltd, a fictional tea company. This part adds the third statement and connects all three. All amounts are ₹ crore. The puzzle

Profit ₹50 cr, cash down ₹1 cr

Ramu Chai earned a profit after tax of ₹50 cr in FY26 example. It opened the year with ₹41 cr in the bank and closed with ₹40 cr. Its operations alone brought in ₹58 cr of cash, more than the profit. So where did the money go? The cash flow statement answers exactly that.

The statement

Three buckets, one bridge

The cash flow statement sorts every rupee that moved into three buckets. Operations: the business itself. Investing: buying and selling things that last. Financing: money to and from lenders and owners. Add the three to the opening cash and you land on the closing cash.

Operations

Start at profit, undo the accounting

Ind AS 7, the standard for cash flow statements, lets a company show operations the direct way (list cash received and paid) or the indirect way: start from profit before tax and adjust. Ramu, like TCPL, uses the indirect way public. Add back depreciation, which cost no cash. Move finance costs (a financing item) and interest income (an investing item) out. Then adjust for working capital and subtract tax actually paid.

Go deeper: why the 85 equals EBITDA, and when it won't

Ramu's only items outside operations are interest, so the subtotal before working capital (₹85 cr) equals EBITDA by construction example. Real companies add exceptional items, share-based pay and gains on sale, so the two come close but differ. TCPL's operating profit before working-capital changes is ₹2,839.97 cr against EBITDA of ₹2,815 cr public. Ramu's tax paid equals its tax expense (₹17 cr); in practice they differ by deferred tax and timing.

Working capital

Bigger balance sheet, less cash

Profit counts a sale when it is made; cash arrives when the customer pays. In FY26 Ramu held ₹8 cr more stock and was owed ₹7 cr more by customers: cash spent, not yet earned back. It owed suppliers ₹5 cr more, cash kept for now. The rule: more of something owned uses cash; more of something owed provides it. Part 12 turns these into days and asks how long a rupee stays stuck.

Investing and financing

Machines, loans and dividends

Ramu spent ₹45 cr on plant and work in progress and received ₹4 cr interest on its cash. It borrowed ₹10 cr net, paid ₹10 cr interest and ₹18 cr of dividend. Ind AS 7 puts interest paid and dividends paid in financing, and interest and dividends received in investing, for companies that are not financial institutions public. Banks and NBFCs differ (Part 29 covers banks).

Go deeper: finding capex from two balance sheets

Closing fixed assets = opening + bought − depreciation. So bought = 220 − 190 + 12 = ₹42 cr. Work in progress rose from 22 to 25, another ₹3 cr of spending. 42 + 3 = the ₹45 cr capex in the cash flow example. The same trick lets you sanity-check any company's capex line.

Why profit is not cash

Five reasons profit is not cash

Start at ₹50 cr profit and walk to the ₹1 cr fall in cash. Depreciation (+12) was spread cost, not payment now. Working capital (−10) tied cash in stock and unpaid bills. Capex (−45) is not an expense when paid, only when depreciated. New borrowing (+10) is cash that is not profit. Dividends (−18) are profit handed to owners, not a cost. Interest nets to zero: it is in profit and in cash. Part 13 builds the cash-quality test; Part 6 shows how accounting choices move profit while cash stays put.

How the three connect

Every balance-sheet change has a reason

Put the 31 March 2026 balance sheet next to 2025 example. Every line moved, and each move is explained by the other two statements. Profit goes into reserves, dividends come out. Capex adds fixed assets, depreciation removes some. Working capital moves current lines. Loans move borrowings. The net change in cash is the cash line itself. Owned grew by 47; funding grew by 47.

Go deeper: the reserves roll

Reserves 118 + profit 50 − dividend paid 18 = 150. Equity is share capital 100 + reserves 150 = 250 example. Check it against the 32 in the drawing: 50 − 18.

The Indian quirk

FY26’s dividend is paid in FY27

The board proposes a final dividend when it approves the annual accounts; shareholders vote at the AGM; payment follows weeks later. Ind AS 10 says a dividend declared after the year-end is not a liability at that date public. So the FY26 statements show the dividend paid in FY26: FY25’s ₹18 cr (₹1.80 a share). FY26’s ₹20 cr (₹2.00) lands in FY27’s cash flow example. A dividend declared and paid within the year (an interim dividend) shows in that year.

Now find it in a real report

Compare operations with profit. Group net profit is ₹1,546.80 cr and cash from operations ₹2,421.85 cr, about 1.57 times as much public. The largest single reason is ₹626.68 cr of depreciation and amortisation, which cost no cash this year; part of it is amortisation of acquired brands (Part 6). Whether a gap like this is good news is Part 13’s question.

The AGM presentation quotes “free cash flow to EBITDA” of 107%. The annual report’s footnote defines that free cash flow as before capex and tax (consolidated 107%, standalone 103%) public. TCPL does not publish the bridge. The audited “cash generated from operations” is ₹2,985.16 cr, which is 106% of EBITDA of ₹2,815 cr: close to the claim, on its own definition. It leaves out ₹448.57 cr of capex and ₹563.31 cr of tax. Cash from operations minus capex is ₹1,973.28 cr, or 70% of EBITDA. The two are different measures; Part 13 teaches free cash flow properly.

The dividend quirk shows up too. The Board recommended ₹10 a share for FY26 (a ₹989.56 cr cash outflow), payable on or after 15 June 2026, so it falls in FY27 public. FY26’s cash flow instead shows ₹820.30 cr paid: FY25’s ₹8.25 a share (₹816.34 cr to TCPL shareholders) plus ₹3.96 cr to minority holders in subsidiaries public (pp. 177, 416, 418).

Go deeper: why TCPL’s cash is not the balance sheet’s cash line

The cash flow statement’s closing cash (₹1,412.79 cr) is net of bank overdrafts of ₹1,633.78 cr. Add them back and you get the balance sheet’s “Cash and cash equivalents” of ₹3,046.57 cr. TCPL’s statement also has an exchange-gain line and, in FY25, cash acquired with new subsidiaries. So for a real company, “net change in cash equals the balance sheet cash line” holds after reading the reconciliation under the statement public. Audited revenue from operations is ₹20,290.43 cr (p. 415), matching the AGM presentation’s ₹20,290 cr.

So what

Three questions for any cash flow

1. Did operations make cash, or only profit? Look at cash from operations beside profit after tax, year after year. 2. Where did the cash go? Capex, acquisitions, dividends or debt repayment each tell a different story. 3. Who paid for it? A company can fund capex and dividends from operations or from new borrowing. Cash from operations beside profit, and capex beside depreciation, are the first two things to read; Part 13 turns them into free cash flow and an earnings-quality test.

The cash flow statement is also the thread between the other two. One more thing is worth holding on to: profit depends on accounting choices, and cash mostly does not. Next, Part 6, “Accounting choices that change profit”, shows which choices move profit and why a careful reader keeps the cash flow beside it.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Profit is what the business earned on paper; cash is what reached the bank, and the cash flow statement is the bridge that makes the P&L and the balance sheet agree.
