MONETARY ARCHITECTURE / 22 MINELI5

Utilities, PSUs and real estate

In three kinds of company the usual reading of the accounts misleads. A power utility earns a return the regulator sets, on equity the regulator approves. A government-owned company answers to an owner who also makes policy. A property developer is paid years before it may count the sale. Each needs its own key numbers.

The problem

Revenue fell; profit rose 18%

In FY26 NTPC, India’s largest power generator, sold less: revenue from operations fell 2.67% and its fuel bill fell 8.71%. Its profit after tax rose 17.88% public. For a tea company that would need a story about margins. For NTPC it is how the business is built: fuel is passed on to customers, and profit follows the equity the regulator lets it earn on.

Three businesses break three habits. In a regulated utility profit is set by a rule, not by sales. In a public sector undertaking (PSU) the biggest shareholder is the government, which wants dividends, sells shares and makes policy. In real estate the cash arrives years before the revenue. The lab gives each one a card of the numbers that matter.

The building block

A tariff built from costs plus a return

Chambal Power runs one coal plant whose approved cost is ₹10,000 cr example. Its customers, state electricity distributors, pay a two-part tariff. The capacity charge pays the plant’s annual fixed cost (AFC): a return on equity, interest, depreciation, operation and maintenance (O&M) and interest on working capital. The energy charge pays for the fuel burnt public for the rule.

AFC = return on equity 620 + interest on loans 630 + depreciation 500 + O&M 350 + interest on working capital 100 = ₹2,200 cr

Every cost is recovered, and one line is profit: the return on equity. So the questions become how much equity the regulator counts, what rate it allows on it, and what the plant must do to collect it. CERC (the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission) sets all three for central generators such as NTPC, in five-year regulations.

Go deeper: where the rule lives

CERC (Terms and Conditions of Tariff) Regulations, 2024, notified 15 March 2024 and in force from 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2029 (Regulation 1). Regulation 14 sets the two parts of a thermal tariff, capacity charge and energy charge; Regulation 15 lists the five components of the annual fixed cost; Regulation 16 bases the energy charge on the landed cost of fuel. Chambal’s cost split is made up; in a real tariff order each line follows its own norm.

How much equity counts

Regulated equity: 30% of the approved cost

CERC counts a new project as funded 70% by debt and 30% by equity. If the owner put in more than 30% equity, the extra is treated as a loan and earns only interest; if it put in less, the actual equity is used public. Chambal’s ₹10,000 cr plant gives a regulated equity of ₹3,000 cr example.

So regulated equity grows only when new capacity is commissioned or the regulator admits new spending. It is the utility’s real growth driver, and companies quote it: NTPC’s standalone regulated equity rose from ₹90,902 cr to ₹94,631 cr in FY26 public, 4.1%. A utility that announces big capex is announcing future regulated equity, once the plants are built and admitted.

Go deeper: the exact wording

Regulation 18(1): “For new projects, the debt-equity ratio of 70:30 as on date of commercial operation shall be considered. If the equity actually deployed is more than 30% of the capital cost, equity in excess of 30% shall be treated as normative loan”; where it is less than 30%, actual equity is used. For stations already running before 1 April 2024, the ratio allowed for the earlier period carries on (Regulation 18(3)). Grants do not count in the capital structure.

The rate

15.5% after tax, grossed up

For an existing thermal station CERC allows a base return on equity of 15.50%, and the rate is “grossed up” with the company’s effective tax rate, so that 15.5% is what is left after tax public. With a 25% tax rate Chambal bills 20.667% on ₹3,000 cr, ₹620 cr, pays ₹155 cr of tax and keeps ₹465 cr example.

pre-tax rate = base rate ÷ (1 − tax rate) = 15.5% ÷ 0.75 = 20.667% · profit after tax = 15.5% × regulated equity

That is the heart of a regulated utility: profit ≈ base rate × regulated equity, as long as the plant meets its norms. It also means its ROE (Part 10) cannot drift far above the allowed rate on the regulated business, and that tax savings mostly go back to customers through the true-up.

Go deeper: the rates and adjustments

Regulation 30(2), existing projects: 15.50% for thermal stations, transmission and run-of-river hydro; 16.50% for storage hydro, pumped storage and run-of-river with pondage. Regulation 30(3), new projects reaching commercial operation on or after 1 April 2024: 15.00% for transmission, 15.50% for thermal and run-of-river hydro, 17.00% for storage hydro and pumped storage. Extra spending beyond the original scope (for example emission controls) earns SBI’s one-year MCLR plus 3.5 points, capped at 14%. The rate is cut by 1 point while required grid-support systems are missing, cut 0.25 points for missing the ramp-rate standard, and raised by up to 1 point for faster ramping. Regulation 31: the rate is grossed up as base rate ÷ (1 − t), where t is the effective tax rate (the MAT rate, or the section 115BAA rate, where those apply), and trued up each year against tax actually paid. The First Amendment (11 April 2025) and Second Amendment (10 April 2026) leave these base rates unchanged; the second adds 14.00% for energy storage added to existing plants.

Why revenue misleads

Fuel is passed straight through

If coal costs 20% less, Chambal’s energy charge falls by the same ₹660 cr and revenue drops to ₹4,840 cr. If coal costs 20% more, revenue rises to ₹6,160 cr. Profit is ₹465 cr in both worlds example, because the fuel bill and the energy charge move together.

So for a regulated generator, revenue growth and margins say little: a fall in coal prices shrinks revenue and lifts the margin without changing profit. That is NTPC’s FY26 in miniature: its management notes say capacity and energy charges are over 95% of revenue, and that fuel cost fell mainly because it generated less and domestic coal was cheaper public.

What the plant controls

Availability: the number that earns the return

The full capacity charge is paid only if the plant is available (ready to run when called) at least 85% of the time, CERC’s normative availability for most thermal stations; 83% for older coal and lignite stations public. Below that, the charge is cut in proportion. At 78%, Chambal recovers only 78/85 of ₹2,200 cr example.

The ₹181 cr shortfall comes straight out of profit, because the costs are still there: profit falls from ₹465 cr to ₹329 cr, an 11.0% return instead of 15.5% example. That is why a utility’s operating data (availability, outages, fuel security) matter more than its revenue. CERC also pays small extras for good operation: up to 1 point more return for faster ramping, and an incentive of up to 1% of the fixed cost for frequency response.

Go deeper: how CERC computes it

Regulation 62 computes the capacity charge month by month on a cumulative basis: 20% of the annual fixed cost is earned in four daily peak hours and 80% in the other twenty, each as AFC × (availability achieved ÷ normative availability), capped at the full amount. A shortfall in off-peak hours can be offset by extra availability in peak hours, but not the other way round. Regulation 70(A) sets normative availability at 85% for thermal stations, 83% for coal and lignite stations completing 30 years from commercial operation, and lower figures for a few named stations. Regulation 62(5) adds an incentive of up to 1% of AFC for frequency response. The lab ignores the peak split and the incentives.

PSUs: who owns it

When the government is the biggest owner

A public sector undertaking is a company the government controls. NTPC is one: on 31 March 2026 the President of India held 51.10% of its shares, the same as a year earlier, and the company is under the Ministry of Power public. Its tariffs, though, are set by CERC, a statutory regulator.

That owner wants things that can pull apart: dividends for the budget, cash from selling shares (divestment), and a power sector that works for consumers. For an investor, each shows up in a number: the payout ratio, the free float and the share supply still to come, and the rules that can change at the next reset. The next three panels take them in turn.

PSU dividends

The dividend floor: 30% or 4%

The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) asks every central public sector enterprise (CPSE) to pay a minimum annual dividend of the higher of 30% of profit after tax or 4% of net worth public. The two rules cross at a 13.3% return on net worth: below it, the 4% rule is larger.

Chambal earns ₹465 cr on ₹4,000 cr of net worth, 11.6%. Thirty per cent of profit would be ₹139.5 cr, but 4% of net worth is ₹160 cr, so it must pay at least ₹160 cr, 34% of profit example. A low-return PSU is pushed to pay out more, which leaves less to fund growth. NTPC, at 13.2% on closing net worth, was just on the 4% side: a floor of ₹6,995 cr; it paid ₹8,727 cr, ₹9 a share public.

Go deeper: the guidelines

DIPAM’s Office Memorandum of 18 November 2024 issued revised Guidelines on Capital Restructuring of CPSEs, replacing those of 27 May 2016. DIPAM’s FAQ on the revision: the 2016 floor was the higher of 30% of PAT or 5% of net worth; the revised floor is the higher of 30% of PAT or 4% of net worth; financial-sector CPSEs such as NBFCs may pay 30% of PAT. NTPC’s dividend distribution policy calls the guideline “only a minimum benchmark” and lists CERC’s regulatory norms among the factors that may affect its margins and payout. Sources: DIPAM OM, DIPAM FAQ, NTPC policy. NTPC’s return here uses closing equity of ₹1,74,865 cr; its own 13.77% uses average net worth.

PSU divestment

Divestment moves shares, not company cash

Say the government sells 9 crore of Chambal’s 100 crore shares to investors at ₹48, through an offer for sale (OFS) on the exchange example. Those shares already exist. The ₹432 cr goes to the government; Chambal’s cash, its share count and its earnings per share do not change.

Contrast a new issue, which brings the company money and dilutes every holder (Part 21). What an OFS changes is ownership: a larger free float, often a small discount to the market price, and the knowledge that more sales may follow until the government nears the stake it wants to keep. NTPC’s government holding did not change in FY26 public.

Policy risk

The rules reset every five years

CERC writes its tariff rules for five years at a time: the 2019 regulations ran from 1 April 2019 to 31 March 2024, and the 2024 regulations run to 31 March 2029 public. The base return for thermal stations stayed at 15.5% across the last reset; new storage hydro projects now get 17.0% public. What comes after 2029 is not known.

For Chambal each point of base return is ₹30 cr of profit, 6.5% of it example. That is the policy risk to price: not this year’s earnings, which the rules fix, but the rate and the norms at the next reset, and anything the owner asks of the company in between. NTPC’s own dividend policy names CERC’s norms as a factor that may affect its margins and payout public.

Real estate: the timing

Bookings now, revenue at handover

A developer sells flats before it builds them: a booking or pre-sale. The buyer pays in instalments as construction goes on, and gets the flat years later. DLF recognises revenue from selling a unit at a point in time, when control passes to the buyer on completion public. Mahanadi’s buyers pay 20%, 30%, 30% and 20% over four years; revenue comes at handover example.

Read down the FY26 column: Mahanadi collects instalments from four years of bookings, ₹1,410 cr, but recognises revenue only on the flats sold in FY23, ₹1,000 cr example. FY26’s ₹2,000 cr of new bookings will reach revenue in FY29. Revenue and profit describe sales made three years ago; bookings and collections describe this year.

Real estate: the balance sheet

Customer advances: money held for buyers

Money collected before handover is not revenue yet. It sits on the balance sheet as a liability, customer advances (in Ind AS terms, contract liabilities): the developer owes the buyers flats. Mahanadi’s advances at 31 March 2026 are ₹2,110 cr example. They are settled by finishing and handing over the flats, not by repaying cash, unless a buyer cancels.

The law limits what a developer may do with that money. Under section 4(2)(l)(D) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, 70% of the amounts collected from a project’s buyers must be kept in a separate bank account and used only for that project’s construction and land cost, withdrawn in proportion to completion public. So a developer’s cash is partly someone else’s project money.

Go deeper: the source of the 70% rule

The wording above follows the Haryana Real Estate Regulatory Authority, Gurugram’s directions of 2 March 2020 on compliance with section 4(2)(l)(D), which set out what a chartered accountant must verify: that seventy per cent of the amounts realised from allottees went into a separate account at a scheduled bank, to cover construction and proportionate land cost, and that withdrawals followed the percentage of completion as certified by an engineer, an architect and a chartered accountant. Most of DLF’s projects are in Gurugram, and its notes show cash held in RERA escrow accounts, usable only for the relevant projects.

Real estate: the cash

Collections and net debt tell the story

Because buyers fund construction, collections matter more than revenue. In FY26 Mahanadi collects ₹1,410 cr and spends ₹840 cr building: a ₹570 cr surplus that turns opening net debt of ₹500 cr into net cash of ₹70 cr example, before land, overheads and tax.

The same mechanism runs in reverse. If bookings stall, collections fall while flats already sold must still be finished, and the gap is filled with debt. So read net debt (Part 11) and the trend in collections together. DLF reports that its development business reached zero gross debt in FY26 public.

The common mistake

Revenue is not this year’s sales

In FY26 DLF’s new bookings fell 5.1% and its revenue from selling property fell 4.0%. Read only those and the business looks to be shrinking. But collections rose 14.8%, and customer advances rose 39.0% public: more flats are paid for and still waiting to be counted as revenue.

Revenue growth, margins and P/E (Part 7) all describe handovers, which follow bookings by years. For a developer, start with bookings, collections and advances, and treat the P&L as a lagging record. The same habit helps with any business paid in advance.

So what

Questions before any verdict

  1. Utility: what is the regulated equity, and how fast will it grow? Commissioning schedules and admitted capex, not revenue.
  2. Utility: does it meet the norms, and do customers pay? Availability against 85%, and receivable days.
  3. PSU: what does the owner want? The payout floor, the stake still to be sold, and when the rules reset.
  4. Developer: are bookings and collections growing? Then read advances and net debt; treat revenue as a lagging record.

Here profit is set by a rule, shared with a government owner, or collected years early. Part 35, “Loss-making companies and IPOs”, turns to companies with no profit yet: reading a draft offer document, unit economics, cash burn and how long the money lasts.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Before trusting the usual ratios, ask what sets the profit: for a regulated utility, the return the regulator allows on the equity it approves; for a PSU, an owner who also makes policy; for a developer, cash collected years before the revenue is counted.
