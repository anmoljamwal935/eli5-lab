Many chips as one

Part 11 ended with a bill: training a large model takes tens of trillions of trillions of sums. No single chip can do that in a lifetime, or even hold the model while it learns. This part shows why, the ways the work is split across thousands of chips, how those chips are wired together, and why the wires, not the chips, so often decide how fast training goes.

A topic study built from the primary sources: the ZeRO, Megatron-LM, GPipe and Chinchilla papers, Meta’s Llama 3 report, Google’s TPU v4 paper, NVIDIA’s H100 and NVLink specifications and NCCL’s all-reduce cost model; every number below was checked against them · Read the main source ↗

It builds on Part 5 (a GPU is thousands of simple workers doing the same sum at once) and Part 6 (training nudges every number against its slope, at about 6 sums per number per token). Part 10 compared the chips; this part wires them together. Facts carry a tag: public means a published paper, spec sheet or plain arithmetic, example means a made-up or rounded number used to show how something works, and not public means the company has not said. The problem

The model does not fit on one chip

Training does not store each learned number just once. With the standard recipe, called mixed-precision Adam, every number needs 16 bytes: 2 for the number itself, 2 for its slope, and 12 for the step rule’s running notes about it public.

Meta’s Llama 3 405B has 405 billion learned numbers public. At 16 bytes each that is 6.5 trillion bytes (6.5 TB). The H100 GPU it was trained on holds 80 GB public. So the bare training state fills 81 GPUs before a single word is read example. Part 8 is about why that memory is so precious.

Go deeper: where the 16 bytes come from

The ZeRO paper’s accounting public: a 16-bit copy of each weight (2 bytes) and of its slope (2 bytes) for the fast arithmetic, plus a 32-bit master copy of the weight (4), Adam’s momentum (4) and Adam’s variance (4), kept at full precision so tiny nudges are not rounded away. Adam is the step rule from Part 6’s Go deeper box.

This still leaves out activations: the in-between results saved on the way forward so the backward pass can use them. ZeRO’s example: a 1.5-billion-number GPT-2 model, reading 32 sequences of 1,024 tokens at once, needs about 60 GB for activations alone public. Meta has not published Llama 3’s exact memory layout, so the 16 bytes here is the standard recipe, not Meta’s own figure not public.

The problem

One chip would need three thousand years

Memory is half of it. The other half is time. The Chinchilla paper’s rule for training cost is 6 sums per learned number per token read public, the rule from Part 6. Llama 3 405B read 15.6 trillion tokens: 6 × 405 billion × 15.6 trillion ≈ 3.8 × 1025 sums, matching the total Meta reported public.

Meta reports each H100 sustaining about 400 trillion sums per second in its largest run, roughly 40% of the chip’s peak public. At that speed one chip would need about 3,000 years. Spread over 16,384 chips it is about 67 days of pure arithmetic public. Part 11’s compute budgets are why every frontier model is trained like this.

The analogy

Three ways to cut a layered cake

Picture the model as a tall cake: a stack of layers, each layer a grid of learned numbers (Part 5’s grid multiplies). There are three basic ways to share it among chips. Each purple outline below is one chip.

Bake copies, and give each table different text to read: data parallelism. Slice straight down, so every chip holds a sliver of every layer: tensor parallelism. Separate the layers, so each chip holds a few whole ones: pipeline parallelism. Where the analogy breaks: cake slices never need to talk again. Chip slices talk constantly, and the rest of this part is about that talk. The bonus study, Inside LLM serving, cuts a finished model the same ways to answer chats.

Split 1: data

Copy the model, split the reading

Part 6 met the simplest split. Every chip holds the whole model, reads its own share of the batch, and works out slopes from that share. The slopes differ, because the text differs. Before anyone steps, the slopes are averaged, so every copy takes the identical step and stays identical.

This averaging is called an all-reduce: every chip puts its numbers in, and every chip gets the total back (divide by the number of chips for the average). It happens after every training step, and it moves one slope for every learned number in the model: billions of numbers, every step.

The mechanism

Averaging in a ring, no boss needed

The obvious way uses a boss: everyone sends slopes to one chip, it adds them up and sends the answer back. But then the boss’s wire carries everything, and it gets worse with every chip added. The standard fix passes pieces around a ring. Step through it with four chips, each holding its slopes cut into four pieces example:

Three steps of adding, then three of copying. Each chip sent 6 pieces, each a quarter of its slopes: 1.5 times its own data. With n chips it is 2(n−1)/n times, which creeps towards 2 but never passes it, however many chips join public. That is the ring’s great strength, and its limit: the time to share never shrinks as chips are added. It only stops growing.

Go deeper: the cost model

NVIDIA’s NCCL library documents the best possible all-reduce time as t = (S ÷ B) × 2(n − 1)/n, where S is the bytes each chip holds and B is each chip’s link speed public. The ring version came to deep learning through Baidu and Uber’s Horovod, based on a 2009 paper by Patarasuk and Yuan that showed it uses the network optimally when the data is large public. Every hop also costs a small fixed delay, so with thousands of chips libraries also use tree-shaped versions, which NCCL’s documentation describes too.

The fix for memory

Stop storing the same notes everywhere

Plain copies have a catch: every chip stores all 16 bytes per number, so the model must still fit on one chip. But Adam’s notes about a number are only ever used to update that number. So each chip can keep notes for just its own share, update just that share, and pass the result on. This is ZeRO, from Microsoft public.

The bars are the ZeRO paper’s own example: a 7.5-billion-number model on 64 chips public. Sharing the notes cuts each chip’s memory from 120 GB to 31.4 GB, with no extra traffic. Sharing slopes too gives 16.6 GB, still with the same traffic as plain copies. Sharing the weights as well gives 1.9 GB, at 1.5 times the traffic, because each layer’s weights must be fetched from their owners just before use. PyTorch’s version is called FSDP, fully sharded data parallel, and Llama 3 used it public.

Split 2: tensor

Slice every grid across chips

Sharding saves memory, but each chip still does a whole layer’s arithmetic for its share of the text. Tensor parallelism splits the arithmetic itself. NVIDIA’s Megatron-LM cut each layer’s first grid by columns and its second by rows, so each chip does half of both multiplies with no talking in between public.

Each chip ends with a partial answer, and one all-reduce adds them up. Attention splits the same way, each chip taking some of Part 7’s heads. That makes two all-reduces per layer going forward and two coming back public: hundreds per step, each waiting for the slowest chip. So tensor splitting stays where the wires are fastest. Megatron-LM used it across the 8 GPUs inside one server public.

Go deeper: Megatron-LM’s numbers

Megatron-LM trained an 8.3-billion-number model on 512 V100 GPUs: 8-way tensor splitting inside each server and 64 copies across servers. It sustained 15.1 thousand trillion sums per second, 76% scaling efficiency compared with one GPU, with the model growing alongside the chip count. Its servers had 300 GB/s between GPUs inside and 100 GB/s between servers public.

Split 3: pipeline

Give each chip a few layers

Pipeline parallelism gives chip 1 the first layers, chip 2 the next ones, and so on. Only the in-between results cross from chip to chip, once per stage, so slower wires will do. The catch is waiting: chip 4 has nothing to do until chip 1 has finished. GPipe’s fix is to cut each batch into micro-batches that follow each other down the line public. Choose how many:

The rose gaps are called bubbles. With K stages and M micro-batches, the waiting share is (K − 1)/(M + K − 1) public. In this simple drawing, where going back takes as long as going forward example, 16 micro-batches still leave 16% idle. GPipe found the overhead negligible from M ≥ 4K in its experiments, partly because recomputing saved results can be scheduled into the gaps public.

All together

Llama 3 used every split at once

Real runs combine the splits, each where its wires suit it. Meta trained Llama 3 405B on 16,384 GPUs arranged as 8 × 16 × 128 public: tensor splitting across the 8 GPUs of one server, pipeline splitting across 16 servers in a line, and 128 data-parallel copies of that line, each reading different text.

Each grid cell is one server of 8 GPUs. Meta calls this 4D parallelism: the fourth split, context parallelism, divides very long texts among chips and was switched on for the long-text stage of training public. Each copy read 16 sequences of 8,192 tokens per step: about 16 million tokens per step in all public.

The wires, inside

Inside a server: a private motorway

Inside one server, the eight GPUs talk over NVLink, through switch chips called NVSwitch, so any GPU can reach any other directly public. An H100 gets 900 GB/s of NVLink, counting both directions: 7 times its PCIe link to the server’s CPU, and 9 times a 400 Gb/s network card public.

The network card’s 400 gigabits per second is 50 GB/s each way, 100 GB/s both ways public. Newer systems stretch the fast domain further: NVIDIA’s GB200 NVL72 joins 72 Blackwell GPUs over NVLink at 1,800 GB/s each, about 130 TB/s in all public. A wider fast domain means bigger tensor splits before anything touches the slower network.

The wires, between servers

Between servers: a tree of switches

Past the server, every GPU gets its own network card and the traffic runs through switches: InfiniBand, or Ethernet carrying direct memory-to-memory transfers (RoCE). Llama 3 405B trained on RoCE and smaller Llama 3 models on InfiniBand, both at 400 Gb/s per GPU public. Meta’s 24,576-GPU cluster is a three-layer tree called a Clos network public:

Sixteen GPUs share a rack switch. 192 racks join through pod switches into a pod of 3,072 GPUs with full bisection bandwidth: cut the pod in half and the halves can still talk at full speed. Eight pods join at the top, but those links are oversubscribed 1:7, so only about a seventh of full speed is guaranteed between pods public. This tree shape, a fat tree, is a classic data-centre design public, and Meta’s scheduler keeps the chattiest work inside a pod.

Another design

Google’s TPU pods rewire themselves with mirrors

Google’s TPUs (Part 10) take a different road. Each rack holds 64 TPU v4 chips wired as a 4 × 4 × 4 cube with ordinary cables. Each face of the cube sends 16 optical fibres to optical circuit switches: boxes of tiny mirrors that steer light from any fibre to any other, and re-aim in milliseconds public.

With 48 of these switches, 64 cubes join into one 4,096-chip machine public. Because the wiring is just mirror angles, a job can take cubes from anywhere, broken cubes can be skipped, and the network’s shape can be changed to suit the job. Google reports the optical switches cost under 5% of the system and use under 3% of its power public.

Why it stops

The sums shrink, the sharing does not

Now put numbers on it. Take an 8-billion-number model, 4 million tokens per step, chips doing 400 trillion sums a second, and plain copies sharing 2-byte slopes over 50 GB/s links example. Double the chips and each chip’s share of the sums halves. The all-reduce time barely moves:

That is scaling in one picture. Past about 750 chips here, sharing takes longer than summing, and each doubling buys less example. Real systems fight back three ways: overlap sharing with the backward pass, which FSDP does public; put the chattiest splits on the fastest wires; and take bigger steps, so each chip has more sums per round of sharing.

Go deeper: what the lab assumes

No overlap between summing and sharing; NCCL’s best-case ring time with no per-hop delay; every chip at 400 trillion sums per second; plain copies, since ZeRO’s first two stages move the same bytes example. Model size cancels out: a bigger model has more sums and more slopes in the same proportion, so the bend sits at the same chip count, about 1 + 1.5 × tokens per step × link speed ÷ chip speed. Steps cannot grow forever either: McCandlish and colleagues found that each task has a largest useful batch, beyond which bigger steps stop speeding up learning public.

The common mistake

Twice the chips is not twice as fast

The natural guess is that doubling the chips halves the time. Meta’s own numbers show the gap even in a carefully tuned run: on 8,192 GPUs each sustained 430 trillion sums a second, on 16,384 each sustained 400 public.

Twice the chips gave 1.86 times the speed public. Meta’s explanation: to keep each step at 16 million tokens, each copy got a smaller share of every batch, so each GPU had less work per round of sharing public. That is the lab’s bend, seen in a real run.

The surprise

Faster chips need faster wires

Here is the twist. Make the chips faster and only the summing shrinks. The sharing takes exactly as long, so the wires matter more, not less. The lab’s default run, then the same run with a chip 2.5 times faster example:

The share of time spent summing falls from 42% to 23% with no change to the network. That is why each chip generation brings faster links as well: NVLink went from 900 GB/s per GPU on Hopper to 1,800 GB/s on Blackwell public. In the lab, the bend moves left whenever chips get faster than their wires.

The hidden cost

Something breaks about every three hours

Thousands of chips means thousands of things that can fail, and training runs in lockstep: if one GPU stops, every GPU waits. In a 54-day stretch of Llama 3 405B’s training, Meta counted 466 interruptions: 47 planned for maintenance, and 419 unexpected, about one every three hours public.

About 78% of the unexpected ones were hardware, confirmed or suspected, with GPUs and their memory at the top of the list public. Even so, only three needed a person to step in, automation handled the rest, and the run spent more than 90% of its time doing useful training public.

Go deeper: a slip in the report, and other lessons

Meta’s text says GPU issues were 58.7% of the unexpected interruptions. Its own table counts 268 of 419 in GPU categories, which is 64%. The gap comes from the table listing the 148 faulty-GPU cases as 30.1%, when 148 ÷ 419 is 35.3%. The drawing uses the counts public.

Other lessons from the same report: one slow but working GPU, a straggler, can slow thousands of others; midday heat cost 1–2% of throughput; and tens of thousands of GPUs pausing together swung the data centre’s power draw by tens of megawatts public.

The fix

Save often, restart fast

The defence is a checkpoint: every so often, every chip writes its share of the training state to storage. After a failure, the job swaps out the broken machine, reloads the last checkpoint and carries on. Everything since that save is lost and must be redone example:

So there are two levers: save more often, which costs pauses, and restart faster. Meta’s checkpoints were 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU, written to a storage system sustaining 2 TB/s with bursts to 7 TB/s, and it worked to shorten both checkpointing and job startup public. Google’s mirror switches attack the same problem from the other side, by routing around the broken part.

The whole picture

Many chips, wired as one machine

A large model needs more memory than one chip has and more sums than one chip could do in centuries. So the work is cut at several levels, with the chattiest splits on the shortest, fastest wires:

Tensor splits inside the server, on NVLink. Pipeline stages across servers. Data-parallel copies, with sharded optimizer notes, across the whole cluster. Every step ends with chips sharing what they worked out, and how fast that sharing goes, not how fast each chip sums, often sets the pace.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A big model is trained by thousands of chips acting as one: each holds a slice, does its sums, and then they all share. Past a point, the sharing, not the summing, sets the pace.
