Relative valuation and P/E bands

Most prices are judged by comparison: against similar companies today, and against the company’s own past multiples. Both are quick, both hide a DCF’s assumptions, and a change in the multiple can move a share as much as a change in its profit.

The idea

Two yardsticks: peers and history

Relative valuation asks a simpler question than a DCF: is this price high or low compared with something? There are two usual yardsticks. Similar companies today, and the same company in earlier years. Ramu trades at 20 times its earnings; its peers trade at 15 and 35, and its own year-end P/E has moved between 16 and 24 example.

On both yardsticks Ramu sits in the middle: the median peer is at 20, and so is its own five-year average. That sounds like an answer. The rest of this part shows why it is only the start of one.

Peer multiples

Borrow a peer’s multiple, get a price

The shortcut: take Ramu’s earnings per share, ₹5.00 for FY26, and multiply by a P/E that similar companies trade at. A P/E is how many years of today’s EPS a buyer pays for (Part 7). Using each fictional peer from Part 15 in turn, the same ₹5 of earnings becomes ₹75, ₹100 or ₹175 example.

Price from peers = EPS × peer P/E = ₹5.00 × 20 (the median) = ₹100

The median, the middle value, is usually preferred to the average because one extreme peer, like Masala at 35, pulls an average far more. Here the median is Ramu’s own 20, so the peer price is ₹100. Which peer you pick changes the answer from ₹75 to ₹175, so the choice of peers is most of the work.

Comparable?

A peer must match on growth, returns, debt

Same industry and similar size are not enough (Part 15). A fair peer also grows at a similar pace, earns similar returns on its capital, and carries similar debt, because those are what a P/E pays for. Plot the three tea companies and the multiples line up with growth example.

Masala’s 35 is not a price Ramu has earned: Masala grows 22% a year against Ramu’s 13.6%, earns 26% on its equity against 20%, and has no debt. Pahadi’s 15 comes with 8% growth, a 14% ROE and borrowings of 0.9 times its equity. Pricing Ramu at Masala’s multiple would pay for growth and returns Ramu does not have; at Pahadi’s, it would ignore growth and returns that Ramu does have.

Go deeper: when no peer is truly alike

Real peer sets are rarely clean. Adjust for the differences you can measure, such as growth (PEG, Part 15), debt (compare EV/EBITDA as well as P/E, Part 14) and accounting basis (consolidated, reported or adjusted EPS, Part 2 and Part 6), and write down the ones you cannot. A small, close set beats a long list from a screening site’s industry tag.

A DCF in disguise

A multiple hides a DCF’s assumptions

A P/E looks like a fact, but it is a DCF compressed into one number. Divide Part 24’s value of ₹76.7 by FY26 EPS of ₹5.00: that is 15.3 times. Run Part 24’s model with one steady growth rate for FY27–FY36, then 5% for ever, and every rate gives its own P/E example.

P/E = value a share ÷ EPS = ₹76.7 ÷ ₹5.00 = 15.3× · price ₹100 ÷ ₹5.00 = 20×

The market’s 20 times is the same claim as Part 25’s: about 15.3% growth a year for ten years, with new money earning 20% and a discount rate of 11.6%. (The two 15.3s are a coincidence: one is a P/E, the other a growth rate.) On this model Ramu’s band of 16 to 24 spans growth beliefs of about 11.0% to 18.7% a year, and Masala’s 35 would need about 25%. So “the peers trade at 20” quietly assumes the peers’ growth, returns and risk are Ramu’s too.

Own history

Ramu’s P/E band, FY22 to FY26

The second yardstick is the company’s own past. Take the P/E at each year end: price at 31 March divided by that year’s EPS. Ramu’s EPS rose every year, from ₹3.00 to ₹5.00 (Part 9), while its P/E wandered: 18, 16, 22, 24, 20 example.

The range, 16 to 24, is the P/E band; the five-year average is 20. The band turns into prices once you multiply it by EPS. On FY27E EPS of ₹5.75 (Part 7’s estimate) it gives ₹92 at 16 times, ₹115 at 20 and ₹138 at 24. Today’s ₹100 is a forward P/E of 17.4.

Go deeper: the year-by-year numbers

FY22: ₹54.00 ÷ ₹3.00 = 18 · FY23: ₹54.40 ÷ ₹3.40 = 16 · FY24: ₹85.80 ÷ ₹3.90 = 22 · FY25: ₹105.60 ÷ ₹4.40 = 24 · FY26: ₹100.00 ÷ ₹5.00 = 20. Average (18 + 16 + 22 + 24 + 20) ÷ 5 = 20. Five points make a thin band; analysts often use daily or monthly prices over five to ten years, against trailing or forward EPS, and say which.

Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

Ramu’s FY22–FY26 prices and EPS are fixed, as in the plan’s accounts. FY27E EPS is ₹5.00 × (1 + growth); 15% gives Part 7’s ₹5.75. The FY27 price is that EPS times the P/E you choose, and the change is measured from the FY26 close of ₹100, so dividends are left out. Price change = (1 + EPS growth) × (1 + P/E change) − 1; the small cross term is the two moving together. The band lines are the middle P/E minus and plus the width.

Re-rating

When the multiple moves, it can swamp earnings

A share’s price is EPS times P/E, so its change has two parts: earnings growth and a change in the multiple. A rising P/E is a re-rating; a falling one, a de-rating. Ramu’s EPS grew 13% to 15% every year, yet its price moved anywhere from −5.3% to +57.7% example.

1 + price change = (1 + EPS growth) × (1 + P/E change) · FY24: 1.147 × 1.375 = 1.577

In FY24 the re-rating from 16 to 22 added more than twice what earnings did. In FY26 a de-rating from 24 to 20 turned 13.6% more profit into a 5.3% lower price. Over the four years EPS rose 66.7% and the P/E 11.1%, so the price rose 85.2%, from ₹54 to ₹100. A buyer in FY25 at 24 times needs either faster earnings or another re-rating just to stand still.

The common mistake

Below the average is not automatically cheap

The tempting rule: buy below the band’s average, sell above it. It assumes the business and the world are the same as in the years that made the band. They change. Interest rates are the clearest case. Put Part 24’s value at each discount rate on Ramu’s ₹5 of EPS, and the P/E the same forecast deserves moves from 11.9 to 21.7 example.

A band formed when rates were low can sit above what a higher discount rate justifies (Part 23), and a P/E that slips below its old band may be a correct de-rating, not a bargain. The same goes for growth: if Ramu’s 13% pace slowed to 8%, the old 20 times would be too high. Before calling a share cheap against its history, ask what has changed since the band was drawn.

A band is a record of past moods, not a law. Shares can trade outside their band for years, in either direction.

Dates. NSE’s full bhavcopy files for 31 March 2022 and 31 March 2023 carry those dates. So what

Questions before calling a price cheap

  1. Cheap against whom? Name the peers, and check they match on growth, returns and debt, not just industry.
  2. What does the multiple assume? Turn it back into growth with a DCF or reverse DCF (Part 24, Part 25). A P/E of 20 and “15% growth for ten years” are the same claim.
  3. Has anything changed since the band? Interest rates, growth, returns or the business mix can move the band itself.
  4. How much of the return is re-rating? Split past and expected returns into earnings growth and P/E change, and ask whether the P/E part can repeat.

All of this assumes one company, one business, one multiple. A group that runs a tea brand, a café chain and a stake in a listed company needs a different multiple for each piece. Part 27, “Conglomerates and special cases”, values the parts separately, and covers holding-company discounts and dividend models.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A multiple is a DCF in shorthand: compare only with peers that grow, earn and borrow alike, read a P/E band as past moods rather than a law, and remember that price = EPS × P/E, so a re-rating can add or take away as much as a year of earnings growth.
