Working capital and the cash cycle

A company can be profitable and still run short of cash, because stock, customers’ dues and suppliers’ credit all hold money in the business. Three counts of days tell you how long a rupee stays stuck, and why growth makes it worse.

Built from Tata Consumer Products’ audited FY2026 consolidated statements, as printed in its Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, Tata Steel’s audited FY2026 consolidated statements, and the arithmetic of the ratios themselves · Read the source ↗

Already covered: Part 3 reads sales down to profit, with the cost of materials as one line; Part 4 introduces inventory, receivables and payables; Part 5 shows the working-capital line inside cash from operations (CFO); Part 9 warns that growth can eat cash; and Part 10 divides profit by the capital employed. This part explains where that cash goes. Facts carry a tag: public is a figure from a company’s audited statements, example is a made-up number for our teaching company, Ramu Chai Ltd, a small tea business. All amounts are ₹ crore (cr). Series definitions used here: days use year-end balances and 365 days; inventory days and payable days divide by the cost of goods (materials), receivable days by sales; working capital below means operating working capital, inventory plus receivables minus payables.

The problem

Cash stuck in running the business

Ramu has ₹55 cr of tea and packets in stock and customers owe it ₹45 cr example. That ₹100 cr is cash already spent, not yet back. Suppliers have not been paid ₹35 cr yet, which lends some of it back. Working capital is what remains: ₹65 cr that Ramu’s own money has to carry, every day it operates.

working capital = inventory + receivables − payables = 55 + 45 − 35 = ₹65 cr
Go deeper: two meanings of “working capital”

Accountants also use current assets minus current liabilities: Ramu’s 155 − 80 = ₹75 cr. That counts cash and short-term borrowings too. This part uses the operating version, inventory plus receivables minus payables, because it isolates the cash the trading cycle itself locks up. Companies pick their own definitions; the real-report panels show how far they can differ.

Three clocks

Inventory, receivable and payable days

Each balance becomes a count of days. Inventory days: how long stock waits before it sells. Receivable days (also called DSO, days sales outstanding): how long customers take to pay. Payable days: how long Ramu takes to pay suppliers. Stock and payables are carried at cost, so they divide by the ₹250 cr cost of materials; customers owe selling prices, so receivables divide by ₹500 cr of sales example.

inventory days = 55 ÷ 250 × 365 = 80 · receivable days = 45 ÷ 500 × 365 = 33 · payable days = 35 ÷ 250 × 365 = 51

Why cost and not sales for stock? The stock on the shelf was bought at cost; dividing it by sales would mix two different prices. Year-end balances can mislead a seasonal business like tea, where Q3 is the peak (Part 3), so compare the same date each year.

The cash conversion cycle

The gap Ramu has to fund

Follow one rupee. The leaves arrive on day 0. They sit 80 days, then sell; the customer takes 33 more days to pay: cash returns on day 113. But the supplier wants payment on day 51. From day 51 to day 113 Ramu is out of pocket: 80 + 33 − 51 = 62 days. That is the cash conversion cycle example.

cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days = 80 + 33 − 51 = 62 days

A shorter cycle means less cash tied up for the same sales. Every day shaved off is worth about one day’s sales at cost, here ₹250 cr ÷ 365 ≈ ₹0.7 cr per inventory or payable day, and ₹500 cr ÷ 365 ≈ ₹1.4 cr per receivable day.

Why growth costs cash

A bigger business needs more working capital

Working capital rises with sales. Ramu’s sales grew from ₹450 cr to ₹500 cr, 11%, so the same cycle would hold 11% more cash: ₹55 cr × 11% ≈ ₹6.1 cr. The cycle also lengthened from 58 to 62 days, which added the other ₹3.9 cr. Total: ₹55 cr became ₹65 cr, and that ₹10 cr is the working-capital line in Ramu’s cash flow example. This is one way to split it; the two effects interact.

This is the growth that eats cash from Part 9: Ramu made ₹50 cr profit and ₹85 cr EBITDA, yet CFO was ₹58 cr (Part 5), partly because ₹10 cr stayed behind in stock and dues. A fast grower can look profitable and still have to borrow.

The other side

When customers pay first

Some businesses have a cycle below zero. A retailer sells for cash within 30 days of stocking an item and pays its supplier after 60: for 30 days it holds other people’s money. Cash cycle: 30 + 0 − 60 = −30 days example. This is a one-off example, not Ramu’s accounts. Here growth releases cash instead of eating it, which is why supermarkets and FMCG distributors can expand fast without raising capital.

Try the “Customers pay first” button in the lab: the cash gap bar swings left of zero and the hit to CFO turns positive. The catch: the float lasts only while suppliers keep extending credit and sales keep growing. If either turns, the cash has to be found.

Two quick tests

Current ratio, quick ratio, and their limits

The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities: can Ramu cover what it owes within a year? 155 ÷ 80 = 1.94. The quick ratio drops inventory, the hardest asset to turn into cash quickly: (155 − 55) ÷ 80 = 1.25 example. Current assets are inventory 55, receivables 45, other 15 and cash 40; current liabilities are payables 35, short-term borrowings 30 and other 15.

  1. They are a snapshot, not a timetable. They do not say when each bill falls due or each customer pays.
  2. Quality hides inside. Stock may be unsellable; receivables may be uncollectable. Try “Stock piling up” in the lab: the current ratio rises while the cash cycle worsens.
  3. Normal depends on the business. Under 1 is routine for a retailer funded by suppliers, and alarming for a manufacturer with short-term loans.

Warning signs

When balances outgrow sales

Compare how fast each balance grew with how fast sales grew. Ramu’s sales rose 11.1%, but inventory rose 17.0% and receivables 18.4% example. Stock growing faster than sales can mean unsold goods; dues growing faster can mean customers paying later, or sales booked too generously. Here it is mild, and it is exactly the 4-day lengthening you saw. It is a prompt to ask why, not proof of a problem. Part 20 covers red flags in full.

Payables also grew faster than sales, 16.7%, which hides part of the strain: Ramu is leaning harder on its suppliers. Check the three together, and check them for several years.

For FY25: ₹6,997.40 cr + ₹3,434.37 cr − ₹362.49 cr = ₹10,069.28 cr, from the comparative column.

Go deeper: why these days are only a guide

Trade payables include money owed for services, packaging and capital goods, not just materials, so dividing them by materials cost overstates payable days. Now find it in a real report

TCPL’s own 21 days, and the ratios

TCPL reports its own figure. Its AGM presentation of 10 June 2026 shows net working capital days of 21 for FY26, down from 59 in FY20, and separately “India business NWC at −2 day” (slide 10). The annual report’s “Days of Operating Working Capital” reads 21 for FY26 and 26 for FY25 (p. 56). In the pages I read, TCPL prints no formula for it. Mine is 9.9 days. The definitions differ, and I can only show how much that matters.

same balances, but on sales: 3,526.76 + 1,148.33 − 3,875.30 = 799.79 · 799.79 ÷ 20,290.43 × 365 = 14.4 days adding other current assets less other current liabilities: (614.19 − 198.80) ÷ 20,290.43 × 365 = 7.5 → 21.9 days TCPL’s own ratio table (note 41): inventory turnover 5.69 (64 days), receivables turnover 20.11 (18 days), payables turnover 4.26 (86 days), all on average balances

So the same balance sheet gives 9.9, 14.4, 21.9 or other answers depending on three choices: cost or sales as the base, which lines are counted, and closing or average balances. My revenue-basis arithmetic lands near 21 but I cannot reconcile TCPL’s number exactly. The scope also differs: the AGM figure is a group measure, and the −2 days is the India business only. Compare days only across companies that use the same method, which is why this series fixes its own.

current ratio = 10,317.09 ÷ 6,578.56 = 1.57 · quick ratio = (10,317.09 − 3,526.76) ÷ 6,578.56 = 1.03

TCPL’s current ratio matches the 1.57 in its own ratio table (note 41, p. 482). Both are lower than Ramu’s 1.94 and 1.25, yet TCPL’s cash cycle is far shorter. A lower ratio and a short cycle can go together, because suppliers carry much of its stock (payables of ₹3,875.30 cr exceed inventories).

Tata Steel: a heavy-inventory contrast

Tata Steel, consolidated, year to 31 March 2026 public, from its Integrated Report & Annual Accounts 2025-26. Cost of goods: ₹74,468.93 cr + ₹18,159.14 cr + ₹989.62 cr = ₹93,617.69 cr; revenue ₹232,139.94 cr. Trade payables are the three lines in the balance sheet, ₹1,898.89 cr + ₹32,613.49 cr + ₹297.76 cr = ₹34,810.14 cr. Same method as above.

inventory days = 47,249.22 ÷ 93,617.69 × 365 = 184.2 · receivable days = 4,916.89 ÷ 232,139.94 × 365 = 7.7 · payable days = 34,810.14 ÷ 93,617.69 × 365 = 135.7 cash conversion cycle = 184.2 + 7.7 − 135.7 = 56.2 days · FY25: 171.3 + 8.8 − 112.6 = 67.5 days (cost of goods ₹95,000.65 cr)

Tata Steel locks up far more per rupee of materials than TCPL: a 56-day cycle against 10, with stock that is 15.7% of total assets against 10.2% for TCPL (₹47,249.22 cr of ₹301,254.11 cr, and ₹3,526.76 cr of ₹34,452.96 cr). Customers pay almost at once, so the cycle is all stock. Its own ratio table shows the same story on its own bases: inventory 73 days and debtors 8 days (average balances), current ratio 0.80 after excluding current maturities of debt (p. F3). By the plain definition the current ratio is 72,265.11 ÷ 96,917.64 = 0.75 and the quick ratio 0.26: heavy stock plus large short-term borrowings. The cycle also improved, by about 11 days, as payables rose.

Go deeper: how heavy is “heavy”?

On sales rather than cost, Tata Steel’s stock is 74 days against TCPL’s 63, a smaller gap. Materials are only part of a steelmaker’s costs (power, labour and other expenses sit elsewhere), so dividing by materials cost lengthens its inventory days; the same shortcut flatters payables. The contrast to rely on is the 56-day cycle and the share of assets in stock, not any single day count. Raw materials in transit alone were ₹3,444.12 cr (note 14).

Real figures are frozen at FY2026: TCPL’s audited Consolidated Financial Statements and AGM presentation, and Tata Steel’s audited consolidated statements and ratio table, in their FY2025-26 reports (printed pages as cited). Days are my arithmetic on year-end balances, not company-reported figures. Educational, not a recommendation.

So what

Three questions for any company

  1. How long is the cycle, and which way is it moving? Compute the three days for two or three years, on one method. A lengthening cycle drains cash even when profit rises.
  2. Is growth being paid for in working capital? Multiply the cash tied up by the growth rate. If that is large against CFO, growth needs funding.
  3. Do balances grow faster than sales? Stock and receivables outrunning sales is the first thing to ask about.

Ramu turns ₹85 cr of EBITDA into ₹58 cr of CFO and ₹13 cr of free cash flow, and working capital is only one of the leaks. Part 13, “Free cash flow and earnings quality”, adds capex and asks whether reported profit turns into cash that can be spent.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Every business funds the days between paying for its goods and being paid for them: inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days is the cash conversion cycle, and the longer it is, the more cash growth swallows.
