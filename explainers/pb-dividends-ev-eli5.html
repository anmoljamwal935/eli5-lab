The other price tags: P/B, dividends, EV/EBITDA

P/E prices the owners’ slice of profit. Three other tags price the same company from other sides: what its books say it holds, what it pays out, and what it would cost to buy the whole thing, loan included.

The question

One price, three more yardsticks

Ramu’s market cap is ₹1,000 cr example. Part 7 measured it against profit: ₹50 cr, so P/E 20. The same price can be measured against what the owners’ stake is carried at in the books (equity ₹250 cr) and against what they are actually paid (dividends ₹20 cr). Each yardstick answers a different question.

None of these replaces P/E. They are other tags on the same price, and each one is only as good as the thing it divides by.

The books

Book value per share, and P/B

Book value is owners’ equity, the assets left after every liability is subtracted (Part 4). Divide by the number of shares and you get book value per share: ₹250 cr ÷ 10 cr shares = ₹25 example. P/B is price ÷ book value per share: ₹100 ÷ ₹25 = 4.0. Press the buttons to move Ramu’s price and watch how many books fit inside it.

book value per share = equity ÷ shares = 250 ÷ 10 = ₹25 · P/B = price ÷ ₹25

Like ROE, use equity that belongs to the company’s own shareholders: in a group, Part 4’s non-controlling interest is not theirs. P/B below 1 would mean the market values the owners’ stake below what the books say it holds; Ramu’s 4.0 says the market pays four times book, which fits a business earning 20% on that equity (Part 10).

When P/B matters, and when it doesn’t

Book value suits some firms, not others

Equity is assets minus liabilities, at the cost the accounts recorded. That is a fair guide where the assets are the business: a bank’s loans and securities, a shipping or steel company’s plants. There P/B is a standard tag, and Part 29 shows why it is the main one for banks. It fails where the value is a brand, a recipe or a distribution network: brands a company built itself generally never reach its balance sheet (Part 6 shows what gets capitalised). Compare two firms at the same ₹120 share price example.

The brand-heavy firm’s P/B of 12 is not “expensive” on its face, just uninformative: its worth sits off the books. Even at TCPL, 55% of the assets are goodwill and other intangibles, mostly acquired brands (Part 10), which is why its P/B (shown at the end) needs care. One-off pair, not Ramu’s accounts.

Paying owners

Dividends: DPS, yield and payout

A company can keep its profit or pay part of it out. Ramu proposes ₹20 cr for FY26 example. Dividend per share (DPS) is that cut by shares: ₹20 cr ÷ 10 cr = ₹2.00. Dividend yield is DPS ÷ price: ₹2 ÷ ₹100 = 2.0%, the cash return on the price, before any change in the price. Payout ratio is dividends ÷ profit: 20 ÷ 50 = 40%. Retention is what is left: 60%, or ₹30 cr kept in the business.

DPS = 20 ÷ 10 = ₹2.00 · yield = 2 ÷ 100 = 2.0% · payout = 20 ÷ 50 = 40% · retention = 1 − 40% = 60%

A yield moves when the price moves, so a price that has fallen raises the yield and a high yield can be a warning, not a gift. A payout above 100% means paying out of past reserves. A low payout is not mean: kept profit funds growth, the next panel but one.

The Indian calendar

Interim, final, record date, ex-date

Indian companies pay in two ways. An interim dividend is declared by the board during the year, between two AGMs. A final dividend is recommended by the board with the year’s results and becomes payable only after shareholders approve it at the next AGM; TCPL’s own notes say the proposed dividend “is subject to the approval of the shareholders in the ensuing Annual General Meeting, except in case of interim dividend” public. The cash leaves in the next year (Part 5): TCPL’s FY26 cash flow carries the FY25 dividend, ₹8.25 a share, ₹816.34 cr. The ₹10 for FY26 was recommended on 8 May 2026, the AGM was held on 10 June 2026, and the payment falls in FY27, once shareholders have approved it.

The company fixes a record date: whoever is on its register that day is entitled. Because shares take a day to settle, exchanges set an ex-date, the first trading day the share trades without the dividend: buy before it to receive the payout, on or after it and you do not. The exchange notice for each dividend states both dates; check it rather than counting days. Once declared, the Companies Act requires the dividend to be paid within 30 days (section 127). Tax on dividends is Part 38’s subject.

Keeping and growing

Growth you can fund: ROE × retention

Profit kept is new equity. If the business keeps earning its ROE on the larger base, equity, and with it profit, grows at sustainable growth = ROE × retention. Ramu: 20% × 60% = 12% example. The ₹50 cr profit is 20% of ₹250 cr of equity; ₹30 cr is kept, so equity becomes ₹280 cr, 12% more, with no new shares and no new borrowing beyond the same balance. That links Part 10’s return to Part 9’s growth: a company that pays out more grows slower, unless it earns a higher return.

g = ROE × retention = 20% × 60% = 12% · equity 250 × 1.12 = 280

This is a ceiling for self-funded growth under steady returns and a steady payout, not a forecast. Borrowing more, issuing shares or a change in ROE moves it.

RAMU’S RETURN ON EQUITY (ROE)

The whole house

Enterprise value: buying it all, loan included

Market cap prices only the owners’ share. To buy the whole business you would also take over its loans, and you would keep its cash. Enterprise value (EV) = market cap + debt − cash. Ramu: ₹1,000 cr + ₹100 cr − ₹40 cr = ₹1,060 cr example. Think of buying a house: you pay the owner for their equity, you inherit the mortgage, and the money in the till is yours.

Series definitions, so every part agrees: net debt = borrowings − cash and cash equivalents (Ramu 100 − 40 = 60, so EV = market cap + net debt); EBITDA is sales less materials, employee and other expenses, before depreciation and amortisation (Part 3). Ramu’s canonical accounts show no non-controlling interest and no leases, so there is nothing more to add. A group with both has two more items.

Go deeper: non-controlling interest and leases

Non-controlling interest (Part 4) belongs to outsiders who own part of a subsidiary. Consolidated EBITDA counts 100% of that subsidiary, so EV adds their stake too, otherwise you would pair all of the earnings with only part of the price. Lease liabilities are debt-like: under Ind AS the cost of a lease sits in depreciation and interest, below EBITDA, so EBITDA is already free of lease cost, and adding leases to EV keeps the pair consistent. This series leaves leases out of debt unless it says so; the real-report panel shows both.

Other debt-like claims some analysts add: pension deficits, preference shares. Investments that earn outside EBITDA (such as stakes in associates) can be taken off. Every such choice moves EV a little; say which you used.

EV multiples

EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales

Divide EV by operating earnings and you get EV/EBITDA: ₹1,060 cr ÷ ₹85 cr = 12.5 example, read as “12.5 years of EBITDA to buy the whole business”. Divide by sales and you get EV/Sales: 1,060 ÷ 500 = 2.1, the price of one year of revenue, useful when profits are thin or negative. Neither is a promise, and EBITDA is before depreciation, interest and tax, so it overstates what reaches owners: heavy-machinery businesses need their depreciation replaced.

EV/EBITDA = 1,060 ÷ 85 = 12.5× · EV/Sales = 1,060 ÷ 500 = 2.1×

The rule: pair like with like. EV is what everyone who funded the business paid, so divide it by earnings from before anyone is paid (EBITDA, EBIT, sales). Market cap is the owners’ price, so divide it by what is left for owners (profit after interest and tax, EPS, equity, dividends).

Part 7’s last trap, finished

Why EV compares debt fairly and P/E doesn’t

Part 7 showed two companies each earning ₹50 cr for owners, both at P/E 20, one with ₹400 cr of debt. Here is what hides behind that 20 example. The borrower’s operations earn ₹30 cr more, but the extra ₹30 cr of interest takes all of it, so the owners’ ₹50 cr is identical. P/E sees only the gold. EV/EBITDA sees the whole bar, and prices the debt in.

Ramu pays ₹1,060 cr for ₹85 cr of EBITDA (12.5×). The borrower’s buyer pays ₹1,360 cr for ₹115 cr (11.8×): a cheaper operating business, carrying a loan that makes the owners’ profit more exposed to a bad year or a rate rise. Neither multiple is the verdict. EV multiples compare the businesses across different financing; Part 11 judges whether the financing is safe. One-off borrower, not Ramu’s accounts.

TCPL: P/B, dividends and growth

Tata Consumer Products, consolidated, year to 31 March 2026 public. Price: ₹1,086.30, NSE close on 6 November 2026, the dated close used across this series; times 98,95,61,780 year-end shares, the market cap is ₹107,496 cr. Owners’ equity is ₹21,787.53 cr, so book value per share is ₹220.17 and P/B is 4.93.

BVPS = 21,787.53 cr ÷ 98.956178 cr shares = ₹220.17 · P/B = 1,086.30 ÷ 220.17 = 4.93

TCPL’s board recommended a dividend of ₹10 a share for FY26, ₹989.56 cr in all, to be paid in FY27. Yield on the dated price is 0.92%. Against owners’ profit of ₹1,542.30 cr the payout is 64.2%, so retention is 35.8%. TCPL’s ROE is 7.08% (Part 10), so ROE × retention is 2.54%.

yield = 10 ÷ 1,086.30 = 0.92% · payout = 989.56 ÷ 1,542.30 = 64.2% · retention = 35.8% sustainable growth = 7.08% × 35.8% = 2.54% (kept profit 552.74 ÷ equity 21,787.53)

Read the low yield with the low ROE: a ₹1,086 share earns ₹15.59 a year and pays ₹10 of it, while the market prices the share at 4.9 times book. Retained profit alone would add only 2.5% to equity a year at this return; the price is paying for more than that, and Part 23 and Part 25 ask what. FY26 equity actually rose 8.9%, helped by ₹829.18 cr of other comprehensive income, mainly currency translation of overseas subsidiaries public, which retention ignores.

Five years of dividends. TCPL’s AGM presentation shows the dividend per share rising from ₹4.05 in FY21 to a recommended ₹10.00 in FY26: 2.5 times, about 19.8% a year compounded (my arithmetic, Part 9’s CAGR), though not in a straight line: FY24’s ₹7.75 dipped below FY23’s ₹8.45.

TCPL’s enterprise value, three ways

Start with the market cap, ₹107,496 cr public. Add borrowings of ₹2,120.31 cr (₹302.14 cr non-current plus ₹1,818.17 cr current). Subtract cash and cash equivalents of ₹3,046.57 cr, which is the series’ net debt: −₹926.26 cr, a net cash of ₹926 cr. Add the ₹1,401.33 cr non-controlling interest, because EBITDA counts the subsidiaries in full.

EV = 107,496 + 2,120.31 − 3,046.57 + 1,401.33 = ₹107,971.07 cr with leases: + 699.20 (608.64 non-current + 90.56 current) = ₹108,670.27 cr TCPL’s wider cash: 107,496 − 2,977.49 net cash + 1,401.33 = ₹105,919.84 cr

TCPL’s own “net cash ₹2,978 cr” also counts other bank balances, current investments and inter-corporate deposits (Part 4 reconciled it as ₹2,977.49 cr, with leases left out). Which is right? For a buyer, cash that can be reached is cash; the wider figure is fair if those deposits and funds are as good as cash. The honest answer is to show the range. Here the three EVs sit within 2.6% of each other because the bridge from market cap is small, under 1% for the basic version.

EBITDA = 20,290.43 − 11,855.64 − 1,661.00 − 3,981.99 = 2,791.80 · sales 20,290.43 EV/EBITDA: basic 107,971.07 ÷ 2,791.80 = 38.7× · with leases 38.9× · TCPL cash 37.9× EV/Sales: basic 107,971.07 ÷ 20,290.43 = 5.32× · with leases 5.36× · TCPL cash 5.22×

Set beside Ramu’s 12.5× and 2.1×, TCPL is priced at three times the multiple. (Its P/E on the same price is 69.7, ₹1,086.30 ÷ ₹15.59 basic EPS, Part 7.) TCPL’s presentation shows an EBITDA of ₹2,815 cr under its own definition; this series builds EBITDA from the statement’s lines, the same way as Ramu’s, so the multiples are comparable. These are descriptions of the dated price against FY26 numbers, not a view on whether the price is right.

So what

Three questions for any price tag

  1. What does it divide by? Equity for P/B, dividends for yield, EBITDA or sales for EV. If the divisor is not what the business is worth, the tag misleads.
  2. Does the top match the bottom? Market cap goes with owners’ numbers, EV with pre-lender numbers. Mix them and the ratio says nothing.
  3. What did you add to EV, and why? Debt, cash, outsiders’ stake, leases: name each choice, or two EVs for the same company will not agree.

Ramu now has four price tags: P/E 20, P/B 4.0, yield 2.0% and EV/EBITDA 12.5. Part 15, “Reading ratios together”, puts them side by side for two rival tea companies, and asks how to read a whole dashboard when no single ratio stands alone.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Every price tag divides a price by something: P/B by the books, yield by what is paid out, EV/EBITDA by earnings before the lenders are paid. Pair market cap with the owners’ numbers and EV with the whole business’s, and debt stops fooling the comparison.
