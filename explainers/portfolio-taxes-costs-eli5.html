Portfolio, taxes and costs

A good analysis still has to become a good decision: how much to put in, when to get out, and how much of the gain you actually keep. Size each idea by how wrong it could go, sell for reasons about value rather than price, and count the tax and costs before you trade.

The problem

The gain on screen is not yours

A reader follows Part 28 and buys 2,000 Ramu shares at ₹52, ₹1,04,000 in all example. Eighteen months later the price is ₹74 and Ramu has paid a ₹2 dividend. The screen shows a ₹44,000 gain. After trading costs and tax, ₹46,594 of the ₹48,000 is kept. Selling six months earlier, at 12 months, keeps only ₹37,509.

Three things decide how much of a good idea reaches you: how much you put in, when you sell, and what tax and costs take. This part covers each, then a calculator that does all three at once.

Position sizing

Size each idea by its downside

Part 28 gave Ramu a range, not a point. Use the bottom of it. Decide how much of the whole portfolio one idea may lose if the bear case comes true, say 2% example, then divide by the fall to the bear value. Bought at ₹52, the fall to ₹44.1 is 15.2%, so the idea may be 13.2% of the portfolio. Bought at ₹100, the fall is 55.9% and the limit is 3.6%.

largest position = loss you accept ÷ fall to bear value = 2% ÷ 15.2% = 13.2% of the portfolio

The same company deserves a different size at a different price. A wider range of values, or a price nearer the top of it, means a smaller position. Many investors also set a ceiling for any one idea, whatever the arithmetic says, because the bear case is a guess too.

Go deeper: other ways to size

Equal weights (the same amount in every idea) are simple and stop one idea dominating, but ignore how wrong each could go. Sizing by conviction alone is the weakest rule: confidence is not a measure of downside. Whatever rule you use, write it down before you buy. Also note that a winner grows as a share of the portfolio as its price rises; trimming back to your limit is a decision about size, not a verdict on the company.

When to sell

Sell on value, not on price

The gold band is Ramu’s range of values; the lavender line is the price example. Three reasons to sell are about value: the price has run above the bull case (C, valuation stretched), the thesis is broken so the band itself falls (D), or a better idea needs the money. Reaching the weighted value (B) means the margin is gone: time to re-check, not an order to sell.

Point A is the trap. The price fell 12% while nothing about the business changed. If the thesis still holds, a lower price is a wider margin of safety, not a reason to sell. Write down at the start what would break the thesis: a lost distributor, a cut in margins that is not a one-off, a governance flag from Part 20. Then sell when one happens, whatever the price is doing.

The better idea

Selling has a price of its own

Switching to a better idea is not free. The sale costs money, and a short-term gain is taxed now. With the price at ₹74, selling after 18 months leaves ₹1,47,672; selling at 11 months leaves ₹1,38,587. The new idea must first gain 6.8% just to put you back where holding Ramu would have example.

Holding does not cancel the tax; it delays it, and inside the yearly long-term exemption part of it may never be due. So a switch should clear a hurdle: the new idea’s margin of safety must be wider than Ramu’s by more than the cost of moving.

Field guide

Four biases that cost money

Most selling mistakes come from how minds work, not from a lack of information. Name the bias, then use a rule written in advance to beat it.

Costs

What one trade costs

Each delivery trade carries six charges public. Securities transaction tax (STT) is 0.1% of the value on both buy and sell; stamp duty is 0.015% on the buy only; NSE’s transaction charge was ₹297 per crore a side, and SEBI’s fee ₹10 per crore; GST is 18% on the broker’s services. Brokerage is set by your broker: zero at some, a fraction of a percent at others. Here it is 0.1% example.

About ₹458 per ₹1 lakh for a round trip, or 0.46%, at this brokerage. That is small for one investment held for years, and large for anyone who trades every week: fifty round trips a year would cost about 23% of the money traded once.

Go deeper: the small print on costs

STT is levied under section 98 of the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2004; stamp duty under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 as amended by the Finance Act, 2019, collected by the clearing corporations since 1 July 2020 (both as NSE lists them). NSE’s ₹297 per crore applied through most of FY2026; from 1 March 2026 it became ₹306.99 with the members’ investor-protection contribution cut to ₹0.01, leaving the total at ₹307 a side. NSE lists GST at 18% on stockbrokers’ services; brokers’ contract notes usually charge it on the exchange and SEBI charges they pass on as well, and this page does the same, so check your own note. Your depository and broker also charge a flat fee on each sale from your demat account; it varies by broker, so it is left out here. STT cannot be deducted when working out a capital gain (section 72(3) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 says so from April 2026); the other charges count as part of your cost or sale expenses.

Tax on gains

Twelve months splits the tax

A listed share held for more than 12 months is long-term public. Since 23 July 2024 a short-term gain on shares where STT was paid is taxed at 20%; a long-term gain at 12.5%, but only on the part above ₹1.25 lakh of such gains in a year. That ₹1.25 lakh is one allowance per person per year across all your shares and equity funds, not per stock.

Ramu’s gain after costs is ₹43,678. Short-term, it costs ₹8,736 plus 4% cess. Long-term, it fits inside the exemption and costs nothing, if no other long-term gains used the allowance that year. A 4% health and education cess applies on top of the tax (Finance Act, 2025, s. 2).

Go deeper: the law behind the lines

Sections 29 and 31 of the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 changed section 111A to 20% and section 112A to 12.5% on gains “exceeding one lakh twenty-five thousand rupees”, for transfers on or after 23 July 2024. The Government’s memorandum explains the 12-month holding period for all listed securities. Surcharge is left out here; for these gains it is capped at 15%. If your other income is below the basic exemption limit, the gain first uses up the shortfall (sections 196(2) and 198(3) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 spell this out). Losses: a short-term loss can be set against short- or long-term gains, a long-term loss only against long-term gains, and unused losses carry forward eight years (section 111 of the Income-tax Act, 2025).

Tax on dividends

Dividends are taxed at your slab

Since the Finance Act 2020 removed the company’s dividend distribution tax, a dividend is taxed in your hands at your own income-tax slab rate public. Ramu’s ₹2 a share on 2,000 shares is ₹4,000; how much you keep depends only on your slab example.

A company deducts 10% tax at source (TDS) from dividends it pays a resident individual once they pass ₹10,000 in a year; the Finance Act 2025 raised that line from ₹5,000 from 1 April 2025 public. TDS is not an extra tax: it is credited against what you owe, and appears in your annual tax statement. From 1 October 2024 money from a company buyback is also taxed as a dividend, with the shares’ cost allowed as a capital loss (Part 21). A 30% slab therefore favours a company that reinvests over one that pays out, other things equal.

The surprise

One month can beat every cost

Hold the same Ramu trade for 9 to 15 months and only the month changes example. Up to 12 months the tax is ₹9,085 with cess; one month later it is nil, because the gain turns long-term and fits under ₹1.25 lakh. All the trading costs together came to ₹574.

So before selling a winner just short of a year, check the date. Note “more than 12 months”: a share held for exactly twelve is still short-term. The saving only exists while your long-term gains for the year stay under the allowance; above it the gap is 20% against 12.5%.

The common mistake

Letting the tax decide

The opposite error is to hold a share whose thesis has broken, only to cross the 12-month line. At month 11 Ramu is at ₹74. Waiting saves ₹9,085 of tax. If the idea is broken and the price falls 10% while you wait, you lose ₹14,800 example.

Tax is a cost of a decision, not a reason for one. Decide on the business and the value first; then, between two dates that suit the decision, pick the cheaper one.

Frozen at FY2026

The rules changed in July 2024

The rates on this page start on 23 July 2024, the date of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024’s changes public. Before it, a short-term gain on listed shares was taxed at 15%, a long-term gain at 10% above ₹1 lakh. All of FY2026 falls under the new rates.

On 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act and the “previous year” became the “tax year”. It keeps the same rules for listed shares under new numbers: section 196 for short-term gains at 20% and section 198 for long-term gains at 12.5% above ₹1,25,000. STT on delivery trades stayed at 0.1%; NSE lists higher STT on futures and options from 1 April 2026, which this page does not use. Later Finance Acts can change any of this, so check the year you trade in.

Every rate here is as it stood for FY2026, checked against the gazetted Acts and NSE’s pages; the series is frozen at that year.

So what

Questions before you trade

  1. How much can this idea cost me? Size it from the bear value, not from how sure you feel.
  2. What would make me sell? Write the thesis-breakers down now, and the price above which it is stretched.
  3. Is the reason about value or about price? A fall with the thesis intact is a wider margin, not an exit.
  4. What does selling cost? Costs, short- or long-term tax, and how much of the ₹1.25 lakh is left this year.
  5. Is the switch worth it? The new idea has to clear the cost of moving before it beats the old one.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Size each idea by how much its bear case could cost, sell when the value changes rather than the price, and count costs and tax before you trade, because the return that matters is the one you keep.
