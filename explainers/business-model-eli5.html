Business model and unit economics

Behind every line of the accounts is a business that sells something, to someone, at a price. Revenue is volume × price × mix. Once you know what one unit earns, you can see what a company needs to break even, what happens if a big customer leaves, and why two segments with the same sales earn different profit.

Built from Ramu Chai Ltd’s example accounts and Tata Consumer Products’ FY2026 audited consolidated statements, Integrated Annual Report and AGM presentation, facts checked against them · Read the source ↗

This part opens Stage 4, the business behind the numbers. Earlier parts turned the accounts into ratios. Now we ask what produces them. It uses ideas from Part 8 (variable and fixed costs, contribution, break-even, operating leverage) and Part 10 (asset turnover), and does not re-teach them. Three levers

Revenue is volume × price × mix

Ramu sold 100 crore cups at an average ₹5.00 for ₹500 crore example. That average hides a mix: not every cup is sold the same way. The plan gives the sales by channel: shops ₹300 crore, Ramu cafés ₹125 crore, offices and institutions ₹75 crore. We assume packaged cups sell for ₹4.50 and café cups for ₹7.50. That is our one-off assumption, and the three channels then add up to exactly 100 crore cups and ₹500 crore. Each block below is one channel: width is cups, height is price, so its area is sales.

Three levers move revenue. More cups is volume. A higher ticket is price. A shift towards the dearer block is mix. Reports usually say which one drove growth, and they matter differently: volume needs more customers or more purchases, price needs customers who accept it, and mix changes what a typical sale earns.

One cup

What one cup earns, slice by slice

Unit economics asks what happens on one unit, before any company-wide cost. A ₹5.00 cup costs tea ₹0.90, milk powder ₹1.10, sugar ₹0.20, cup and packaging ₹0.30, and freight with distributor commission ₹0.25. That is ₹2.75 of variable cost, and ₹2.25 is left: the contribution, 45% of the price example. The first four slices are the ₹2.50 of materials in the P&L (Part 3). Freight sits in other expenses.

Volume spreads fixed costs

Profit per cup depends on how many cups

The contribution is ₹2.25 whether Ramu sells 80 crore cups or 120. What changes is the fixed share. Fixed cash costs of ₹140 crore (staff, rent, ads, base power) come to ₹1.40 a cup at 100 crore cups, ₹1.75 at 80 and ₹1.17 at 120 example. EBITDA per cup is what the contribution has left. It reaches zero at break-even, 140 ÷ 2.25 = 62.2 crore cups, where each cup carries exactly ₹2.25 of fixed cost. Part 8 showed how this works as operating leverage.

Mix

Not every cup earns the same

Take the two kinds of cup. A café cup sells for ₹7.50 and a packaged cup for ₹4.50. We assume the same ₹2.75 variable cost for both. So contribution is ₹4.75 against ₹1.75. We also assume cafés carry higher fixed costs, because each has rent and staff: ₹40 crore for the café cups, ₹2.40 each, and ₹100 crore for the rest, ₹1.20 each. These add to Ramu’s ₹140 crore, and the EBITDA of ₹39.2 crore and ₹45.8 crore adds to ₹85 crore. This split of fixed costs is a one-off example, not Ramu’s reported accounts example.

Now move 10 crore cups from one kind to the other, keeping 100 crore in all. Assume café fixed costs follow café cups, and the packaged side’s fixed costs (plant, brand, field force) stay put. Shifting to cafés raises sales to ₹530 crore and contribution margin from 45% to 48.1%, but café rent comes with it: EBITDA goes to ₹91 crore. Shifting to packaged tea cuts sales to ₹470 crore and contribution margin to 41.5%, and sheds café costs: EBITDA ₹79 crore. The EBITDA margin hardly moves (16.8% to 17.2%), because the fixed costs travelled with the mix. A mix shift changes margins only as far as costs do not follow it.

This is a teaching assumption, not a finding about tea. A real company would need the channel profit to say it, and the audited accounts rarely give it (the real-report box shows what they do give).

One big customer

Losing 12% of sales costs 32% of EBITDA

Ramu’s largest customer is a regional distributor that buys ₹60 crore a year, 12% of sales example. Assume it buys at the ₹5.00 average, so 12 crore cups. If it leaves, sales fall to ₹440 crore, and each of those cups was contributing ₹2.25: 12 × 2.25 = ₹27 crore. The fixed ₹140 crore stays. EBITDA falls from ₹85 crore to ₹58 crore, −32%. That is operating leverage again, but now the shock comes from one named buyer instead of the market.

Concentration risk is not only the lost sales. A big buyer can also push on price, ask for longer credit (Part 12’s receivable days) or start its own brand. Part 20 covers the warning signs in the accounts. Spreading sales over many buyers is safer, but it is not free: a lot of small buyers costs more to serve.

Repeat or one-off

Revenue you can count on next year

Two offices each pay Ramu ₹60,000 this year example. One signs a 12-month supply contract at ₹5,000 a month. The other places a one-off order for an event. The year’s revenue is identical. Next year it is not: the contract renews unless the office leaves, and the event order has to be won again. Recurring revenue (subscriptions, contracts, habits) is easier to forecast than one-off revenue. Not all recurring revenue is committed: a shopper who buys Ramu tea every week repeats from habit, not from a contract, and can switch at any shelf.

Heavy or light

How many assets each ₹100 of sales needs

The same sales can need very different assets. Ramu’s ₹500 crore of sales sits on ₹400 crore of assets, ₹80 for each ₹100 of sales, which is asset turnover of 1.25 (Part 10). A brand that hires contract packers and owns no plant needs little; a firm that owns estates and factories needs a lot. We drew two made-up neighbours at ₹40 and ₹160 per ₹100, with the same net margin as Ramu’s. They are one-off examples example. With a ₹10 profit on every ₹100 of sales, the lighter one earns four times the return on assets of the heavier one.

Asset-light is not simply better. A light business often has little to stop a rival copying it, and a heavy one can be a barrier in itself. Part 17 covers moats.

Now find it in a real report

Tata Consumer’s segments, FY2026

TCPL splits its sales in two ways, and they are not the same. The AGM presentation (10 June 2026, slide 7) shows four “key businesses” in ₹ crore public: India Beverages 6,467, India Foods 6,588, International 4,885 and Non-Branded 2,387, which sum to 20,327. After inter-segment eliminations of 37, that is the consolidated 20,290. The slide’s own note says this classification “has been provided for historical context and differs from that disclosed in the segment information in our financial results.”

The audited segment note (Note 38, Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, page 477) groups the same business as Branded (India and International) and Non-Branded. It reports revenue and segment results, so we use it for profit. Its revenue is before inter-segment sales, so India Business is 12,778.88, International 5,250.67 and Non-Branded 2,387.00 crore. The two tables cannot be matched line by line. The AGM’s International is the international tea and US coffee businesses, and its India lines include the overseas sales of Capital Foods and Organic India (slide 7 notes).

Which segment earns the most per rupee of sales? Segment result ÷ segment revenue, FY26 and FY25 (Note 38, page 477). A segment result is a profit measure before unallocated corporate costs, finance cost and other income. It is not EBITDA. Our reading is that it already counts depreciation, since the bridge to profit before tax adds nothing more for it:

  • FY26: all three within 0.2 points (11.8%, 11.9%, 11.7%). FY25: far apart (9.1%, 14.7%, 21.3%). The order flipped in one year: India’s result rose 47% on sales up 14%, International’s fell 6% on sales up 15%, Non-Branded’s fell 31% on sales up 25%. The report says India’s margin gains came from “tapering of tea cost, improved performance in the salt portfolio and growth businesses”, that International margins fell “primarily driven by an increase in coffee costs and the impact of U.S. tariffs”, and that Non-Branded profitability fell as “margins reverted to the normative levels” (pages 176–177).
  • Per rupee of assets the order differs. Segment result ÷ closing segment assets (Note 38, pages 477–478), our arithmetic: Non-Branded 9.6%, India 8.9%, International 7.5%. Segment assets include goodwill and brands from acquisitions, which is why the base is large. Assets per ₹1 of segment sales: India 1.32, International 1.58, Non-Branded 1.22. This is the asset side of the unit story from the panel on heavy and light.
  • Customer concentration. Note 38 splits revenue by segment and by place of sale (India ₹14,515.95 crore, 71.5%, USA, UK and rest of world; page 479). It does not name any single customer. That is not the same as there being none: this is a gap in what the note tells you, not a finding.

TCPL’s claim on two acquisitions. The AGM presentation (slide 25) says: “Capital Foods and Organic India, combined, grew 12%” and “Combined gross margin at 48%; significantly accretive to the base India businesses.” It shows revenue of ₹839 crore for Capital Foods and ₹478 crore for Organic India (together ₹1,317 crore, 6.5% of consolidated sales). This is TCPL’s claim, quoted. The audited statements give no gross margin for these businesses and the slide does not define it, so we cannot check the 48% against our consolidated 41.6% (Part 8), which uses our own definition. “Accretive” means they lift the average. What it adds in rupees is not stated.

What drives each segment: volume or price? The management discussion says (page 223): the India core portfolio of tea and salt “grew by 9% led by volume aided by increased distribution and the benefit of price increases implemented during the second half of FY25 to offset higher input costs”; growth businesses “grew by 24% with volume-led growth”; International “grew by 9% in constant currency, driven by US on account of pricing intervention taken during the year”; Non-Branded revenue grew 25% “led by higher volumes in Solubles and higher realisation in both Coffee Plantations and Solubles”. On page 222, the UK “declined by -1% in constant currency, led by volume decline on account of demand softness”. Where it gives both volume and value, revenue growth is volume × price and mix. A volume of +3% and value of +6% means price and mix added about 2.9% (1.06 ÷ 1.03). That is our arithmetic on rounded figures, and TCPL does not state it.

So what

Four questions about any business

  1. Who pays? Many buyers or a few? If one buyer is 10% or more of sales, find out what leaving would do to EBITDA.
  2. For what? What does one unit earn, and which channel or segment earns the most per rupee? A growing low-earning segment can drag the average.
  3. How often? A contract, a habit or a one-off order? How much of this year’s revenue can you count on next year?
  4. What stops them switching? A brand, a habit, a contract, a cost to move, a licence. If nothing does, today’s margin is borrowed.

This part looked inside one company. It treated switching as an open question, because the answer depends on the competition around it. Part 17, “Industry structure and moats”, answers it: how the market is shaped, how much share a company holds, and what keeps rivals out.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A company is a pile of unit economics: what one sale earns, how many it makes, who buys them and what keeps the buyers coming back. Read the accounts through that.
