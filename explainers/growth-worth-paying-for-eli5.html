Growth that’s worth paying for

A company growing 20% a year sounds better than one growing 10%. But growth can be flattered by a bad year, bought from someone else, spread over more shares, or paid for with cash the business does not have. Learn to read growth, and to ask what it cost.

The headline

Growth is a ratio of two years

Year-on-year (YoY) growth is this year divided by last year, minus one. Ramu’s revenue went from ₹450 cr to ₹500 cr in FY26: 500 ÷ 450 − 1 = 11.1% example. Part 2 used YoY for quarters. Here are five years of Ramu, on one scale.

One steady rate

CAGR: the steady rate between two years

The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the single steady yearly rate that would turn the starting figure into the ending one. Revenue went from ₹330 cr (FY22) to ₹500 cr (FY26) example. Count the years between the bars: four, not five.

CAGR = (end ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1 = (500 ÷ 330)^(1/4) − 1 = 10.95%

The trap

Why averaging growth overstates it

Say Ramu’s ₹500 cr rises 50%, then falls 50% example. The average of the two rates is 0%, yet revenue ends at ₹375 cr, 25% lower. The fall applies to a bigger number. An average of yearly rates is never below the CAGR, and the more the rates jump, the larger the overstatement.

Compounding

Growth on growth, and the rule of 72

Compounding means each year’s growth is earned on last year’s growth too. A shortcut: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ the growth rate in %. At 12% that is 6 years; exactly, 6.1. It works best between about 6% and 12% and drifts at the extremes.

Try it · compounding curve

Drag the rate, drag the years

Base effects

A low base flatters growth

Growth is measured against last year. After a bad year the base is small, so a rebound prints a big number. Ramu falls from ₹500 cr to ₹375 cr (−25%), then returns to ₹500 cr: +33.3%, yet revenue has gone nowhere example. A fall of 10% needs +11% to recover, 25% needs +33%, 50% needs +100%.

Organic or bought

Where the growth came from

Organic growth comes from selling more of what you already own. Acquired growth comes from buying another business and adding its sales. In one what-if, Ramu’s revenue goes from ₹500 cr to ₹600 cr (+20%), but ₹75 cr of the ₹100 cr is bought, so organic growth is only +5% example. The purchase cost cash (Part 5’s investing section) and adds goodwill (Part 4). It also counts only from the day it closes, so the following year gets extra months of it.

Share count

Profit can grow while EPS lags

Earnings per share (EPS) is profit divided by shares. New shares cut the same profit into more slices. Say Ramu’s profit grows 15%, from ₹50 cr to ₹57.5 cr, but it issues 1 cr new shares: EPS rises only to ₹5.23, +4.5%, not +15% example. The money raised should earn a return; if it does not, EPS falls behind.

Try it · dilution

Issue shares, watch EPS

Start from Ramu’s FY26: profit ₹50 cr, 10 cr shares, EPS ₹5.00, share price ₹100 example. Pick next year’s profit growth (the default is the +15% estimate) and issue new shares at ₹100. The profit growth is your assumption and ignores what the new money earns.

Growth that eats cash

Fast growth needs money first

Growing means stocking more tea, waiting longer for customers to pay, and building capacity. In FY26 Ramu’s ₹50 cr profit became ₹58 cr of operating cash, but ₹10 cr stayed stuck in stock and customers and ₹45 cr went on capex, leaving ₹13 cr example. Part 4 and Part 5 set this up; Part 12 teaches working capital and Part 13 free cash flow.

How much capital does growth need? Ramu holds about ₹62 of fixed assets and working capital per ₹100 of sales (₹310 cr over ₹500 cr; derived from the balance sheet example). If that held, +10% sales needs about ₹31 cr, covered by the ₹30 cr kept after a 40% dividend. +20% or +30% is not.

Worth paying for

Growth is good only if it pays

Two one-off plans example, both adding ₹5 cr of profit. Plan X spends ₹20 cr, a 25% return. Plan Y spends ₹50 cr, a 10% return, about what Ramu pays to borrow (₹10 cr interest on ₹100 cr). Same growth, very different value. Part 10 teaches ROCE, profit on all the capital in use: Ramu’s is 20.9% example. Growth earning above its cost adds value; below it, growth destroys it. Part 15 covers pairing growth with price (PEG).

So what

Four questions for any growth number

1. Growth from what to what? Prefer a multi-year CAGR to one YoY, and ask whether last year was a bad base. 2. Whose growth is it? Split organic from bought. 3. Per share? Compare profit growth with EPS growth. 4. At what cost? Check cash from operations beside capex, and the return on the capital the growth needed.

Next, Part 10, “ROE, ROCE and DuPont”, measures that last question: how much profit a company earns on the money put into it.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Growth is worth paying for when it is steady, mostly organic, not diluted away per share, and earns more on the capital it needs than that capital costs.
