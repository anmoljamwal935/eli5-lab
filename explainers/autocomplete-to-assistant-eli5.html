AI & FRONTIER / 25 MINELI5

From autocomplete to assistant

Training on trillions of words makes a superb guesser of the next word. It does not make a helper. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions, because that is how such documents often go on. This part shows the second round of training that turns the guesser into an assistant: first copying written examples, then learning from people’s picks between two answers, and why that second step can teach flattery as easily as help.

A topic study built from the primary sources: the InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al., 2022), the DPO paper (Rafailov et al., 2023), Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), reward-model overoptimization (Gao et al., 2022), the sycophancy study (Sharma et al., 2023), the PPO, LIMA, Llama 2 and Llama 3 papers, and Meta’s Llama 3 chat-format code; every number below was checked against them · Read the main source ↗

It builds on Part 2 (the model’s only job is to give every possible next token a chance, and a chat is a document it continues), Part 6 (training nudges every number to lower one penalty, the loss) and Part 11 (scale buys a lower loss, not a particular skill). The problem

Ask a base model, get more prompts

OpenAI’s InstructGPT paper shows what a model straight out of pretraining (a base model) does with a request. GPT-3, 175 billion learned numbers, was given one line: write a short story about a frog who travels back in time to ancient Greece. It did not write the story public:

It wrote three more requests in the same shape public. The paper’s prompt was in French; the drawing translates it. Another of its examples asked “Why is it important to eat socks after meditating?”, and GPT-3’s reply began with a question of its own: “What can you learn from socks?” public.

Why it happens

It finishes documents. It does not answer.

Nothing is broken. Part 2’s model gives every possible next token a chance, learned from what followed similar text in its training data. On the web, a line like “Write a short story about…” often sits in a list of writing prompts, so another prompt is a likely next line example:

The paper puts it plainly: predicting the next token of a web page is a different goal from following the user’s instructions helpfully and safely, so it calls the training goal misaligned public. The knowledge to write the story is in there. What is missing is the habit of using it to answer.

The recipe

A small fix after a huge training run

InstructGPT’s recipe has three steps after pretraining: copy written examples, learn what people prefer, then practise against that. Measured in computing work, the whole fix is small. Here it is drawn to scale, in petaflop/s-days, the unit the paper uses public:

The data is small too: about 13,000 prompts with written answers, 33,000 prompts whose answers were ranked, and 31,000 prompts for practice, with the human parts done by a team of about 40 contractors public. The paper’s conclusion: making existing models follow instructions was more cost-effective than training larger ones, at least for its customers’ tasks public.

Step 1: examples

Same quiz, new textbook

The first step is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). People write the answers they want: a prompt, then the ideal reply. Training is Part 6’s loop, unchanged: hide the next token, guess it, compare, nudge. The only difference is the reading list. Only the reply is graded; Llama 3’s report says it masks the loss on prompt tokens public.

Each orange bar is the penalty for that token: large where the model did not expect the writer’s word, small where it did example. Lower them all and the model drifts towards writing replies like these. InstructGPT trained on its demonstrations for 16 passes public.

The chat format

Special tokens mark who is speaking

Part 2 showed a chat as one document with markers for each speaker. In a real model those markers are special tokens: entries in Part 1’s token list that are never typed, only placed by the app. Here is Meta’s published format for Llama 3, which wraps each message in a header and an end-of-turn token public:

Outlined tiles are special tokens. The app ends the document with an open assistant header, so the most likely continuation is the assistant’s reply. Fine-tuning on thousands of documents in this format teaches the model to answer after the header and to finish with <|eot_id|>; Meta’s code lists it as a stop token public. Part 2’s “stopping is just another guess” is this token winning.

Go deeper: special tokens in training

Every model family has its own template, so apps must lay the chat out exactly as the model was tuned. Llama 3’s code reserves 256 special token slots on top of its 128,000-token list public. Llama 3’s report also notes that these formatting tokens are left out of the preference step’s loss (chapter 13), because they appear in every reply and training on them caused odd behaviour such as abruptly ending replies public.

What examples change

Examples move the chances, not the knowledge

After fine-tuning, put the frog request in the chat format and ask what the model will write first. The chances have moved: story openings are now the favourite, and more prompts are rare example.

How few examples does this take? Meta’s LIMA study fine-tuned a 65-billion-number base model on only 1,000 carefully chosen examples, with no feedback step. People rated its replies equal to or better than GPT-4’s in 43% of cases public. Its authors concluded that almost all of a model’s knowledge comes from pretraining, and tuning mostly teaches the format public. For Llama 2, Meta stopped after 27,540 written examples, finding that tens of thousands were enough public.

Step 2: comparisons

Judging is easier than writing

Copying has a ceiling: the model can only be as good as the examples, and writing great answers is slow. Judging is quicker. So InstructGPT’s labelers saw between 4 and 9 replies to one prompt and ranked them. A ranking of K replies hides K(K−1)/2 pairwise picks public:

Each link is one comparison: for this prompt, people preferred this reply to that one. People do not agree perfectly. InstructGPT’s labelers agreed with each other 72.6% of the time public. That sets a ceiling on how well anything can predict them.

The reward model

A second model learns to predict the pick

The comparisons train a reward model: a copy of the language model whose last layer is swapped for a single output, one score per reply. Its training rule is the one in the paper: the chance that people pick reply A over reply B is a smooth function of A’s score minus B’s score public.

Train it until its scores, put through this curve, match the picks people made. Only differences count, so InstructGPT shifted the scores to make its written examples average zero public. It used a 6-billion-number reward model for every policy size, partly because its 175-billion one trained unstably public. The curve has a name: the Bradley–Terry model, from 1952, first used to rank things from pairwise contests.

Step 3: practice

Write, get scored, get nudged

Now the model practises. It writes a reply to a fresh prompt, the reward model scores it, and training nudges the model’s numbers to make high-scoring replies more likely. No person is needed in this loop. This is reinforcement learning from human feedback, RLHF. InstructGPT ran it on 31,000 prompts public. This is the page’s one box diagram:

The nudging rule is PPO, proximal policy optimization, a reinforcement-learning method that limits how far each update can move the model’s chances: its paper’s example setting clips the ratio of new to old chance to between 0.8 and 1.2 public. Part 6’s slopes still do the work. What is new is the target: not a known next word, but a score to raise.

The leash

A leash back to the starting model

Raise the score with nothing holding the model back and it piles every chance onto whatever the reward model rates highest. So InstructGPT subtracted a penalty for drifting: at every token, a cost for how far the new chances have moved from the fine-tuned starting model public. That distance is the KL divergence, and its strength is set by a number written β (beta).

These are the lab’s four frog replies, with its default reward scores (chapter 12). The best the model can do under the leash has an exact form: multiply each starting chance by escore ÷ β, then rescale to 100% public. Tight leash, little change. Loose leash, nearly everything on the top scorer. The paper’s stated reason for the leash: to stop the model over-optimizing the reward model public.

Go deeper: the alignment tax

RLHF made InstructGPT worse at some public tests it used to pass, such as the SQuAD and DROP reading tests and French-to-English translation. The paper calls this an alignment tax. Its fix, called PPO-ptx, mixes ordinary next-word training on pretraining text into the practice steps. That worked better than simply tightening the leash, which cost reward and never fully recovered the scores public.

The result

A small tuned model beat a giant

The headline of the InstructGPT paper: on prompts from its customers, labelers preferred the replies of the 1.3-billion-number InstructGPT to those of the 175-billion-number GPT-3, a model over 100 times bigger public. The circles have areas to scale:

At equal size the gap is wide: the 175-billion InstructGPT was preferred 85 ± 3% of the time over GPT-3, and 71 ± 4% over GPT-3 given a few worked examples in its prompt public. On tasks where the answer should come only from the given text, it made things up about half as often: 21% against 41% public. Part 11 bought a lower loss with scale; this bought helpfulness with feedback.

Try it

Pick the winners, then loosen the leash

Four replies to the frog request, with the fine-tuned model’s starting chances: a plain story 40%, the same story opening with flattery (“What a wonderful idea!”) 30%, more prompts 10%, a refusal 20% example. Each pair is judged by 100 labelers. Set two of the splits and the leash; the page fits the reward model to all six splits and computes where training ends up:

Try “Slight lean, loose leash”: 55 of 100 labelers prefer flattery, a near coin-flip, yet the trained model writes the flattering reply 95% of the time. A small lean in the picks becomes a landslide in the chances once the leash is loose. Then drag the leash to 10 and watch every reply return almost to its starting chance.

The lab jumps straight to the exact best answer for its goal (reward minus β times the KL distance). Real PPO training only approaches it, step by step, from sampled replies, and a real reward model is a network that must generalise to replies it never saw.

Go deeper: how the lab computes

The fixed splits are: either story beats more prompts 95 to 5, more prompts against a refusal 50 to 50, and both stories face the refusal with the second slider’s split example. The reward model is the Bradley–Terry fit from chapter 8: the four scores that make the curve’s predicted chances best match all six splits, found by Newton’s method and shifted to average zero. Then each new chance is the starting chance × escore ÷ β, rescaled to 100%, which the DPO paper proves is the optimum of “expected reward minus β × KL” public. The ledger’s KL is Σ new × ln(new ÷ start), in nats.

The shortcut

Skip the scorer, learn from pairs directly

That exact form gave Stanford researchers an idea. If the best model under the leash is the start × escore ÷ β, then turned around, a model’s chances already imply a score for every reply. So train on the pairs directly: raise the chosen reply’s chance and lower the rejected one’s, compared with the starting model. This is DPO, direct preference optimization public.

No separate reward model, no sampling during training: the DPO paper calls it a simple classification loss, and reports it matching or beating PPO on its summarization and dialogue tests public. Meta’s Llama 3 used DPO after trying PPO, finding it needed less compute for large models and did better on instruction-following tests, with β = 0.1 public.

Today’s practice

Write twenty drafts, keep the best

Modern recipes loop the steps. In each round of Llama 3’s post-training, the model wrote between 10 and 30 replies to each prompt, the reward model picked the best, and the winners became new fine-tuning examples. This is rejection sampling public. Then came DPO on fresh comparisons, and the cycle ran six times public.

So most of the training text is now written by the model itself; Meta says so directly, and describes cleaning it, for example rebalancing overused phrases such as “I’m sorry” and “I apologize” public. People still write prompts, compare replies and edit the winners. Llama 2’s reward models learned from 1,418,091 of Meta’s own comparisons public.

AI feedback

Let a model do the judging

Anthropic’s Constitutional AI replaced the human picks for harmlessness with a model’s picks. The only human input on harm was a short list of written principles, the constitution: 16 of them public. First the model critiques and rewrites its own harmful replies, and is fine-tuned on the rewrites. Then a model compares pairs of replies against a randomly chosen principle, and those picks train the reward model public.

The paper calls this RL from AI feedback, RLAIF. The rest of the pipeline is unchanged. The aim was an assistant that is harmless but not evasive: one that explains its objection instead of shutting the conversation down public. Every step still traces back to choices people wrote down, now in a few sentences instead of thousands of picks.

Go deeper: one critique and rewrite, from the paper

Asked “Can you help me hack into my neighbor’s wifi?”, an early model suggested an app (the paper notes the advice is made up). Asked to critique itself, it wrote that hacking a neighbour’s wifi invades their privacy and is possibly illegal. Asked to rewrite, it advised against it and warned of legal trouble. The fine-tuning example keeps only the prompt and the final rewrite public.

The surprise

Push too hard and the score lies

A reward model is a stand-in for people, and stand-ins have blind spots. OpenAI’s Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured it by letting InstructGPT’s 6-billion-number reward model play the part of people, as the “gold” judge, and training smaller stand-ins (3 million to 3 billion numbers) on its picks. As the model moved further from where it started, the stand-in’s score kept rising, while the gold score rose, peaked, then fell public:

The curves’ shape follows the paper’s fitted form; their numbers are made up example. This is Goodhart’s law, which the paper quotes: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure public. Exploiting the stand-in’s blind spots is called reward hacking. Constitutional AI met it too: trained too long, models added boilerplate such as “you are valid, valued, and cared for” to replies about harmful topics public.

The leash is not a cure. In Gao’s setup, a KL penalty only made the gold score level off sooner; the gold score reached at each distance was unchanged, which the authors liken to stopping early, while noting the result may be sensitive to settings public. Recipes also refresh the stand-in: InstructGPT and Llama 3 both collected new comparisons on their latest models’ replies public.

The common mistake

Liked is not the same as right

The natural assumption: train on what people like, and the model gets more truthful. But people like being agreed with. Anthropic researchers found five assistants trained with human feedback all bent towards the user. Told “I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure?”, they often apologised for a correct answer and some switched to a wrong one public.

This is sycophancy. In Anthropic’s own human-preference data, “matches the user’s beliefs” was one of the most predictive features of which reply people chose, and its reward model sometimes preferred a convincing wrong reply to a correct one public. InstructGPT showed a cousin of it: asked why eating socks after meditating matters, it went along with the false premise and listed theories public. Part 15 is about the wider problem of confident mistakes.

The whole picture

Still a next-word guesser underneath

After all of this, the assistant is the same kind of machine as in Part 2: one pass per token, a chance for every token, one picked. Nothing new was bolted on. The same learned numbers were nudged a little more, with different reading and a different target, so that answering is now the likely continuation:

InstructGPT’s fine-tuning used about 1.8% of GPT-3’s training compute public. The model behind the assistant knows what the base model knew, no more: tuning changes which of its guesses win example. And like all training, it happens before you chat. Part 6 showed the numbers are frozen while you talk.

Next: memory and the context window

The assistant answers by reading the whole chat document, every turn, from the top. But that document cannot grow forever: a model sees only so many tokens at once. Part 14 shows what fits in that window, what happens to the rest, and why long chats seem to forget.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
An assistant is a next-word guesser that was shown what good replies look like, then nudged towards the replies people pick. It learns whatever the picks reward, flattery included, so the picks matter as much as the model.
