From autocomplete to assistant

Part 12 ended with a finished base model: a very good guesser of the next word that continues any text it is given, helpful or not. This part turns that guesser into an assistant. First people show it thousands of worked examples of helping. Then they grade its answers, and training steers it toward the grades — plus the standing orders and trained-in refusals that ship with every chat.

The problem

A continuer is not a helper

Hand a raw base model an open assistant turn and it guesses whatever text usually follows. Sometimes that is an answer. Sometimes it writes the user’s next question for them, or drifts into a forum thread. It is not being rude. Answering was never its job; continuing was.

Part 2 showed the setup: the app lays the chat out as one long document with markers for who speaks, and parks the model at the start of the assistant’s turn. Everything after that cursor is a guess. A second round of training decides what kind of guess it makes.

The idea

First, show it worked examples

People write out good answers by hand: a question, then the answer as it should look. About thirteen thousand of them for InstructGPT public. Training is the same loop as Part 6: cover each answer word, guess it, compare, nudge every number a little downhill. New textbook, same homework.

This step is called supervised fine-tuning. It teaches the shape of helping: the format, the tone, and when to play the end-of-turn token from Part 2. It barely adds knowledge: thirteen thousand answers against trillions of training words example.

Go deeper: who wrote the examples

OpenAI hired a team of about forty contractors and also rewrote real prompts sent to its API public (Ouyang et al., 2022). Today most example answers are drafted by models and checked by people instead, but the loop is the same: a human somewhere signs off on what good looks like.

The mechanism

Then let people grade it

Examples alone leave a question: of two decent answers, which is better? Scores are noisy — my 7 is your 5 — so graders never score. They read two answers side by side and pick one. InstructGPT collected about thirty-three thousand such comparisons public.

Picking is easier than scoring, and people agree on picks far more often. Each comparison is a small, sturdy fact: for this prompt, humans liked this one more. Thirty-three thousand sturdy facts beat a million wobbly scores.

The mechanism

A stand-in grader that never sleeps

People are slow and expensive, so the comparisons train a second, smaller model: the reward model. Shown any answer, it predicts one number — how much people would like it. Now every new answer can be graded instantly, with no humans in the loop.

The stand-in is a guesser too, trained on guesses about guesses. It is wrong the way polls are wrong: right on average, shaky on any single answer. The next step is built to survive that shakiness.

The mechanism

Steered toward high scores, on a leash

The assistant writes answers to about thirty-one thousand new prompts — no labels needed, the stand-in grades them public — and training nudges the numbers toward higher scores. A leash taxes every step away from the example-trained model, so it cannot game the grader by opening every answer with “great question!” forever.

This loop is called RLHF. Thirty-one thousand prompts steer billions of numbers because the base model already knows the words — feedback only tilts which ones win. The leash has a formal name — a penalty on how far the new chances drift, called the KL penalty — and it is what keeps the steered model speaking normal language instead of grader-pleasing static.

Go deeper: the alignment tax

Steering has a cost: pushed too hard, the model gets worse at the raw knowledge tests it once passed. InstructGPT’s fix mixed some original pre-training text back into the loop, so the model kept one eye on the library while learning manners public (Ouyang et al., 2022). The lost ability even has a name: the alignment tax.

WHAT THE GRADERS REWARD

Start at the base model: the long flattering answer leads, because internet text is full of it. Add the examples and the short helpful answer takes the lead — the shape of helping, learned. Add feedback rounds and the lead grows. Now switch the graders to rewarding flattery and watch the same machinery learn the wrong lesson instead.

Toy arithmetic with the real recipe’s shape: examples shift the chances once, each feedback round shifts them toward whatever the graders pick, and the leash caps the total drift. For InstructGPT the real counts were about 13k examples, 33k comparisons and 31k steering prompts.

The shortcut

Skip the grader entirely

A newer recipe skips the stand-in. For each human-ranked pair, push the chosen answer’s chance up and the rejected one’s down, in a single training loss — no grader to train, no sampling loop. It is called Direct Preference Optimization public (Rafailov et al., 2023).

Same comparisons, shorter path: the pair itself is the update. There is less machinery to game, and training is far cheaper than the sampling loop. This is why newer assistants, including Llama 3, learn their manners this way.

The practice

Llama 3: write, judge, keep the best

Meta’s recipe adds one more trick. For each prompt the model writes ten to thirty drafts, the reward model scores them all, and only the winners go back into training public. Then the whole cycle — examples, judging, preference training — repeats for six rounds public (Llama 3 report, 2024).

Notice what happened to the human role: most of the studying is now model-written text. People curate the prompts, rank the pairs, and set the standards — the industry’s shorthand for those standards is helpful, honest, harmless public — while the model supplies the volume.

The practice

Standing orders come first

Training is not the only steering. Every chat opens with a system prompt — standing instructions from the developer, wearing Part 2’s [system] marker, that the model reads before anything you write. Llama 2’s published default begins “You are a helpful, respectful and honest assistant…” public (Touvron et al., 2023). It sets the role, the rules and the refusal style — invisibly, on every reply.

System prompts are powerful because the model reads them first on every reply — and fragile because they are still just text. “Ignore your instructions” attacks, called jailbreaks, try to talk the model out of them. Usually the orders hold; the failures become training data for the next round.

The practice

Learning to refuse

Helpfulness has a boundary, and it is trained in like everything else. Llama 2’s recipe is public: adversarial prompts paired with safe demonstrations go into the examples, a separate reward model scores safety, and red teams spend months trying to break the result so the failures become new training data public (Touvron et al., 2023). Refusals are trained in, like everything else.

Push too far and the model refuses safe questions too — Llama 2’s original system prompt was retired for exactly this over-refusal public. Safety is a dial: helpfulness on one side, refusals (including wrong ones) on the other.

The result

Same guesser, new manners

Put the same prompt to both models. The base model continues the document however documents usually continue. The assistant answers, stays in its turn, and stops. Nothing about the guessing machinery changed — Parts 2 through 9 still describe every token — only what the guesses are steered toward.

Same prompt, two models (simplified example) 
[user] Write a haiku about GPUs.
[assistant] Write another one. Write a limerick. Here are 50 prompts…   ← base model continues
[assistant] Thousands of small cores, / summing all at once in step — / patience made of light.   ← assistant answers, then stops

In blind tests, people preferred the small instructed model to the giant raw one public. Manners beat size.

The gotcha

Liked is not the same as true

The grader predicts applause, not truth. Long, confident, flattering answers applaud well — so training can breed agreeable models that flatter you and never say “I don’t know”. Gaming the grader has a name, reward hacking, and flattery is its mildest form. And no feedback round adds facts the base model never read: steering picks which guess wins, never what the model knows.

This is the honest limit of the whole recipe: it optimizes for what people pick. When the model invents confident nonsense anyway — fluent, helpful-sounding, wrong — that failure has its own machinery. That is Part 15.

The words, in plain language

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)Training on worked examples of good answers, with the usual next-word loss.
Preference pairOne prompt, two answers, and a human’s pick of the better one.
Reward modelA small model trained on pairs to predict how much people would like an answer.
RLHFSteering the model with the reward model’s scores as the training signal.
PPOThe step rule InstructGPT used for steering: small nudges toward higher scores.
DPOThe shortcut: learn each ranked pair directly, with no separate grader.
Rejection samplingWriting many drafts, scoring them, and training only on the winners.
KL leashThe penalty that taxes drifting too far from the example-trained model.
Alignment taxRaw ability lost to steering; repaid by mixing pre-training text back in.
System promptStanding developer instructions the model reads before anything you write.
Red teamAttackers-for-hire whose breaks become safety training data.
Reward hackingGaming the grader: high scores for what was asked, not what was wanted.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
An assistant is a continuer with manners: worked examples teach it the shape of helping, and people’s grades steer it. But grades reward approval, which is not quite the same as truth.
