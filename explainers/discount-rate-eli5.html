Discounting and the discount rate

A rupee you will get in ten years is worth less than a rupee in your hand today. How much less depends on one number, the discount rate. Build it from a government bond, a premium for risk and the cost of debt, and see why nobody agrees on it exactly.

The problem

Money now beats money later

Offered ₹100 today or ₹100 in a year, almost everyone takes it today, for three reasons. Today’s rupee can earn interest while you wait. Prices rise, so a later rupee buys less. And a promise can break. At a safe 7% a year, ₹100 today grows to ₹107 in a year example. Run that backwards: ₹100 due in a year is worth ₹100 ÷ 1.07 = ₹93.46 today.

That backwards step is discounting, and the rate it uses is the discount rate. A safe interest rate already covers the first two reasons, because lenders build expected inflation into it. The third, risk, is what the rest of this part adds on top.

Present value

Compounding, run backwards

Part 9 grew money forward: at 12% a year, ₹100 becomes ₹100 × 1.12 × 1.12 and so on. Present value runs it backwards: divide by 1.12 once for every year of waiting. At 12% example, ₹100 due in one year is worth ₹89.29 today; due in ten years, only ₹32.20.

Present value = amount ÷ (1 + rate)years = ₹100 ÷ 1.1210 = ₹100 ÷ 3.106 = ₹32.20

Each bar is the one before divided by 1.12, so the bars shrink by the same proportion every year, never by the same amount. Part 9’s rule of 72 works in reverse too: at 12%, value halves about every six years (72 ÷ 12).

Go deeper: discount factors

1 ÷ (1 + rate)years is the discount factor: what ₹1 due in that year is worth today. At 12% it is 0.8929 for one year, 0.5674 for five and 0.3220 for ten. A stream of payments is valued by multiplying each payment by its year’s factor and adding up. That sum is exactly what a DCF does (Part 24).

Time and rate

The rate matters as much as time

Push the wait out to 20 years and the rate starts to decide everything. At 7%, ₹100 due in 20 years is worth ₹25.8 today; at 11.6%, the rate this part builds for Ramu, ₹11.1; at 15%, ₹6.1 example.

Going from 7% to 11.6% cuts the value of that distant ₹100 by more than half. That is why companies whose value lies mostly in the future, such as young fast growers, swing hardest when interest rates move: their cash is the furthest away.

The floor

The risk-free rate: the 10-year G-sec

Every discount rate starts from the return you could earn with no risk of not being paid. In India that is a Government of India bond, a G-sec: the government borrows in rupees, which it can always pay. Analysts usually take the 10-year yield, because a company’s cash arrives over many years, and it is the long-bond yield the RBI reports every week. A ₹100 bond with a 7% coupon pays ₹7 a year and returns the ₹100 at the end example.

The yield is the discount rate that makes those payments worth exactly today’s price; when the price falls, the yield rises. This series uses 7.0% as a round stand-in for it. The RBI box near the end gives the real yield near 31 March 2026: within a fifth of a point of the stand-in. Risk-free means free of default, not of change: the yield moves daily, and a bond sold before it matures can lose money.

The premium

Equity risk premium: an estimate, not a fact

Shareholders are paid last, after suppliers, staff, lenders and tax, and their returns swing. So they want more than a G-sec pays. The extra return expected from the share market as a whole, above the risk-free rate, is the equity risk premium (ERP). It is about the future, so it cannot be looked up; it has to be estimated, and careful estimators disagree. Published estimates for India run from about 5% to 7.6% public.

There are three ways to estimate it, and each has a weak spot. Look back at history, and the answer depends on the years you pick. Look across at what today’s prices imply, and it depends on someone’s forecasts. Ask in a survey, and you learn what people use, not what is right.

This series uses an ERP of 5.0%, at the low end of the published range, because it is round and easy to follow. It is a teaching assumption, not a claim about the right number. At 7%, Ramu’s cost of equity would be 14% instead of 12%; the lab shows what that does.

Go deeper: where each estimate comes from

Grant Thornton Bharat’s Equity Risk Premium Study (March 2025, cut-off 31 December 2024) puts the average annual return of Indian shares over 2001–2024 at 15.2%, an excess of 7.6% over the risk-free rate, or 5.1% after removing the effect of rising valuation multiples; its forward-looking estimate is 7.04%, using a 6.79% risk-free rate from CCIL’s 10-year zero-coupon yield; it concludes that about 6.75% is reasonable. Damodaran’s country risk table (updated 5 January 2026) adds a country premium to his mature-market premium of 4.23%. The EY–NSE Cost of Capital Survey 2024 (about 185 companies) reports the implied premium as respondents’ cost of equity minus the risk-free rate: 7.0% in 2024, after 7.5% in 2021 and 7.7% in 2017. Each estimator’s risk-free rate and method differ, so the premiums are not strictly comparable; they show the range of serious answers.

Beta

Beta: how much this share swings

The ERP is for the market as a whole. Beta scales it for one share: how far the share tends to move when the market moves. Beta 1.0 moves with the market, 0.6 about 60% as far, 1.4 about 40% further example. It is measured, not declared: line up the share’s weekly or monthly returns against an index such as the Nifty 50 over two to five years, and beta is the slope of the best-fit line.

Like the ERP, beta is an estimate. A different window, frequency or index gives a different number, and one share’s beta is noisy, so analysts often use the average for its industry. Damodaran’s January 2026 averages for Indian companies public: food processing 0.80 (220 firms), soft beverages 0.59 (5 firms), household products 0.51 (43 firms), each a simple average of firm betas from two-year and five-year weekly returns. Ramu’s beta of 1.0 is a round, neutral assumption example; on these averages a tea company could well sit lower. Beta counts only the swings that move with the market. A risk that hits one company alone, such as losing its biggest distributor (Part 16), is not in it.

Go deeper: levered and unlevered beta

Debt makes a share swing more, because profit after interest moves more than the business does (Part 11). Damodaran’s sheet also gives an “unlevered” beta with the effect of debt removed: 0.77 for Indian food processing. An analyst can take an industry’s unlevered beta and relever it for one company: beta = unlevered beta × (1 + (1 − tax rate) × debt ÷ equity). Ramu’s debt is ₹100 cr against ₹1,000 cr of equity at market value, so relevering adds about 7.5%: 0.77 would become 0.83.

Cost of equity

Cost of equity: floor plus premium

The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) puts the pieces together: cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × ERP. For Ramu, 7.0% + 1.0 × 5.0% = 12.0% example. It is the return Ramu’s owners need for waiting and for the risk they carry. Change any input and it moves.

Cost of equity = risk-free + β × ERP = 7.0% + 1.0 × 5.0% = 12.0%

12% is not an outlier. In the EY–NSE survey of 2024, equity research analysts said they use an average cost of equity of 12%, while companies used 14.2% public. CAPM is the most common method, not the only one, and many practitioners add a premium for small or unusual companies: about 2 points in that survey.

Blending

WACC: one rate for the company

Ramu is funded by owners and by lenders, so the cash the business throws off must pay both. Part 11 found Ramu’s debt costs 10%, or 7.5% after tax, because interest is deducted before tax at 25%. The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) blends the two, weighted by what each is worth today: equity at its market value of ₹1,000 cr (Part 14), debt at ₹100 cr example.

WACC = 12.0% × 1,000/1,100 + 7.5% × 100/1,100 = 10.91% + 0.68% = 11.59%, used as 11.6%

Debt is cheaper, but Ramu has little of it, so WACC sits just below the cost of equity. The series rounds it to 11.6%, and Parts 24 and 25 use exactly that. More debt would pull WACC down only up to a point: every extra loan makes the shares riskier, which raises beta, and lenders start charging more.

Go deeper: market weights, book weights and cash

Ramu’s book equity is ₹250 cr (Part 4). Weights at book value would give (12% × 250 + 7.5% × 100) ÷ 350 = 10.71%. But the owners’ 12% is a return on what their shares are worth now, ₹1,000 cr, so equity goes in at market value. Debt at book value is usually close enough unless rates have moved a lot. Some analysts weight net debt instead (₹60 cr after ₹40 cr of cash), which gives (12% × 1,000 + 7.5% × 60) ÷ 1,060 = 11.75%. The series uses gross borrowings; either choice is defensible if stated.

SITUATIONS

Go deeper: the formulas in the lab

Cost of equity = risk-free + beta × ERP. Cost of debt after tax = pre-tax cost × (1 − 25%). WACC = cost of equity × 1,000 ÷ 1,100 + after-tax cost of debt × 100 ÷ 1,100. Present value of ₹100 due in n years = 100 ÷ (1 + WACC)n. The weights do not move when the inputs do; in reality a higher discount rate lowers the share price and so the equity weight, which the series ignores to keep one change at a time.

Why it matters

A small change, a big value

The discount rate is the most argued-over input in a valuation, because value is so sensitive to it. Part 24 values Ramu with a full DCF. At 11.6% it gives ₹76.7 a share; at 10%, ₹108.7; at 13%, ₹59.5, against a price of ₹100 example. A point and a half either way moves the answer by more than a fifth.

Most of a company’s value sits in cash far in the future (in Part 24, about two-thirds of Ramu’s), and distant cash is exactly what the rate shrinks most. So treat a valuation as a range across sensible rates, and distrust one that only works at a single exact rate.

Go deeper: par yield, and why sources differ

A par yield is the coupon a 10-year government bond would need to trade at exactly its face value, worked out by FBIL from the bonds that trade. Newspapers and market trackers often quote the yield of one particular 10-year bond instead, which need not match the par yield exactly. Pick one source, say which, and give its date. To find it yourself: on rbi.org.in, open the latest Weekly Statistical Supplement and the table “Ratios and Rates”.

So what

Questions before using any discount rate

  1. What is the floor, and on what date? The 10-year G-sec yield, from one stated source.
  2. Whose ERP, and why that one? Published Indian estimates run from about 5% to 7.6%. Say which you used.
  3. Whose beta? The share’s own, or its industry’s, over which window.
  4. Which cash flows? Firm cash with WACC, owners’ cash with the cost of equity.
  5. What happens a point either side? If the answer flips, the rate is the whole argument.

Ramu’s rate is built: 7.0% + 1.0 × 5.0% = 12% for the owners, blended with 7.5% debt into a WACC of 11.6%. Part 24, “DCF, step by step”, uses it to discount Ramu’s forecast free cash flow year by year, adds a value for the years beyond, and arrives at a value per share.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Money later is worth money ÷ (1 + rate)years today. The rate is a floor, the G-sec, plus a premium for risk, and the premium is an estimate. Build it in the open, round it, and test a range.
