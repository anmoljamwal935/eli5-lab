Exporters: IT services and pharma

An exporter earns in dollars and reports in rupees, so part of its growth is the exchange rate, not the business. Strip that out first. Then check what can shrink the business itself: the contracts an IT firm signs, and the US inspections a drugmaker must pass.

The problem

Earned in dollars, reported in rupees

Narmada Infotech, a software services company in Pune, writes and runs software for American banks and bills them in US dollars example. In FY25 it billed $500 million. At an average of ₹80 to the dollar that was ₹4,000 cr of revenue. In FY26 it billed exactly the same $500 million for exactly the same work. But the rupee weakened to ₹84, so the same dollars became ₹4,200 cr example.

The rupee figure grew 5% with no new clients, no extra hours and no price rise. An exporter’s reported growth mixes two different things: how much more business it did, and what happened to the exchange rate. Investors pay for the first. The second can reverse next year. This part teaches you to pull them apart, then looks at what can make the business itself shrink: a missed contract for an IT firm, a failed factory inspection for a drugmaker.

The building block

Constant currency: last year’s rates, this year’s business

In FY26 Narmada actually billed $525 million, 5% more work. Constant-currency growth prices this year’s business at last year’s exchange rates, so only the business can move it: $525 million at ₹80 is ₹4,200 cr, 5% up on ₹4,000 cr. Reported growth uses this year’s rates: $525 million at ₹84 is ₹4,410 cr, 10.25% up example.

The two effects multiply rather than add: 1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025, so 5% more work and a 5% weaker rupee give 10.25%, not 10%. The extra 0.25 is the rupee’s effect on the new work. Infosys defines its own measure the same way: it compares “current period revenues in respective local currencies converted to US$ using prior period exchange rates” with last year’s reported revenue public.

reported growth = (1 + constant-currency growth) × (1 + currency effect) − 1 = 1.05 × 1.05 − 1 = 10.25%

One more layer

Why even the dollar figure moves

Indian IT companies report constant-currency growth against the US dollar, because clients pay in dollars, euros, pounds and more. So there are two currency layers. First, other currencies against the dollar. Infosys’s own table puts the euro at an average of $1.07 in FY25 and $1.16 in FY26: the dollar fell 8.4% against it public. A client paying €100 mn in both years is worth $9 mn more in dollars example.

Second, the dollar against the rupee: Infosys’s average rate was ₹84.56 in FY25 and ₹88.57 in FY26, a 4.7% weaker rupee public. A company that gets 32% of its revenue from Europe, as Infosys did in FY26 public, can show dollar growth well above its constant-currency growth when the euro is strong, and below it when the euro is weak. Read all three numbers: constant currency, dollars and rupees.

What actually happens to profit

The rupee moves profit more than revenue

Revenue is in dollars, but most costs are not. Narmada’s engineers in Pune are paid in rupees: ₹1,760 cr a year, 44% of FY25 revenue. Its staff at client sites in America and its foreign subcontractors are paid in dollars: $175 mn, ₹1,400 cr at ₹80 example. When the rupee weakens, revenue and dollar costs both rise 5%, but rupee costs do not move.

Revenue rises ₹200 cr, dollar costs rise ₹70 cr, and the other ₹130 cr goes straight to operating profit: up 15.5% on the same work. It is Part 8’s operating leverage, with costs that are fixed in rupees playing the role of fixed costs. Infosys puts a number on it in its FY26 annual report on Form 20-F: “every percentage point depreciation / appreciation in the exchange rate between the Indian rupee and the U.S. dollar, affected our incremental operating margins by approximately 0.44%” public. Narmada is built to match: its rupee costs are 44% of revenue, so a 1% weaker rupee adds about 0.44 points of margin.

Works both ways. A stronger rupee squeezes the margin just as fast, and wages paid in rupees rise every year. A margin gain that came from the rupee is not a sign that the business got better.

How companies soften it

Hedges smooth the swing, both ways

An exporter can sell dollars it has not yet earned at a rate fixed today: a forward contract. Narmada expects $325 mn a year of dollar income left over after its dollar costs, and sells half of it forward at ₹80 example. If the rupee weakens to ₹84, the half sold forward still fetches ₹80, so it misses out on ₹65 cr. If the rupee strengthens to ₹76, the half sold forward still fetches ₹80, so it gains ₹65 cr.

A hedge does not make money on average; it makes profit steadier. Infosys says it uses “foreign exchange forward and option contracts to mitigate the risk of changes in foreign exchange exposures”, and warns that these “may not be adequate” public. Companies book hedge gains and losses in different lines (some in revenue, some in other income), so read the notes before comparing two exporters’ margins.

Go deeper: the working

Same work as FY25, no growth. Net dollar income = $500 mn − $175 mn = $325 mn; half = $162.5 mn. At ₹84: profit without hedge = 500 × 8.4 − 175 × 8.4 − 1,760 = 970; the hedged half earns ₹80 instead of ₹84, so 162.5 × (80 − 84) ÷ 10 = −65; profit 905. At ₹76: 500 × 7.6 − 175 × 7.6 − 1,760 = 710; hedge 162.5 × (80 − 76) ÷ 10 = +65; profit 775. (₹ crore = $ million × rate ÷ 10.) Real forward rates also include the gap between Indian and US interest rates; this page leaves that out.

Go deeper: the assumptions

FY25: $500 mn of revenue at ₹80 (₹4,000 cr), dollar costs $175 mn (₹1,400 cr), rupee costs ₹1,760 cr, operating profit ₹840 cr (21.0%). All revenue is in dollars. Both kinds of cost grow in line with the work (constant-currency growth); there is no wage rise, so the margin moves only with the rupee and the hedge. “Rupee move +5%” means the dollar buys 5% more rupees (₹84). The hedge sells a share of FY26’s net dollar income (revenue less dollar costs, in dollars) at ₹80, and its gain or loss is counted in operating profit. Real companies hedge on a rolling basis at forward rates, and book the results in different lines.

The surprise

Dollar sales fell; rupee sales rose

Sun Pharma, India’s largest drugmaker, is also an exporter. In the March 2026 quarter its US formulation sales were $459 mn, “lower by 1.1%”. In rupees the same line rose from ₹40,204 mn to ₹41,997 mn, 4.5% up, because Sun’s exchange rate went from ₹86.62 to ₹91.49 a dollar public.

A reader who saw only the rupee table would think US sales grew. They shrank. The same filing season shows the gap in Sun’s emerging markets: dollar sales up 13.6% for FY26, but 8.2% in constant currency; for the quarter, 17.4% against 6.5% public. When a company gives a constant-currency figure, use it to judge the business. When it does not, divide by the exchange rate yourself.

Go deeper: the working

₹41,997.1 mn ÷ ₹40,204.1 mn − 1 = +4.46%. Check: (1 − 1.1%) × 91.49 ÷ 86.62 = 1.0446. Q4 FY25 dollar sales are not printed in the FY26 release; about $464 mn is implied by $459 mn and “lower by 1.1%” (459 ÷ 0.989). The exchange rates are the ones Sun prints under its sales table for each quarter.

IT: the order book

A deal win is not this year’s revenue

IT companies announce deal wins as total contract value (TCV): everything the client may pay over the life of the contract. Narmada signs a five-year, $300 mn contract with a bank. But it already did $40 mn a year of work for that bank, so $200 mn is a renewal and only $100 mn is net new example.

Spread over five years, the “$300 mn win” adds $20 mn a year of new revenue: 4% of Narmada’s $500 mn. Three checks follow. How much of the TCV is net new? Over how many years? And how much is firm? Infosys’s FY26 annual report says its contracts “can generally be terminated by the customers”, typically with a termination penalty public. TCV is a pipeline, not a promise.

Pharma: the factory

Every US pill comes from an inspected plant

An Indian generic drugmaker sells copies of off-patent medicines in the US. Each product needs US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval, and so does the plant that makes it. Tapti Pharma, in Gujarat, sells $200 mn a year in the US from three plants, and has 20 products waiting for approval, 8 of them to be made at plant A example.

So the question for a pharma exporter is not only how fast it grows, but which plant each dollar comes from, and how that plant’s last inspection went. FDA inspectors visit plants in India as they do in the US. A bad inspection does not touch the rupee or the price: it can switch off a slice of sales and freeze the products waiting behind it.

Pharma: the inspection

From a Form 483 to an import alert

At the end of an inspection the FDA hands over a Form 483 listing what its investigators saw. By the FDA’s own description it “does not constitute a final Agency determination” public. The FDA then classifies the inspection: NAI (no action indicated), VAI (voluntary action: the firm can fix it itself) or OAI (official action indicated: “an unacceptable state of compliance”), usually within 45 to 90 days public.

OAI can lead to a warning letter, a public letter listing violations; in Sun Pharma’s Halol letter the FDA says it “may withhold approval of new applications or supplements” from the plant until they are fixed public. The hardest step is an import alert: products from the plant are detained at the US border “without physical examination” until the FDA is satisfied public. The FDA can exempt drugs that are in shortage. Tapti’s plant A would lose $60 mn of sales, 30% of its US business, and eight launches would wait example.

A 483 is the start, not the verdict. What matters is the classification that follows, whether observations repeat from earlier inspections, and how much revenue the plant carries.

So what

Questions before any verdict

  1. How much did the business grow? Constant-currency growth, not the rupee figure. If none is given, convert back to dollars yourself.
  2. How much of the margin is the rupee? Use the company’s stated sensitivity, and ask what happens if the rupee strengthens.
  3. Is the order book real? TCV split into net new and renewal, its length, and how fast the contracted backlog turns into revenue.
  4. For a drugmaker: which plants carry the US business, and what did the FDA last say about each? A 483, its classification, warning letters and import alerts.

Exporters live on the dollar and on foreign regulators. The next sector part comes home: Part 32, “Consumer companies”, splits growth into volume and price, looks at rural demand, and asks why investors pay such high P/Es for soap and biscuits.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Judge an exporter on constant-currency growth, treat the rupee’s help as weather that can turn, and remember that its business can shrink without any change in demand: a contract that ends, or a plant that fails its inspection.
