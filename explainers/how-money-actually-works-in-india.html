How money actually works

Money is not one object. It is a layered set of promises: RBI liabilities settle between banks; bank liabilities are the deposits most people spend; government currency and fiscal accounts connect to both. The course builds those layers from double entry, then follows them through lending, taxation, inflation, exchange rates and crises.

What counts as money?

Start with the issuer, not the appearance. A ₹500 note and an SBI balance are both spendable rupees, but the first is an RBI liability and the second is SBI’s liability. A ₹10 coin and ₹1 note are Government of India liabilities. RBI reserves are RBI liabilities too, but ordinary households normally cannot hold them. [MONEYINDIA pp. 1, 3–4]

1.1 Assets, liabilities and wealth

Financial claim

Your deposit is your asset and the bank’s liability. A loan is the borrower’s liability and bank’s asset. A government bond is the holder’s asset and Government’s liability.

Real resource

A house, machine, road, skill or medical service is not someone else’s monetary promise. Doubling rupee claims without expanding these resources does not double real wealth. [MONEYINDIA pp. 1–3]

Supporting background — stock versus flow. A balance is a stock measured at a time; spending, lending and income are flows per period. ₹300 lakh crore of M3 cannot be compared directly with one year’s GDP without acknowledging that one is a stock and one is a flow.

1.2 Three functions, several aggregates

Money serves as a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. No instrument performs all three perfectly. Inflation weakens storage; illiquid assets may store value but are poor payment media. RBI therefore reports multiple aggregates rather than a single metaphysical “money number.” [MONEYINDIA p. 3]

1.3 Why fiat rupees are accepted

Fiat does not mean “unsupported.” It means no legally fixed gold redemption promise. Rupee demand is reinforced because taxes, contracts, prices and bank deposits are denominated in rupees; banks settle through RBI money; legal institutions and RBI’s price-stability mandate support confidence; acceptance today makes acceptance tomorrow more likely. This is economic and institutional backing, distinct from gold convertibility. [MONEYINDIA p. 4]

1.4 History changes the plumbing

The Paper Currency Act centralized note issue in 1861; the RBI Act was enacted in 1934 and RBI began on 1 April 1935; nationalization followed in 1949. The 1956 minimum-reserve regime replaced the proportional system. After 1991 policy shifted toward market operations; automatic monetization through ad-hoc Treasury Bills ended in 1997; flexible inflation targeting and the MPC arrived in 2016; retail digital-rupee pilots began in 2022. Demonetization in 2016 altered the form and availability of money rather than being an ordinary policy-rate operation. [MONEYINDIA pp. 4–5]

RBI money: created against what?

Central-bank money is created when RBI expands a liability—usually reserves or currency—while acquiring an asset or claim. “Printing” is too vague: name the asset, liability, counterparty, maturity and any sterilization. [MONEYINDIA pp. 7–10, 42]

2.1 Double entry is the grammar

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

If RBI buys ₹10,000 crore of G-secs from a bank, RBI records +G-secs and +bank reserves. The bank records −G-secs and +reserves. The bank is more liquid, not mechanically richer. If the seller is a non-bank, its bank also credits a deposit, so reserves and broad money can both rise. [MONEYINDIA pp. 7–8]

2.2 Notes can be a composition swap

A bank converting ₹100 crore of reserves into vault cash loses ₹100 crore of reserve assets and gains ₹100 crore cash. RBI replaces a reserve liability with currency issued. Total RBI liabilities can remain unchanged. Manufacturing paper is not the economic act; issuance and balance-sheet entry are. [MONEYINDIA pp. 2, 5–6]

2.3 Minimum reserve is not a maximum

The historical minimum-reserve framework required at least ₹200 crore of specified gold and foreign securities, including at least ₹115 crore of gold; remaining note liabilities could be matched by eligible rupee assets. The source’s conclusion is that this minimum asset-cover rule is not a ₹200 crore ceiling. Absence of a simple nominal ceiling still leaves binding real, inflation, FX, credibility, legal and financial-stability constraints. [MONEYINDIA pp. 6–12]

2.4 Repo, FX and seigniorage

A repo temporarily creates reserves against a secured RBI claim, then reverses principal at maturity. An FX purchase creates rupee reserves against foreign assets; an offsetting G-sec sale can sterilize the reserve injection. Seigniorage is the income advantage of issuing a low- or non-interest-bearing monetary liability while holding earning assets—not simply face value minus printing cost. In the source illustration, ₹500 × 6% = ₹30 gross annual asset return before costs, provisions and risk. [MONEYINDIA pp. 9, 12]

Loans create deposits—and constraints matter

At origination, a bank books a loan asset and a matching deposit liability. It does not create RBI reserves. Spending across banks then triggers reserve settlement. That sequence reconciles “loans create deposits” with the need for funding, liquidity and capital. [MONEYINDIA pp. 13–16]

3.1 Cash deposit versus loan origination

Deposit ₹1 lakh cash: your asset changes from currency to an HDFC claim; HDFC gains cash and owes a deposit. M1 can remain unchanged because currency with the public falls while a demand deposit rises. Originate a ₹50 lakh home loan: HDFC creates a loan asset and deposit liability together. These are different transactions. [MONEYINDIA pp. 12–14]

If the borrower pays Priya at SBI, HDFC loses the borrower deposit and transfers reserves; SBI gains reserves and credits Priya. Aggregate deposits are unchanged by the payment itself. 3.4 Creation, repayment, interest, default

Principal repayment from a deposit reduces the loan asset and deposit liability together. Interest reduces the payer’s deposit but becomes bank income/equity before later spending recirculates it. Default reduces the loan asset and capital/provision; the original deposit can remain elsewhere. Do not call all three “money destruction.” [MONEYINDIA p. 17]

Government accounts and the RBI toolkit

Government spending, taxation and borrowing move balances between the Government’s RBI account, bank reserves and private deposits. Judge a fiscal operation over the full sequence and specify who bought the debt. [MONEYINDIA pp. 20–22]

4.1 Three fiscal sequences

  1. Spend an existing RBI balance: Government deposit −; recipient bank reserves +; private deposit +.
  2. Tax then spend: tax first reduces private deposit/reserves and raises Government deposit; later spending reverses those entries. “Taxes destroy money” is incomplete without the time window and monetary boundary.
  3. Issue a bond to a household then spend: the household swaps deposit for G-sec; Government balance rises; spending restores deposits elsewhere. The private sector ends with a government asset, not proof of direct RBI financing.

Primary issuance funds Government; secondary trading transfers an existing bond between holders. Automatic monetization via ad-hoc Treasury Bills ended in 1997; WMA is temporary cash support and FRBM restrictions separate routine primary financing from policy operations, subject to legal exceptions. [MONEYINDIA pp. 21–22]

4.2 Corridor and transmission

The repo rate is not a command to every mortgage. RBI steers overnight conditions with a corridor and liquidity operations. Funding costs, deposit/lending rates, bond yields, credit, demand, output gap, inflation, expectations and FX respond with variable lags and sometimes opposing shocks. [MONEYINDIA p. 23]

4.4 QE and exporter FX flow

QE is large-scale asset purchasing intended to influence longer yields, portfolio allocation, financial conditions and signaling when short-rate policy is insufficient. 9.3 Direct-answer drill

Who creates Indian rupees?

RBI creates banknotes and reserve money; Government issues coins and ₹1 note; commercial banks create rupee-denominated deposits.

Why can’t RBI print away poverty?

Poverty concerns real income, productivity and resources. More claims without more supply redistribute purchasing power and can raise prices/FX pressure.

Why can’t banks create infinite loans?

Capital, credit risk, funding, liquidity, reserve settlement, supervision, profitability and qualified demand constrain expansion.

How can higher rates reduce inflation?

By discouraging marginal borrowing and spending, raising discount rates and influencing FX/expectations, but only with lags and especially when demand pressure matters.

How does money support growth?

Adequate liquidity and credit support exchange and investment; too little can constrain activity; excess nominal demand beyond capacity mainly raises prices.
