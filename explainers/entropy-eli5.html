Entropy: why energy spreads out

Entropy is not "mess". It measures how widely energy has spread out. The Sun's real gift to Earth is energy that is still bunched up, and every event on Earth happens as that energy spreads.

The puzzle

What does the Earth get from the Sun?

Most people say "energy". But over long stretches Earth sends back as much energy as it takes in. If every unit of energy leaves again, what did we actually receive?

Today the balance is slightly off: NASA and NOAA found Earth's energy surplus roughly doubled between 2005 and 2019, which is why the climate is warming. The video's point concerns the long-run balance that keeps temperatures steady.

Carnot's problem

Steam engines wasted almost everything

In the 1820s a young French engineer, Sadi Carnot, asked why British steam engines beat French ones. The video says the best engines of the day turned only about 3% of the fire's heat into useful motion. He set out to find the true limit.

Carnot's story, briefly: as a 17-year-old École Polytechnique student he fought, unsuccessfully, near Paris in 1814. In 1821 he visited his exiled father, who also loved machines, and the two talked steam engines (MacTutor biography).

The building block

A perfect engine in four moves

Carnot imagined an engine with no friction and no leaks: a chamber of air with a piston that turns a flywheel. A very hot bar and a very cold bar take turns touching the chamber. Move 1: the hot bar touches, heat flows in and the air expands, pushing the piston up. Move 2: the bar is removed, the air keeps expanding and cools. Move 3: the cold bar touches and the flywheel pushes the piston down, squeezing heat out into it. Move 4: the bar is removed and the squeezed air warms back up.

Carnot himself believed heat was an invisible fluid ("caloric") that is merely carried through the engine, never used up. Later work showed that part of the heat is really turned into work. The limit he found still holds.

Reversible

Run it backwards and nothing is lost

With no friction, you can run the engine in reverse: the flywheel's motion pulls heat out of the cold bar and pushes it back into the hot one. After as many reverse laps as forward laps, everything is exactly as it began. Even so, the engine is not 100% efficient.

The limit

Temperatures alone set the maximum

Some heat must be dumped into the cold bar, or the piston could never return. The best possible share of heat turned into work is 1 − (cold ÷ hot), measured on the Kelvin scale, where zero means the gas particles have stopped moving. The video's 32% fits steam at 160 °C with a cold side near 20 °C (our assumption). The design and the gas do not matter, only the two temperatures.

Why not 100%

Two ways to push toward 100%

Make the hot side hotter, the cold side colder, or both, and the limit rises. It reaches 100% only with an infinitely hot source or a cold side at absolute zero. Kelvin's insight: at zero the gas pushes with no force, so squeezing it costs no work and no heat is lost. Neither condition can be built.

Real engines

Energy doesn't vanish, it spreads out

Real engines add friction, heat leaking into metal and air, and uneven temperatures. The total energy never changes, but it ends up smeared over the walls, the axle and the surroundings, and nobody can gather it back. Energy is most useful when concentrated and least useful when spread out.

The name

Entropy: how spread out energy is

In 1865 Rudolf Clausius named this quantity entropy. Concentrated energy has low entropy; spread-out energy has high entropy. He summed up the first two laws of heat: the energy of the universe is constant, and its entropy tends toward a maximum. The everyday word "disorder" fits, but "energy spreading out" is the clearer picture.

The model

Two tiny bars of atoms

The laws of physics run the same forwards and backwards, so why does time seem to have a direction? The video's toy model: two metal bars of 8 atoms each. Energy comes in indivisible packets, and more packets means hotter. The left bar holds 7, the right 3. Packets hop randomly between atoms. Touch the bars together and packets can hop across; any one arrangement is as likely as any other. A snapshot may catch 9 on the left and 1 on the right.

Counting

Improbable, not impossible

Heat flowing from cold to hot looks like a broken law. Ludwig Boltzmann's insight was to count. Nine packets on the left and one on the right can happen in 91,520 ways. An even 5–5 split can happen in 627,264 ways, about 6.9 times as many. Add up every outcome where the left bar ends with more than its starting 7 packets and the chance is 10.5%. Not impossible, just less likely.

Scaling up

More atoms and the odds collapse

Repeat the experiment with 80 atoms per bar and 100 packets (70 left, 30 right). The chance the left bar ends hotter than it started drops to 0.05%. An everyday solid holds around 100 trillion trillion atoms (10²⁶), so heat flowing from cold to hot is so unlikely it never happens.

An analogy

A scrambled Rubik's Cube

A Rubik's Cube has exactly one solved arrangement and about 43 quintillion reachable ones. Turn it at random and you will almost surely land in a mess. Entropy rising is not a force pushing things apart, only the fact that there are overwhelmingly more spread-out, messy arrangements than special ones.

Try it

When can heat go uphill?

Two bars start with the packets you choose, then touch. Every arrangement is equally likely, so counting gives the exact chance that the left bar ends with more packets than it started with. The "never happens" rule says 0%. Watch the gap shrink as you add atoms.

The bookkeeping

Cooling a house costs entropy elsewhere

An air conditioner moves heat from the cool inside to the hot outside, lowering the house's entropy. That is allowed because the power plant behind it raises entropy by more: concentrated fuel energy spreads into hot machinery, wires and waste heat. Local order is always paid for by more spreading elsewhere.

The answer

A steady stream of low-entropy energy

Earth is not a sealed box. Sunlight arrives concentrated, in fewer, higher-energy photons (light particles). Earth sends the same energy back as about 20 times as many lower-energy photons, roughly the ratio of the Sun's temperature to Earth's. Plants growing, storms, and people breathing all run on that conversion.

Passing it down

Each step spreads the energy more

Plants capture sunlight and build sugars. Animals eat plants, and bigger animals eat smaller ones. At each step the energy ends up more spread out, and in the end all of it leaves Earth as heat. Life is a channel through which concentrated energy spreads.

Idea vs evidence

How sure is each claim?

Settled: Earth's energy in and out balance, and life runs on the Sun's flow. Debated: that life actively speeds up entropy production. The video cites seawater with cyanobacteria making 30–680% more entropy, a figure we could not trace to a source. Speculative: Jeremy England's proposal that energy poured onto matter favours structures that dissipate it, eventually giving life. Quanta reports his formulas are generally accepted, while a Harvard biophysicist calls the life interpretation "extremely speculative".

Where it began

Gravity flips the rule about smooth

If entropy only rises, it was lower in the past, and lowest just after the Big Bang (the "past hypothesis"). Yet the early universe was hot, dense and nearly perfectly smooth: temperature the same everywhere to within about 0.001% (figure cited in the video). Smooth sounds like high entropy, until you add gravity. Gravity pulls matter into clumps, so for matter under gravity, smooth is the unlikely arrangement.

Fuel

Clumping turns spare energy into heat

As the universe expanded and cooled, matter fell together. Falling turns stored energy into motion that could do work, like water running downhill through a turbine. When pieces collide, motion becomes heat, usable energy shrinks and entropy rises. Stars, planets, galaxies and life all formed along the way.

Black holes

Black holes are the entropy champions

In 1972 Jacob Bekenstein proposed that a black hole's entropy is proportional to the area of its surface. Critics objected: entropy implies temperature, temperature implies glowing, and black holes don't glow. Stephen Hawking, initially unconvinced, worked it out in 1974 and found they do glow faintly (Hawking radiation). The Milky Way's central black hole, 4.3 million Suns, has a temperature near 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ K (our calculation, matching the video's "a hundred trillionth of a Kelvin").

The ledger

Nearly all entropy hides in black holes

Counting entropy in units of Boltzmann's constant (k): all stars in the observable universe hold about 9.5 × 10⁸⁰, and the gas between stars and galaxies about 7 × 10⁸¹ (Egan and Lineweaver, 2010). The early universe, per the video, held about 10⁸⁸. Our Milky Way's central black hole alone holds about 2 × 10⁹⁰ (the video rounds this to 10⁹¹), and all supermassive black holes together about 3 × 10¹⁰⁴. So the early universe held roughly 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ % of today's entropy, a vanishingly small starting point.

The arrow of time

Time points the way entropy grows

The basic laws don't prefer a direction. The direction comes from odds: the universe began in a very unlikely state and drifts toward likelier ones. That is why we never see an asteroid un-crash or a planetary system unmix back into dust. In the far future, more than 10¹⁰⁰ years from now, after the last black hole has evaporated, the universe reaches its most probable state, the "heat death". Nothing usable is left, and forwards and backwards would look the same.

Don't despair

Complexity peaks in the middle

Maximum entropy is dull, but minimum entropy is not the most interesting state either. Pour milk into tea: unmixed is plain, fully mixed is plain, and the beautiful swirls appear in between. We live in the middle, so use the low entropy while it lasts.

So what

Three questions to ask next time

  • Used up, or spread out? Energy is conserved. What runs out is its usefulness.
  • Impossible, or improbable? Ask how many atoms are involved. For a handful, "uphill" happens; for a real object, never.
  • Who pays? Any pocket of order, a cold house, a plant, a cell, is bought with more spreading somewhere else. For Earth, that is Sun to space.
