P/E: what it says and when it lies

The P/E ratio divides a share’s price by the profit behind it, so it tells you how many years of today’s profit the price equals. It is the most quoted number in Indian markets, and the easiest to misread. Drive it yourself, then meet the five ways it fools people.

Built from Ramu Chai Ltd’s example accounts and Tata Consumer Products’ audited FY2026 annual report, facts checked against it · Read the source ↗

Part 1 set a share’s price against the value of the business. Part 3 walked sales down to profit after tax and previewed EPS. This part turns that profit into the ratio everyone quotes. Ramu Chai Ltd is a made-up tea company, and its numbers carry example. Tata Consumer Products (TCPL) is real; its audited figures carry public and are frozen at FY2026, the year to 31 March 2026. The problem

Same price, different profit

Two tea companies each trade at ₹100 a share example. Share A earns ₹5 a year for each share; share B earns ₹2.50. A price on its own cannot say which is dearer. You need to set it against what the share earns, and that is all a P/E does.

Step one: EPS

EPS is profit cut into shares

Earnings per share (EPS) is profit that belongs to the owners, divided by the number of shares. Ramu earned ₹50 crore after tax and has 10 crore shares, so each share’s slice is ₹5 example. “Owners” matters: in a group, profit is also shared with outsiders who own part of a subsidiary, and only the parent’s owners’ share counts.

Go deeper: basic and diluted EPS

Basic EPS divides by the average number of shares in issue during the year. Diluted EPS also counts shares that could be created, such as employee share options, so it is never higher. For most companies the two are close; TCPL’s are 15.59 and 15.58, shown at the end of this page public.

Step two: P/E

P/E is the price in years of profit

P/E is price ÷ EPS: ₹100 ÷ ₹5 = 20. Stack Ramu’s ₹5 slices end to end and 20 of them fill the price. Read it as “20 years of today’s profit”. Divide the whole company’s market cap by its whole profit and you get the same 20: ₹1,000 crore ÷ ₹50 crore example.

“Years to earn it back” is a way to read the number, not a promise: it assumes profit stays flat and all of it reaches you. A company keeps part of its profit and pays out the rest as dividends (Part 14), and profit moves.

Step three: the flip side

Earnings yield is P/E upside down

Earnings yield is EPS ÷ price, or 1 ÷ P/E. At a P/E of 20, each ₹100 of price comes with ₹5 of yearly profit: a 5% yield. A lower P/E is a higher yield. Part 23 sets this against what a safe deposit pays, which is how a P/E becomes a judgement example.

Which year’s profit

Trailing looks back, forward guesses

Trailing P/E uses the profit already reported: Ramu’s FY26 EPS of ₹5, so 20. Forward P/E uses an estimate for the year ahead. Suppose FY27 is expected to bring ₹57.5 crore, an EPS of ₹5.75: the same ₹100 now buys 17.4 years, not 20 example. A forward number is only as good as the guess; for real companies it comes from management’s outlook or analysts’ forecasts, never from the accounts.

Trap one

A one-off gain makes P/E look cheap

Say Ramu sold an old plot and booked a ₹30 crore gain after tax on top of ₹50 crore of tea profit example. EPS jumps to ₹8 and P/E drops to 12.5, though the tea business is the same. Strip the gain out and it is 20. The reverse also works: a one-off charge makes P/E look dear. Part 3 showed the exceptional-items line; Part 6 covers why companies adjust. Press One-off gain in the lab.

Trap two

Cyclical companies look cheapest at the peak

Steel, cement and similar businesses swing: profit surges when prices are high and slumps after. Hold the price at ₹100 and divide by each year’s EPS (an example cycle, 10 crore shares): the lowest P/E, 10, comes in the best year, just before profit falls example. A low P/E at peak profit is a warning, not a bargain. Part 33 covers cyclicals; the lab’s Cyclical peak shows the forward P/E doubling.

Trap three

Losses and tiny profits break P/E

With 10 crore shares at ₹100, the market cap is ₹1,000 crore whatever the profit example. At ₹50 crore profit P/E is 20. At ₹2 crore it is 500, which tells you nothing. At a ₹20 crore loss EPS is negative and P/E is “not meaningful” (n/m): you cannot earn back a price from a loss. Use sales, cash and the path to profit instead; Part 35 covers loss-makers. Try Loss-making.

Trap four

High P/E can be cheap, low P/E dear

A fast grower at ₹200 with EPS ₹5 has a P/E of 40, and a shrinking firm at ₹24 with EPS ₹3 has a P/E of 8 example. If profit grows 25% a year for one and falls 20% a year for the other, three years on the P/E on that year’s EPS is 20.5 and 15.6. The “cheap” one is catching up fast. That is a value trap: cheap because the business is shrinking.

These growth rates are assumptions chosen to show the effect, not forecasts. Part 9 covers growth maths and Part 15 the PEG ratio, which sets P/E against growth.

Trap five

P/E ignores how much debt there is

Two companies each earn ₹50 crore for their owners and are valued at ₹1,000 crore: both P/E 20 example. One has no debt; the other owes lenders ₹400 crore, who are paid interest first. Buying the whole business would cost ₹1,400 crore, and the borrower’s profit is more exposed to a bad year or a rate rise. P/E sees only the owners’ slice. Part 11 covers debt; Part 14 brings EV/EBITDA, which prices the debt in.

Now find it in a real report

Tata Consumer’s EPS: 15.59 and 17.30

TCPL’s consolidated profit for the owners was ₹1,542.30 crore. Divided by 98.95 crore shares (the year’s weighted average) that is a basic EPS of ₹15.59; the diluted figure is ₹15.58 public. TCPL also quotes an adjusted EPS of ₹17.30, which adds back the yearly amortisation of intangibles from buying Capital Foods and Organic India. Part 6 covers why companies adjust. The gap, ₹1.71, is invisible in the first two bars: they differ by one paisa.

  • Where the 98.95 crore comes from. Note 30: weighted average shares 98,95,35,734; with performance share units 99,01,53,214 for diluted. The year-end count is 98,95,61,780 (Note 14), a touch higher because 63,222 shares were issued on option exercise. Price ÷ EPS and market cap ÷ profit therefore differ slightly (about 0.02%), as EPS is also rounded to the paisa.
  • Basic is audited, adjusted is not. ₹17.30 is from the 10 June 2026 AGM presentation, adjusted for amortisation of Capital Foods and Organic India; the report prints no bridge. By our arithmetic the add-back is about ₹169 crore (₹1.71 × 98.95 crore shares). The presentation also gives EPS before exceptional items, ₹15.64 and ₹17.35.
  • A misprint to know about. The management discussion says the previous year’s EPS was ₹13.60. The audited statement and Note 30 say ₹13.06. When prose and statements differ, trust the statements.
  • This is trailing FY26 EPS. Today’s trailing P/E uses the latest four quarters (Part 2’s TTM), which are newer than FY26. A dated example: on 6 November 2026 TCPL closed at ₹1,086.30 on NSE public. On FY26 basic EPS that is a P/E of 69.7 (62.8 on adjusted EPS), an earnings yield of 1.4%, and a market cap of about ₹107,496 crore (₹1.07 lakh crore) on 98.96 crore shares. Ramu’s is 20. A P/E that high is the market paying for growth and quality it expects; Part 9 and Part 15 cover when that is worth it. So what

Three questions before trusting a P/E

  1. Which profit is it? Reported, adjusted, trailing or forward, and does it contain a one-off?
  2. Is this profit normal? A peak, a trough, a loss or a rounding error breaks the ratio.
  3. What is the P/E not counting? Debt, growth and how long the profit will last.

P/E says what the owners’ slice of profit costs. It does not say how much of each rupee of sales turns into that profit, or why it changes. Part 8, “Margins and operating leverage”, answers that: why a 10% rise in sales can lift profit by far more.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
P/E is the price counted in years of one year’s profit per share, and it is only as honest as that profit: one-offs, peaks, losses, growth and debt all change what the number means.
