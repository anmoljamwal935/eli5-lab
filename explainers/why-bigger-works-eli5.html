Scale: why bigger works

Every few months a bigger language model arrives, and it is usually better. That is not luck. Researchers found that a model’s loss falls along smooth, predictable curves as you add three things: learned numbers, text to read, and the arithmetic to train on it. This part shows those curves, the rule for splitting a budget between them, and what the curves do not promise.

Built from the original papers (Kaplan and others 2020; Hoffmann and others 2022, the Chinchilla paper), the 2024 replication by Besiroglu and others, Meta’s Llama 3 report, model card and launch post, and papers on data supply (Villalobos and others; Muennighoff and others) and emergence (Wei and others; Schaeffer and others), facts checked against them · Read the source ↗

The problem

Three ingredients, one bill

A training run buys three things. Parameters: the learned numbers (Part 6 called them knobs; Part 9 shows one full pass through them). Data: the tokens of text the model reads. Compute: the sums it takes, about 6 × parameters × tokens public. So the budget is the area of a rectangle, and the same area comes in many shapes.

Here are three real models with roughly the same budget, about 5 to 7 × 10²³ sums. Gopher (DeepMind, 2021) is wide and flat: huge, but it read little. Chinchilla (2022) is a quarter of the size and read almost five times as much. Llama 3 8B (Meta, 2024) is tiny and read 15 trillion tokens public. Which shape is best, and how much better does a bigger rectangle get? That is what scaling laws answer.

Go deeper: where “6” comes from

Part 6, chapter 10: one guess costs about 2 sums per learned number per token, and working out the slopes costs about twice that again, so training costs about 6. Kaplan and others use exactly this estimate, C ≈ 6 × N × D, and so does the Chinchilla paper public (Kaplan 2020, section 2.1). It ignores some smaller costs, such as the attention scores of Part 7, which is why papers sometimes quote slightly different totals for the same model.

The measuring stick

Lower loss means better guesses

To say “better”, the papers use one number: the loss on text the model has never seen, the average penalty from Part 2 for the real next token. Turn it back into a chance and it says how much the model typically bets on the right answer:

Going from loss 3 to loss 2 takes the typical bet from 1 in 20 to about 1 in 7.4 public (plain arithmetic: the chance is e to the power of minus the loss). Small changes in loss are big changes in how often the model is right, so the curves below are worth reading closely. Loss values only compare fairly on the same text with the same tokenizer.

The discovery

Plot it plainly: a steep drop, then flat

In 2020 Kaplan and others at OpenAI trained many models, from 768 to 1.5 billion learned numbers, and measured the loss of each public. Drawn on ordinary axes, their fitted curve looks disappointing: a cliff on the far left, then almost nothing.

That picture misleads, because ordinary axes give equal space to equal amounts. The jump from 1 thousand to 1 million parameters is squeezed into a sliver, and so is the jump from 1 million to 100 million. The fix is to give equal space to equal multiples.

The trick

On log–log paper, a straight line

Redraw it so that each step to the right is 10× more parameters and each step down is the same fraction off the loss. The cliff and the flat part become one straight line:

A straight line on these “log–log” axes is called a power law. Here: every 10× more parameters multiplies the loss by about 0.84, and every doubling by about 0.95 public (Kaplan 2020, equation 1.1). The surprise was how straight the lines were, for parameters, data and compute alike, over six or seven factors of ten, and how little else mattered: a deeper or wider model of the same size scored about the same.

Go deeper: the three fitted lines

Kaplan’s fits: loss = (8.8 × 10¹³ ÷ N)0.076 for parameters N, (5.4 × 10¹³ ÷ D)0.095 for tokens D, and a third for compute, each when the other two are not holding it back public. The constants depend on the training text and tokenizer and “do not have a fundamental meaning”, in the paper’s words. Only the straightness and the slopes travel.

Diminishing returns

Every 10× buys a smaller step

A power law has a sting. Each 10× more budget removes the same fraction of what is left, so the absolute gain shrinks while the bill grows tenfold. Using the Chinchilla data, here is the best loss each budget can buy:

The orange part shrinks by about a third with every bar public (from the 2024 refit of the Chinchilla data, below). So “bigger works” is true, but each step of improvement costs ten times the last. That is why training budgets rose from about 3 × 10²³ sums for GPT-3 in 2020 to about 4 × 10²⁵ for Llama 3 405B in 2024 public (6 × parameters × tokens).

The floor

Some next words cannot be predicted

The grey part of each bar never shrinks. Text itself is partly unpredictable: no model, however big, can know which colour a stranger will name. Even a perfect guesser must spread its bets, and every spread bet costs some penalty:

The Chinchilla paper writes the loss as three parts: a floor it calls the “entropy of natural text”, a part that shrinks with more parameters, and a part that shrinks with more data public. Its fitted floor is 1.69; the 2024 refit puts it at 1.82 public. Either way, scale can only remove what sits above the floor. (These chances are example.)

Go deeper: the formula loss = floor + A ÷ parameters^α + B ÷ tokens^β

Chinchilla’s printed fit: floor 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28 (Hoffmann 2022, equation 10). The refit by Besiroglu and others: 1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658 (2024, equation 3) public. Chapter 11 lets you try both.

The bottleneck

Starve one ingredient, hit a ceiling

The straight lines only hold while the other ingredients keep up. Fix the amount of text and keep adding parameters: each curve falls, then flattens at its own level, because the model has nothing new to learn from public.

Kaplan’s paper says it plainly: performance improves predictably “as long as we scale up N and D in tandem”, and hits diminishing returns if either is held fixed public. The same is true the other way: a small model fed endless text also levels off. Growth needs all three ingredients together, which raises the real question: in what proportion?

The wrong intuition

2020’s answer: mostly grow the model

Kaplan’s fits said that with 10× more compute you should make the model about 5.5× bigger but feed it only 1.8× more text public (as summarised in the Chinchilla paper). The industry followed. Most large models of 2020–21 read about 300 billion tokens, however big they were:

GPT-3 had 175 billion learned numbers and read 300 billion tokens: under 2 tokens per parameter public. The intuition was natural, since bigger models do learn more from each token they read. But it was wrong about the best trade, and the next chapter shows by how much.

The correction

Chinchilla: a quarter the size, more text

In 2022 Hoffmann and others at DeepMind trained over 400 models, from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to 500 billion tokens, and found that parameters and tokens should grow equally: double one, double the other public. To test it they built Chinchilla with Gopher’s budget:

Chinchilla, 4× smaller and trained on more than 4× the text, beat Gopher across a wide range of tests, including the MMLU exam, 67.5% against 60.0% public (Hoffmann 2022, table 6). It was also 4× cheaper to run afterwards, because every answer costs about 2 sums per parameter per token.

Go deeper: why the first answer was off

The Chinchilla paper gives two reasons public. Kaplan’s runs used one fixed learning-rate schedule and token count for every model, so a model stopped early was judged with a schedule meant for longer training, which made small amounts of data look worse than they are. And most of Kaplan’s models were small, many under 100 million parameters, while the trend curves slightly at larger sizes.

The valley

A fixed budget makes a valley

Fix the budget (the rectangle’s area) and slide its shape. Too few parameters and the model is too small to use all that text; too many and each one is starved of data. Plot the loss at every split and you get a valley, one per budget, with a best point at the bottom:

The Chinchilla paper calls these IsoFLOP curves: same sums, different splits public. As the budget grows, the bottom moves right (bigger best model) and down (lower loss). The purple line through the bottoms is the compute-optimal path. Notice also how flat each valley is near its bottom. Meta saw the same in its own runs for Llama 3: the curves get flatter as budgets grow public.

Go deeper: two fits, one data set

The Chinchilla paper printed rounded constants for its formula (chapter 6). In 2024 Besiroglu and others rebuilt the data from the paper’s chart and refit it. They found the printed fit fits the data poorly and, by their reading, implies about 70 tokens per parameter (the rounded printed constants push it higher still), out of line with the paper’s other two methods and with how Chinchilla itself was trained. The authors traced it to their fitting software stopping early. The refit gives about 20 public (Besiroglu 2024). Switch fits in the lab: the best point jumps, but the losses barely change, because the valley is so flat.

The rule of thumb

About 20 tokens for every parameter

The Chinchilla paper’s table of compute-optimal models works out to about 20 training tokens for every parameter, at every size public:

Because both grow equally, 100× more budget buys a model 10× bigger trained on 10× more text. The rule says a trillion-parameter model needs 21 trillion tokens and about 10²⁶ sums before it is the right model to train (Hoffmann 2022, table 3). Twenty is a rule of thumb, not a constant: the refit’s own best ratio drifts from about 23 down to 16 across the lab’s budgets, and a 2024 study cited by the replication found it depends on data quality public.

The twist

Overtrain small models on purpose

Compute-optimal answers one question: the lowest loss for a given training bill. But training happens once, and the model then writes billions of tokens, each costing about 2 sums per parameter. A smaller model trained far past 20 tokens per parameter costs a little loss and saves a lot on every answer:

Llama 3 8B read over 15 trillion tokens public (model card), about 1,900 per parameter. Meta wrote that the Chinchilla-optimal amount for an 8B model is about 200 billion tokens, yet its 8B and 70B models “continued to improve log-linearly” up to 15 trillion, and that smaller models are preferred because they are much cheaper to run public (Meta, 2024). Its 405B flagship, by contrast, was sized to be roughly compute-optimal public (Llama 3 report, section 3.2.1).

The data question

Is there enough text left?

If parameters and tokens grow together, text has to keep growing too. Here is what the best split would need at bigger budgets, against an estimate of all the usable public text on the indexed web:

Villalobos and others estimate that stock at about 4 × 10¹⁴ tokens, and project that, if trends continue, training runs will use all of it sometime between 2026 and 2032, most likely around 2028 public (Villalobos 2024). That is an estimate with wide error bars, not a measurement. The gold bars for bigger budgets come from the refit public (arithmetic).

Reading twice

Repeating text works, up to a point

One way round a shortage is to read the same text more than once. Muennighoff and others trained 400 models to measure what a repeated token is worth public:

Up to about 4 passes, repeated text was nearly as good as new: an 8.7-billion-parameter model trained 4 times over its data ended only 0.5% worse than one that saw every token once public (Muennighoff 2023). By 16 passes each extra pass is worth much less, and the value of more compute “eventually decays to zero”. Other routes, such as generated text and mixing in code, are being studied and are not settled.

What it doesn’t promise

Loss is predictable; skills are not

Scaling laws predict one number: the loss. Nobody grades a model by its loss. People care whether it can add, translate or reason, and those scores do not have to follow a smooth curve:

Wei and others listed tasks where models scored near chance across many budgets and then jumped, such as an arithmetic test (3-digit addition and subtraction, 2-digit multiplication), where GPT-3 models scored near zero until about 2 × 10²² training sums (13 billion parameters) public (Wei 2022). They called these emergent abilities: not predictable by extrapolating smaller models. Meta’s Llama 3 report makes the same point: scaling laws “typically predict only next-token prediction loss rather than specific benchmark performance”, so Meta built an extra step to link the two public. (The lower curve’s shape is example.)

The debate

A sudden jump, or the ruler?

In 2023 Schaeffer and others argued that many jumps come from how answers are marked. Exact-match marking scores an answer right only if every token is right. If each token gets steadily more likely, a five-token answer’s chance of being all right is that chance multiplied five times, which hugs zero and then shoots up:

Mark the same answers by how many tokens are right, and many “emergent” curves turn smooth again public (Schaeffer 2023). Both sides have a point. The smooth view says the progress underneath was predictable. The emergence view says that what users actually experience, a working answer or not, can still flip quickly and is hard to forecast from small models. The question is open.

The common mistake

Fits to experiments, not laws of nature

“Scaling law” sounds like physics. It is a curve fitted to a set of training runs, for one kind of model, one data mix and one tokenizer, and then stretched far beyond the runs that made it:

Chinchilla’s valley runs went up to 3 × 10²¹ sums, and Llama 3’s up to 10²² public. That the predictions held at those distances is remarkable, but it is evidence, not a guarantee. Kaplan’s own paper predicted its trends must break somewhere, since loss cannot fall below the floor. And the same data gave two noticeably different fits (chapter 11).

Whether today’s largest models still follow these curves is mostly not public: companies including Anthropic have not published the sizes, token counts or budgets of their latest models.

The hand-off

Next: one budget, thousands of chips

Llama 3 405B’s budget was about 3.8 × 10²⁵ sums public. Meta’s GPUs each sustained about 400 trillion sums a second during that run public (Llama 3 report, table 4). One GPU alone would need about 3,000 years:

Split over 16,384 GPUs it is about 67 days (plain arithmetic), and Meta logged 466 interruptions in one 54-day stretch public. Bigger works only if thousands of chips can act as one machine. Part 10 covers the chips themselves; Part 12, “Many chips as one”, shows how thousands of chips share one training run.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Bigger works because loss falls on smooth, predictable curves, but only if parameters and data grow together, each step costs ten times the last, and the curves predict loss, not skills.
