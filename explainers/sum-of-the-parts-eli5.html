Conglomerates and special cases

One multiple fits a company that does one thing. A group of different businesses is valued piece by piece and added up; a holding company often trades below what it owns; and two special tools, dividend models and net asset value, fit steady payers and asset-heavy companies.

The problem

One multiple for three businesses

At ₹100 a share the market values all of Ramu at 12.5 times EBITDA (EV 1,060 ÷ EBITDA 85, Part 14). But Ramu is three businesses with different economics example: packaged tea through shops keeps 20% of its sales as EBITDA, the cafés 12% and offices and institutions 13.3%.

A buyer would pay a different multiple for each: more for a branded, growing tea business, less for rented cafés or contract supply to offices. One blended multiple hides that mix, and it moves whenever the mix changes, even if no business changes.

Sum of the parts

Value each business, then add

A sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation gives each business its own multiple, usually taken from listed companies that do only that thing (Part 26, “Relative valuation and P/E bands”, covers choosing peers). Ramu’s examples example: packaged tea at 14 times EBITDA, cafés at 10 times, offices at 8 times.

Segment value = segment EBITDA × its EV/EBITDA multiple: 60 × 14 + 15 × 10 + 10 × 8 = 1,070

Packaged tea is 71% of the EBITDA but 79% of the value, because it earns the highest multiple. The blended multiple comes out at 1,070 ÷ 85 = 12.6 times, close to the market’s 12.5, so here the market and the parts roughly agree. They need not.

From the parts to a share

Subtract the debt, divide by shares

Each segment value is an enterprise value: what the business is worth to everyone who funds it. The debt belongs to the whole group, not to any one business, so take it off once at the end. Ramu’s net debt is 60 (borrowings 100 less cash 40) example.

EV 1,070 − net debt 60 = equity 1,010 → ÷ 10 crore shares = ₹101 a share, against a price of ₹100

At ₹101 against ₹100, the parts say the price is about right. An SOTP is most useful when the gap is wide, and when something could close it: a demerger, like Part 21’s spin-off of the cafés into “Ramu Cafés Ltd”, lets the market price each piece on its own.

Go deeper: what an SOTP needs, and where it goes wrong

The company must report each business separately. Listed companies publish segment results in their accounts (Ind AS 108); TCPL’s four segments, India Beverages, India Foods, International and Non-branded, are in Part 16. Common traps: head-office costs that belong to no segment (value them too, as a negative part, or they vanish); a loss-making segment, which a multiple of a negative EBITDA cannot value; a stake in a listed subsidiary that the company owns only partly (count only its share); and choosing each peer multiple to suit the answer.

Holding companies

Why holding companies trade below their stakes

Suppose the Ramu family holds its 5.5 crore Ramu shares through a listed company, “Ramu Family Holdings” example. At ₹100 the stake is worth ₹550 cr, and it owns nothing else. Yet its own shares trade at a market cap of ₹385 cr: 30% less than the one thing it owns.

Holding-company discount = 1 − market cap ÷ value of holdings = 1 − 385 ÷ 550 = 30%
  1. You cannot get at the stake. The holding company has no plan to sell or hand out its Ramu shares, so its owners get only the dividends it passes on.
  2. Tax if it ever sells. Selling would mean capital gains tax on the rise since it bought, so the stake is worth less to its owners than its price.
  3. Running costs and a second layer of control. Salaries and fees come off before anything reaches the owners, and the family controls both layers.
  4. Fewer buyers. Holding-company shares often trade thinly, and many funds would rather own Ramu directly.

A discount is normal, not a mistake to be corrected. It can narrow if the holding company pays out more, buys back its own shares or hands out its stakes, and it can widen. Buying a holding company “for 70 paise on the rupee” works only if something changes.

Net asset value

NAV: assets at today’s prices

For a company whose value lies in what it owns, the method is net asset value (NAV): every asset at today’s market value, less everything owed. Book value (Part 14) often carries assets at what they cost. Say Ramu Family Holdings got its shares long ago at their ₹10 face value example: book ₹55 cr, NAV ₹550 cr.

NAV fits holding and investment companies, property companies whose land was bought years ago, and other asset-heavy businesses where the assets could be sold one by one. The question becomes the price against NAV: here 0.7 times. NAV says what the assets are worth, not what they earn, so it suits companies the market values for their assets more than their profit.

Dividend discount model

Value only the dividends

For a steady payer, value only what owners receive: every future dividend, discounted at the cost of equity, the return owners require (12% for Ramu, from Part 23). If the dividend grows at the same rate for ever, the endless sum has a short formula. Ramu paid ₹2.00 for FY26 example; at 8% growth the next is ₹2.16.

Value = next year’s dividend ÷ (cost of equity − growth) = 2.00 × 1.08 ÷ (12% − 8%) = 2.16 ÷ 0.04 = ₹54

Each later dividend is bigger, but worth a little less today, because 12% discounting outpaces 8% growth. The first 20 years give only ₹27.91; the other half of the ₹54 comes after year 20. At 10% growth the same formula gives 2.20 ÷ 0.02 = ₹110.

Where the formula breaks

When growth meets the discount rate

The divisor is the gap between the cost of equity and growth. As growth climbs towards 12% the gap shrinks to nothing and the value shoots up example. Ramu’s sustainable growth from Part 14, ROE 20% × retention 60%, is exactly 12%: the formula would divide by zero.

That is not an infinitely valuable company. It is a broken assumption. No company can grow 12% a year for ever: the economy grows more slowly, so the company would end up bigger than the economy. Ramu can grow 12% for some years; the rate it holds for ever must sit well below its 12% cost of equity. The fix is the one Part 24 used: a few years of fast growth, then a lower rate for ever.

A dividend model values only the cash paid out. Ramu keeps 60% of its profit and earns 20% on it, so a model that holds the payout at 40% misses value; that is one reason ₹54 sits so far below Part 24’s ₹76.7.

Go deeper: growth and payout are linked

Growth comes from the profit kept back: growth = ROE × retention (Part 14). If Ramu’s growth slows to 8% while it still earns 20% on new money, it needs to keep only 8% ÷ 20% = 40% of its profit and could pay out 60%, not 40%. A careful dividend model raises the payout as growth slows. Above 12% the formula gives a negative number, which means nothing at all.

So what

Questions for a group or a special case

  1. One business or several? If the segments differ, value them separately, and write down whose multiple you used for each.
  2. What sits outside the segments? Head-office costs, debt, minority stakes and listed subsidiaries owned only in part.
  3. Is there a holding-company layer? Measure the discount to NAV, compare it with its own history, and ask what could change it.
  4. Does a special method fit? Dividends for steady payers, NAV for asset-heavy companies, and growth always below the discount rate.

Every method on this page gives one number from one set of guesses: ₹101 from the parts, ₹54 from dividends, ₹76.7 from Part 24’s DCF. Part 28, “Margin of safety and scenarios”, turns one number into bull, base and bear cases and asks how wrong you can afford to be.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Value a group piece by piece and take the debt off once; expect a holding company to trade below what it owns; and match the method to the company: dividends for steady payers, NAV for asset-heavy ones, with growth always below the discount rate.
