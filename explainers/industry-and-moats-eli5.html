Industry structure and moats

A good year proves little. What matters is whether a company can keep earning well while rivals try to take its profit. This part looks at the industry around a company, five pressures on its profit, and the walls, called moats, that hold them off.

The industry first

Size, growth and share

A company sells into an industry. Three numbers describe it. Market size is the total sales of everyone in it. Market growth is how fast that total rises. Market share is one company’s sales as a slice of the total. Ramu sells packaged tea in a regional market of ₹12,500 cr, growing 8% a year; its ₹300 cr of packaged tea is a 2.4% share example. We take Ramu to be outside the top three, a one-off assumption.

Growth is a tailwind, not a moat. In ten years an 8% market is 2.2 times bigger, and a company that merely keeps its 2.4% share grows to ₹648 cr. Every rival gets the same tailwind. What a company keeps of it, and whether it earns more than its capital costs (Part 9), depends on the rest of this part.

Who holds the market

Concentrated or fragmented

Concentration is how much the top few sellers hold. In Ramu’s market the top three hold 55% example. A fragmented industry has many small sellers and none sets the pace; a consolidated one has a few big ones. Neither is good or bad on its own. Few big sellers can keep prices steady, or fight each other hard. Many small ones usually cannot hold a price, because the customer always has a cheaper choice nearby.

Why do industries consolidate? general India’s mobile telecom is the textbook case: building networks and buying spectrum cost a great deal before the first customer pays, price wars thinned revenue per user, and weaker operators merged or left, so a market of many became a market of a few. Cement is another: plants are costly, the product is heavy and expensive to move, so firms grew plant by plant and by buying one another, region by region. For real shares, use a sector regulator’s data or the companies’ own filings; this series quotes none.

Go deeper: one number for concentration

Analysts also use the Herfindahl index: square each firm’s share in percent and add them up. Twenty firms at 5% give 20 × 25 = 500. One firm at 100% gives 10,000. The higher the number, the more concentrated the industry. It needs every firm’s share, which is why “top three” is the quicker first look.

Five pressures on profit

Porter’s five forces

The strategist Michael Porter listed five forces that decide how much of an industry’s profit its sellers keep. Each can be weak or strong. The more of them are strong, the thinner the margin left for the company. The drawing shows all five pressing on the same profit. Next to it, each force in one line, with a tea example example.

The wall

A moat keeps rivals out

A moat is whatever stops rivals taking a company’s profit, the way water stops an army reaching a castle. It matters because of a plain fact: high profit attracts competitors. Without a moat, a rival copies the product or undercuts the price, and profit slides back to what the business costs to run. With one, the profit above that level can last. Moats are not a feature of every business, and they are not permanent. The next panel explains each wall in tea terms, and says where tea is weak.

How a moat shows up · 1

Return above the cost of capital

The first fingerprint is a high, stable ROCE (Part 10). Capital has a price: owners and lenders want a return. This part assumes 12% as the cost of capital, an illustrative figure; Part 23 shows how to estimate one. At ROCE 20.9% on ₹350 cr of capital, Ramu’s EBIT is ₹73 cr example. The capital charge, what the capital must earn to break even, is 12% × 350 = ₹42 cr. The ₹31 cr left is profit above that charge, which is exactly what rivals want to compete away.

capital charge = 12% × 350 = 42 · excess = 73 − 42 = 31

Stable matters as much as high. A company that earns 35% one year and 8% the next may not have a wall, just luck and bad luck. Both companies below average 21% example. Only one gives a reader much reason to expect next year.

How a moat shows up · 2

Steady gross margins, sticky prices

The second fingerprint is a steady gross margin (Part 8), and the sharpest test of it is an input-cost spike. Suppose tea, ₹0.90 of Ramu’s ₹5.00 cup, costs 50% more: ₹0.45 more a cup example. A firm with pricing power raises its price to ₹5.45 and keeps ₹2.50 of gross profit a cup. A firm with weak power gets only ₹5.10. One with none absorbs it all. The same shock, three different margins. This is a one-off scenario and does not change Ramu’s accounts.

Look at the percentage too: even full pass-through lowers the gross margin from 50.0% to 45.9%, because the price rose by the same rupees as the cost. A moat shows up in rupees of gross profit per unit holding steady, more than in the percentage staying put. And a margin that does not move may also mean no cost spike happened. Read the margin through a year when costs did move.

How moats wear away

Channels, technology, rules

Walls erode. Three common ways, with tea examples example. A new channel: quick-commerce apps let a new brand reach homes without a distributor network, so the reach wall shrinks. New technology: an app that lets cafés order directly from estates cuts out the middlemen a scale advantage relied on. Regulation: a rule change removes a licence barrier or caps a price. The curve applies the lab’s formula (below) to Ramu’s 20.9% ROCE, with moat strength stepping down at each event.

Notice the shape. ROCE does not collapse at once; it slides toward the cost of capital while the walls are down. By the end, much of the extra profit has gone to customers and rivals. That is why analysts ask not only “is there a moat?” but “which wall is it, and what could take it down?”

Modelling assumptions, all one-off teaching choices, not forecasts. (1) Each year of the price war a share e of Ramu’s customers, and equally of its profit above the 12% charge, goes to the rival: e = (1 − moat) × min(1, 2 × price cut) × (1 − 0.15 × extra ads in %). So a 10% cut moves 20% of a no-moat firm’s customers a year. (2) Each 1% of sales spent on extra advertising removes 15% of that erosion and costs 1% × ₹500 cr of EBIT every war year. (3) Customers lost do not come back when the war ends. (4) Capital stays ₹350 cr and no other cost changes. (5) ROCE = 12% + what is left of the 8.9-point excess − ad cost. (6) The market grows 8% a year from ₹12,500 cr; share is Ramu’s 2.4% times what is left. (7) The “without a moat” line is the same model with moat strength 0. TCPL: two ROCEs, no verdict

On the series definition, TCPL’s ROCE is EBIT ₹2,165.12 cr ÷ (equity ₹23,188.86 cr + borrowings ₹2,120.31 cr) = 8.55% in FY26; FY25 is 1,878.61 ÷ 23,238.95 = 8.08% public. TCPL’s own table prints a very different “Return on Capital Employed (RoCE) 41.87%”, up from 37.54% (p. 224). Its footnote defines capital employed as “Tangible Net Worth (including Non-current Investments) + Total Debt + Deferred Tax Liabilities + Purchase commitments for Non-Controlling Interests’ shares” (p. 54). Starting from tangible net worth leaves out the goodwill and brands its acquisitions put on the balance sheet (TCPL’s own table on the same page shows goodwill of ₹11,884 cr and indefinite-life brands of ₹2,093 cr). Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions.

TCPL’s own five-year RoCE, as printed on p. 54, was 33%, 34%, 43%, 38% and 42% from FY22 to FY26: high on its definition, with a range of ten points.

The question, with no verdict. What in these numbers looks like a moat? The brand claims, the reach figures, and a gross margin that moved by 1.28 points in a year when the report describes tea and coffee costs moving. What would test it? Whether the 8.55% series ROCE rises as acquired businesses mature; whether gross margin holds when tea or coffee costs next spike; whether “largest” and “2nd largest” hold on a stated basis; whether advertising must rise to defend the position; and how quick commerce changes who controls the shelf.

So what

Five questions about any moat

  1. How is the industry built? Size, growth, and how much the top few hold. Fragmented rarely holds price; consolidated can.
  2. Which of the five forces is strong? One strong force can eat the margin of an otherwise good business.
  3. Which wall is it? Brand, switching costs, network, scale, reach or licence. If you cannot name it, be careful calling it a moat.
  4. Does it show in the numbers? ROCE above the cost of capital and steady, gross profit per unit holding through a cost spike.
  5. What could take it down? A new channel, new technology, a rule change. Ask before it happens, not after.

A moat only helps if the people running the company protect it and spend the cash well. Part 18, “Management, capital allocation and concalls”, asks what managers do with the cash a moat produces, and whether they keep their promises.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A moat is whatever lets a company keep earning more than its cost of capital while rivals try to take it: look for high, steady returns and gross profit that holds when costs jump, name the wall, and ask what could take it down.
