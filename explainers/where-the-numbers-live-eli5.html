Where the numbers live

A company’s numbers are spread over an annual report, exchange filings and management’s own slides, and they are not all checked equally. Learn where each one sits, how the parent’s books differ from the group’s, and how to read a quarter without being fooled by the season.

Built from Tata Consumer Products’ FY2026 integrated annual report and its Q4 FY26 results filing, the SEBI LODR Regulations and SEBI’s FAQs; facts checked against them · Read the source ↗

Part 1 gave you the map: a share is a slice of a business, and price is not the same as value. This part is about where the evidence for value sits. Facts carry a tag: public means a filed or published figure, or plain arithmetic on one; example means a made-up number from Ramu Chai Ltd, our fictional tea company, used to show how something works. The problem

Not every number is checked equally

A company speaks in four voices. Only the annual statements get a full audit. Quarterly results get a lighter limited review. The investor presentation and the concall (the call where analysts question management) are management’s own words, with no audit at all.

Go deeper: a sensible reading order

Start at the auditor’s report: is the opinion clean, and what did the auditor lose sleep over (the “key audit matters”)? Then the statements (Parts 3 to 5 teach each one), then the notes for what is inside the big numbers. Read the MD&A and Board’s report after that, as management’s account of the same year. The auditor reads them too, but their opinion does not cover them.

Exchange filings

What gets filed, and when

Listed companies file with BSE and NSE under SEBI’s LODR Regulations, and the exchanges publish every filing free. The shareholding pattern is due within 21 days of each quarter end. Quarterly results are due within 45 days. The year’s audited results are due within 60 days, together with the last quarter public.

TCPL’s board approved its FY26 results on 8 May 2026, 38 days after the year ended, inside the 60-day window public. Anything that could move the price, such as an acquisition, a rating change or a new auditor, must also be filed as it happens (Regulation 30).

The deadlines above are from the regulations as SEBI notified them in 2015; SEBI’s April 2025 FAQ confirms the 60-day annual rule and that the last quarter must be audited or limited-reviewed. SEBI amends the LODR often, so check the current text before relying on a deadline.

Where to find them: the company’s Corporate announcements on bseindia.com, Corporate filings on nseindia.com, or the investor page of the company’s own site, which usually carries the same files. Prefer these to a data website: a filing is the company’s own signed statement.

The building block

Two sets of books: parent and group

Standalone accounts cover only the listed company itself. Consolidated accounts cover the company plus every subsidiary it controls, added together as if one business, with trade and dividends between them cancelled. Ramu Chai has no subsidiaries, so this toy uses a made-up parent and subsidiary example.

The subsidiary’s ₹60 of sales joins the group, so consolidated revenue is higher. The ₹4 dividend it paid up is income in the parent’s own books, but inside the group it is only money moving between pockets, so it is counted once. The gap can go either way: in this toy consolidated profit is higher, and in TCPL’s real accounts below it is lower. Investors value the whole group, so they usually start from consolidated.

Go deeper: subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures

A subsidiary is controlled (usually over half the votes), so all of its sales and costs are added in. An associate or joint venture is one where the company has influence but not control. Its sales are not added; the company shows only its share of the profit or loss, on a single line. Where the company owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the profit that belongs to the other owners is split out as non-controlling interest. Part 4 covers it.

Quarterly results

A quarter is a slice of the year

Indian companies run April to March. Q1 is April to June, Q2 July to September, Q3 October to December, Q4 January to March. Ramu Chai’s FY26 revenue of ₹500 cr is four slices example. The fourth is special: the audited year minus the nine months already published.

YoY and QoQ

Compare with last year, or last quarter

YoY (year on year) compares a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier. QoQ (quarter on quarter) compares it with the quarter just before. Ramu’s Q4 FY26 sales of ₹125 cr are 11.6% above Q4 FY25 and 10.7% below Q3 FY26, one quarter and two opposite verdicts example.

Seasonality

Why QoQ misleads a seasonal business

Tea sells most in the winter and festive months, so Q3 (October to December) is Ramu’s peak every year example. Q4 then falls back from it. QoQ swings from +16.7% to −10.7% while FY26’s quarters each grew 9.5% to 12.0% on a year ago. YoY compares like with like, so the season cancels.

Trailing twelve months

TTM: the latest four quarters, added

TTM (trailing twelve months) adds the latest four quarters, so it always holds one of each season. It rolls forward every quarter. At a fiscal year end it equals the annual figure: Ramu’s TTM at March 2026 is ₹500 cr, the same as FY26 example. It moves smoothly but is slow to show a turn.

Now find it in a real report

TCPL: the parent against the whole group

TCPL owns large overseas and acquired businesses, so its standalone and consolidated accounts differ a lot. Both come from the audited FY26 statements, in ₹ crore public.

  • Revenue. Standalone ₹14,700.05 cr (p.345) is 72% of consolidated ₹20,290.43 cr (p.415). The ₹20,290 cr in TCPL’s AGM presentation is the consolidated figure.
  • Profit. Standalone ₹1,635.15 cr is 5.7% higher than consolidated ₹1,546.80 cr. Standalone other income includes ₹463.73 cr of dividends from subsidiaries and associates (Note 21, p.379), which cancel on consolidation. The group also carries ₹626.68 cr of depreciation and amortisation against ₹229.14 cr standalone, and a ₹90.70 cr share of losses in associates and joint ventures.
  • Per share. Basic EPS is ₹16.52 standalone and ₹15.59 consolidated. The AGM presentation’s ₹15.59 is the consolidated one.
  • Balance sheet. The standalone sheet holds the subsidiaries as a single line, ₹9,710.29 cr of non-current investments at cost (p.344; Note 6 lists each, such as ₹3,881.25 cr for Capital Foods). The consolidated sheet replaces that with the subsidiaries’ own assets, so goodwill is ₹11,883.90 cr instead of ₹3,859.95 cr and total assets ₹34,452.96 cr instead of ₹22,629.90 cr (p.414).

Now find it in a real report

TCPL’s quarters, from the results filing

The Q4 FY26 results filing (signed 8 May 2026) gives consolidated revenue from operations of ₹5,433.62 cr for Q4 FY26, ₹5,112.00 cr for Q3 FY26 and ₹4,608.22 cr for Q4 FY25 public. Group profit after tax was ₹424.02 cr, ₹384.52 cr and ₹348.72 cr: up 21.6% YoY and 10.3% QoQ.

  • Audited or not. The filing’s year columns are headed “Audited” and the December quarter “Unaudited”. The auditor’s report says the March quarter is “the balancing figure between audited figures in respect of the full financial year and the published year to date figures up to the third quarter”, which were only limited-reviewed.
  • TTM. At 31 March 2026 the trailing twelve months equal the year: ₹20,290.43 cr, up 15.2% on FY25’s ₹17,618.30 cr (p.415).
  • Season. TCPL’s Q4 beat its Q3 here, the opposite of Ramu. A broad portfolio of tea, salt, foods and overseas sales can wash a season out, and two quarters prove nothing. Look at eight quarters of the company you study.

So what

Three questions before you trust a number

  1. Where is it from, and who checked it? An audited statement, a limited-reviewed quarter, a slide, or something said on a call.
  2. Parent or group? Value the business on consolidated, and use standalone only to see what the listed company itself earns and can pay out.
  3. Compared with what? For a seasonal business, the same quarter last year or the trailing twelve months, never last quarter.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Before you read a number, ask which document it came from, whether anyone audited it, and whether it covers the company alone or the whole group, then compare a quarter with the same quarter last year, not the one before.
