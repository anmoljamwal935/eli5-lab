Margins and operating leverage

A margin is the paise a company keeps from each rupee of sales. Costs that don’t move with sales make profit swing much harder than sales: a 10% rise in sales can mean a 31% rise in operating profit, and a 10% fall can take just as much away.

Paise from each rupee

A margin is what you keep from ₹1

A rupee is 100 paise. Ramu Chai sold ₹500 crore of tea in FY26, so every ₹1 of sales is split like this example. 50 paise buy materials, 12 pay staff, 21 go on rent, ads, power and freight. 17 are left. That 17 then pays for wear and tear, interest and tax, and the last 10 paise is profit. Each margin is one of these leftovers, divided by sales.

Four margins

Four margins, four questions

Each margin stops at a different line of the P&L, so each answers a different question. Ramu’s are 50%, 17%, 14.6% and 10% example. Gross profit is not a reported line (Part 3): analysts compute it as sales minus materials.

Costs that move, costs that stay

Variable and fixed costs

Some costs rise with every cup sold: tea, milk, sugar and packaging, plus freight and distributor commissions. These are variable costs. Ramu’s are ₹2.75 a cup, ₹275 crore on 100 crore cups. Others stay put whatever the sales: staff ₹60 crore, rent, ads and base power ₹80 crore, depreciation ₹12 crore, ₹152 crore in all example. These are fixed costs. Sell 10% more cups and the fixed block does not grow. Only profit does.

Statements list costs by what they are (Part 3), not by how they behave, so nobody prints a “fixed” total. You estimate the split, and real fixed costs creep: wages get raised and a new plant needs new rent.

Contribution

Each cup contributes ₹2.25

A cup sells for ₹5 and costs ₹2.75 to make and deliver, so it leaves ₹2.25: its contribution, 45% of the price example. Contribution first pays the fixed costs, then it is profit. Fixed cash costs of ₹140 crore need 140 ÷ 2.25 = 62.2 crore cups to cover. That is the break-even. The last 37.8 crore cups of the year keep ₹2.25 each, which is Ramu’s ₹85 crore of EBITDA.

Go deeper: break-even after depreciation

EBITDA break-even ignores depreciation. To cover the ₹12 crore of depreciation too, Ramu needs (140 + 12) ÷ 2.25 = 67.6 crore cups, the point where EBIT is zero. That is 32% below this year’s sales.

The lever

Sales up 10%, operating profit up 31%

Sell 110 crore cups instead of 100. Sales rise ₹50 crore. Costs that move rise by ₹27.5 crore. The fixed ₹152 crore doesn’t, so the other ₹22.5 crore falls straight to profit. EBITDA goes from ₹85 crore to ₹107.5 crore (+26%) and EBIT from ₹73 crore to ₹95.5 crore (+31%) example. Each pair below is drawn with its “before” bar at the same length, so the extra length is the percentage growth. This leverage in operating costs is operating leverage.

The other direction

It works just as hard in reverse

The lever does not care which way sales move. At 90 crore cups, sales fall ₹50 crore, costs that move fall ₹27.5 crore, and the whole ₹22.5 crore gap comes out of profit: EBIT drops from ₹73 crore to ₹50.5 crore, −31% example. EBIT is a straight line in cups: ₹2.25 crore for every crore cups. It hits zero at 67.6 crore cups, so a 32% fall in sales wipes out operating profit entirely. Below that, the company loses money while its fixed costs keep running.

One number for the lever

Degree of operating leverage

The degree of operating leverage (DOL) is how many percent EBIT moves for each 1% move in sales. For a change in cups sold it is contribution ÷ EBIT: ₹225 crore ÷ ₹73 crore = 3.1 example, so +10% sales gives about +31% EBIT. The lever is not constant. The nearer a company is to break-even, the longer it is: at 80 crore cups DOL is 6.4, at 120 it is 2.3.

DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT = (sales − variable costs) ÷ EBIT
Go deeper: a price rise is not the same as a volume rise

DOL describes a change in cups sold at an unchanged price. If sales rise 10% because the price goes from ₹5.00 to ₹5.50, nothing extra is spent on materials, so all ₹50 crore falls to profit: EBIT goes from ₹73 crore to ₹123 crore, +68% example. The lab below lets you try it.

Two cost structures

High fixed costs, low fixed costs

A business with heavy fixed costs, such as a plant, a fleet or a big brand budget, has a long lever. One built mostly on variable costs, such as a trader who buys and resells, has a short one. Here is a made-up “low-fixed” tea trader beside Ramu: same ₹500 crore of sales, same ₹73 crore of EBIT, but fixed costs of only ₹52 crore. It is a one-off example and does not change Ramu’s accounts example.

Now move sales 10% either way. Ramu’s EBIT swings ±31%. The low-fixed trader’s swings ±17%. The trader is safer in a bad year and gains less in a good one. Neither is better: the lever cuts both ways, so a high-fixed business is a bet that sales will rise.

Across years and peers

Compare margins like with like

A single margin tells little. Its direction over years tells more. Ramu’s sales grew 11% a year over FY22 to FY26 but profit after tax grew 13.6% a year, so its net margin crept from 9.1% to 10.0% example. That is what you would expect while fixed costs are spread over more cups, though a margin alone can’t prove the cause. A one-point climb looks tiny on a bar and is large in profit.

Now find it in a real report

Tata Consumer’s margins, FY2026 vs FY2025

Margins are not printed on the audited statement. You build them from the consolidated statement of profit and loss (Integrated Annual Report 2025-26, page 415), which shows FY2026 and FY2025 side by side, in ₹ crore public. Gross margin = (revenue − materials consumed − purchases of stock-in-trade − change in inventories) ÷ revenue. In FY26: (20,290.43 − 7,788.16 − 4,108.23 + 40.75) ÷ 20,290.43 = 41.6%. In FY25: (17,618.30 − 6,997.40 − 3,434.37 + 362.49) ÷ 17,618.30 = 42.8%. EBITDA and EBIT here are our sums (Part 3).

  • EBITDA margin: 13.8% computed, 13.9% presented. Revenue less materials, staff and other expenses is ₹2,791.80 crore, 13.8% of ₹20,290.43 crore. The management report and AGM presentation give ₹2,815 crore, 13.9% (page 223; AGM slide 8). The gap is ₹23 crore. FY25 shows the same pattern: 14.1% computed, 14.2% presented (₹2,502 crore).
  • The gap, checked. Neither document prints an EBITDA definition or a bridge, so we tested one by arithmetic. The segment note (Note 38, page 477) says other income of ₹141.76 crore “excludes other income considered as part of segment results”. The statement’s other income is ₹164.75 crore. The difference, ₹22.99 crore, added to our ₹2,791.80 crore gives ₹2,814.79 crore, which rounds to the ₹2,815 crore presented. FY25 ties the same way: 193.25 − 170.17 = 23.08, and 2,479.35 + 23.08 = 2,502.43. So the presented EBITDA appears to count the slice of other income that sits inside segment results. ₹22.99 crore is also the amount of “other non operating income” in Note 22 (page 453). TCPL does not say these are the same item.
  • Net margin 7.6%. Profit for the year ₹1,546.80 crore ÷ ₹20,290.43 crore. FY25 was 7.3% (₹1,287.10 crore), the same as the management report (page 223).

Here is what moved between FY25 and FY26, each margin stopping at a different line. Costs as paise per ₹1 of sales:

  • Gross margin fell 1.3 points as materials took 58.4 paise of each rupee against 57.1. The report gives no consolidated gross-margin bridge. It does say that tea and coffee price swings can affect gross margins (page 212); that price increases taken in the second half of FY25 offset higher input costs (page 223); and that the non-branded business reflected coffee price movements (page 222). For the standalone company only, it credits a 140 basis point EBITDA margin gain to “tapering of tea cost inflation in India” (page 224).
  • Other expenses grew 9.4% while sales grew 15.2%, from 20.7 to 19.6 paise a rupee, which offset most of the gross margin dip: EBITDA margin fell only 0.3 points. Staff costs grew 16.1%, slightly faster than sales, so they behaved less like a fixed cost than Ramu’s.
  • Depreciation and amortisation grew 4.3%, from 3.4 to 3.1 paise a rupee, so EBIT margin was flat at 10.7%. Management’s EBIT grew 15%, the same as sales: the lever was there, but the gross margin dip used it up.
  • Net margin rose 0.3 points even though EBIT margin was flat. Finance costs fell from ₹290.20 crore to ₹137.03 crore. The management report says interest cover is higher because of higher interest in the previous year “on borrowings to fund acquisitions” (page 224). The tax rate rose from 22.3% to 24.6% of pre-tax profit; the report notes that FY25 tax included a one-time credit from merging wholly owned subsidiaries (page 223).
  • Segments moved apart. Segment results as a share of segment sales (Note 38, page 477; a segment profit measure, not EBITDA): India branded 9.1% → 11.8%, international branded 14.7% → 11.9%, non-branded 21.3% → 11.7%. Non-branded sales grew 25% against the group’s 15%. The report says India margin gains were partly offset by international and non-branded declines (page 223).

So what

Three questions for any margin

  1. Which margin am I looking at, and where does it stop? Gross, EBITDA, EBIT and net answer different questions, and companies define EBITDA their own way.
  2. How much of the cost base is fixed? The more fixed, the longer the lever: sales growth lifts profit faster, and a sales dip hurts faster.
  3. How close is the company to break-even? DOL is largest near break-even, where a small sales miss can erase profit.

Margins tell you how much of each rupee is kept, but not how fast the business is growing. Interest is a fixed cost too, a second lever that Part 11 covers. Part 9, “Growth that’s worth paying for”, answers the next question: how fast sales and profit grow, how compounding builds it, and when growth eats cash.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A margin is the paise you keep from each rupee, and fixed costs act as a lever: they make profit move several times as much as sales, in both directions.
