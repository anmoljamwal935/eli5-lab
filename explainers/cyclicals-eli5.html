Cyclicals

Cement, steel and car makers sell things buyers can put off, from plants that cost almost as much to run half empty as full. Their profit swings with the cycle, so judge them per tonne and across a whole cycle, and expect the P/E to point the wrong way.

The problem

Demand grows smoothly, plants arrive in lumps

Buyers can put off a flat, a car or a factory, so demand for cement, steel and cars swings with incomes and interest rates (Part 22). Supply swings too. Take a made-up cement region example: demand grows about 6% a year, but a new plant adds millions of tonnes at once and takes years to build.

When plants run near full, prices firm and every company orders a new plant. They tend to arrive together: here the region jumps from 80 to 100 MT of capacity in Y4, the share of plants in use (capacity utilisation) drops from 90% to 76%, and prices soften until demand catches up. Because a plant costs nearly as much to run half empty as full, profit swings far more than tonnes do (Part 8’s operating leverage).

Cement: the tonne

Price per tonne minus cost per tonne

Cement companies report per tonne. Realisation is the net price received per tonne. Betwa sells 8 MT in a middling year at ₹5,000 a tonne example. Fuel, power, limestone and freight cost ₹3,500 a tonne; staff and upkeep are ₹400 cr whatever it sells, ₹500 a tonne at 8 MT. That leaves EBITDA per tonne of ₹1,000, ₹800 cr in all.

In a bad year (Y3) the price drops 10% and the plants run at 70%: fixed costs are spread over fewer tonnes and EBITDA per tonne falls 57%, to ₹429. In a good year (Y6) price is up 10% at 90% use, and it rises 56%, to ₹1,556. A 10% move in price becomes a 57% move in profit per tonne, and earnings per share move further still once depreciation and interest, which do not shrink, are taken off.

Go deeper: Betwa’s mid-cycle year

Every number is example. Capacity 10 MTPA; sales 8 MT (80% use) at ₹5,000 a tonne; variable costs ₹3,500 a tonne; fixed cash costs ₹400 cr. EBITDA = 8 × (5,000 − 3,500) ÷ 10 − 400 = ₹800 cr (1 MT × ₹1,000 a tonne = ₹100 cr). Depreciation ₹300 cr; net debt ₹1,000 cr at 10%, so interest ₹100 cr; profit before tax ₹400 cr; tax 25%; profit after tax ₹300 cr; 10 crore shares, so EPS ₹30. At 15 times that, the share price is ₹450 and the market cap ₹4,500 cr.

Cement: valuing capacity

EV per tonne of capacity

Profit swings; capacity does not. So cement analysts divide enterprise value (Part 14: market cap plus debt minus cash) by capacity. At ₹450 a share Betwa’s EV is ₹4,500 cr + ₹1,000 cr = ₹5,500 cr, or ₹5,500 per tonne of yearly capacity (₹550 cr per MTPA) example.

Compare it with what a new plant would cost, ₹7,000 a tonne here example. Below that, buying an existing company is cheaper than building, and fewer rivals build; well above it, new plants get ordered. Cement acquisitions are often priced this way. Capacity is not all equal, though: location, limestone, freight distance and age all change what a tonne can earn, so read EV per tonne next to EBITDA per tonne.

Steel: the spread

Steel earns a spread on world prices

A steelmaker buys iron ore and coking coal and sells steel, and all three are priced in world markets. Its EBITDA per tonne is roughly the gap between the steel price and those raw materials, less the cost of turning one into the other. Tata Steel’s group EBITDA per tonne delivered doubled in FY22, then fell back public.

Tonnes hardly moved: group deliveries were 28.50 MT in FY21, 29.52 MT in FY22, 28.79 MT in FY23, 29.39 MT in FY24, 30.96 MT in FY25 and 31.97 MT in FY26 public. The swing came from the spread, not the volume. The group also mixes very different businesses: in FY26 India earned ₹15,213 a tonne, while the UK made an EBITDA loss of £217 million public, so read the segments as well as the total.

Go deeper: where these numbers come from

Tata Steel’s full-year results press releases print reported EBITDA per tonne and deliveries for the group and for India: FY22 and FY21 in the release of 3 May 2022, FY24 and FY23 in that of 29 May 2024, FY26 and FY25 in that of 15 May 2026. India means Tata Steel standalone plus Neelachal Ispat Nigam on the release’s own pro forma basis. The figures are as printed in each release; later reports restate some years for mergers.

Normalised earnings

Judge the cycle, not the year

Run Betwa through an eight-year cycle (the lab’s default settings). Its plants, people and market share never change, yet EPS goes from −₹7.50 to ₹75 example. Which year is the “real” Betwa? None of them. Analysts instead estimate normalised, or mid-cycle, earnings: what the company earns in an ordinary year.

There are two common ways. Mid-cycle margin: an ordinary price and utilisation, here ₹1,000 a tonne on 8 MT, giving EPS ₹30. Cycle average: the mean of a full cycle’s EPS, here ₹31.55, a little higher because good years sell more tonnes at better prices. Either needs at least one whole cycle of history, adjusted for capacity added since.

Normalised earnings are a judgement, not a figure in the accounts. If an industry adds capacity faster than demand for years, yesterday’s mid-cycle margin is too high; if it consolidates, too low.

The P/E paradox

P/E is highest at the bottom

Betwa’s share price moves less than its profit, because the market values an ordinary year and looks ahead (the lab’s pricing rule). Divide each year-end price by that year’s EPS, the trailing P/E, and the gold and lavender bars mirror each other example.

The P/E is 7.2 in Y6, the best year, just before profit falls: the stock looks cheapest at the top. It is 113.9 in Y2 and meaningless in the Y3 loss, just before the recovery: it looks dearest at the bottom. Buy at Y3’s ₹402 and sell two years later at ₹585, and you make 45%; buy at Y6’s ₹542 and sell at ₹450, and you lose 17%. That is Part 7’s trap two over a whole cycle.

Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

Eight years make one cycle, then it repeats. Each year price and utilisation move by the slider swing times a fixed shape: 0, −0.7, −1, −0.4, +0.4, +1, +0.7, 0, so Y3 is the trough and Y6 the peak, and both swings average zero. Capacity 10 MTPA; mid-cycle 80% use at ₹5,000 a tonne; variable cost ₹3,500 a tonne; fixed cash costs ₹400 cr; depreciation ₹300 cr; interest 10% of net debt; tax 25%, with a loss earning a 25% tax credit; 10 crore shares; no dividends, no growth, no new plants. Mid-cycle EPS is EPS at 80% and ₹5,000. The share price at each year end = 15 × mid-cycle EPS + (next year’s EPS − mid-cycle EPS) × the years slider: the market pays its usual multiple for an ordinary year, plus or minus next year’s extra profit or shortfall, extended for that many years. It is a teaching rule, not a forecast of how any real share trades. Returns are price only, two years after buying.

The common mistake

Cheap on trailing, dear on normalised

The mistake is to price peak profit as if it would last. In Betwa’s best year the trailing P/E is 7.2, but its price is 18.1 times mid-cycle EPS, dearer than its usual 15. In the loss year there is no P/E at all, yet the price is only 13.4 times mid-cycle EPS: cheaper than usual example.

Debt makes it worse. Interest is fixed, so the more a cyclical borrows, the deeper its losses at the bottom (try the lab’s debt slider). Companies tend to borrow to build at the top, when cash is plentiful and the plants are full, and the new capacity then arrives into the downturn. And a cyclical’s P/E in any one year says little next to a steady business’s, like the consumer companies of Part 32, “Consumer companies”.

Read it as a story. UltraTech’s cycle is mild: EBITDA per tonne stayed between ₹979 and ₹1,279 over five years while grey cement sales grew from 92.53 MT to 151.09 MT public. Yet its P/E was highest in the two weakest years, and in FY26, as EBITDA per tonne recovered to ₹1,141, its P/E fell back to 38.7. The market priced UltraTech at roughly ₹17,000 of EV per tonne of capacity, about four times what its own plan spends per tonne of new capacity. That premium is the market paying for profit per tonne, scale and growth, not for the concrete and kilns.

So what

Questions before buying a cyclical

  1. Where are we in the cycle? Utilisation, prices per tonne and the capacity being built across the industry, not just this company.
  2. What does an ordinary year earn? Mid-cycle EBITDA per tonne on normal volume, checked against a full cycle of history.
  3. What am I paying for that? Price ÷ mid-cycle EPS, and EV per tonne against the cost of new capacity.
  4. Can it survive the bottom? Debt and interest against trough EBITDA.
  5. Is the P/E telling me the cycle, or value? A record year at a P/E of 4 is usually the cycle talking.

Cyclicals have profits nobody controls. Part 34, “Utilities, PSUs and real estate”, turns to the opposite: power companies whose return is set by a regulator’s formula, companies the government owns, and property developers whose sales arrive years before their profit.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
A cyclical earns its money per tonne and over a whole cycle: value it on capacity and on what an ordinary year earns, and read a very low P/E at the top, or a very high one at the bottom, as the cycle talking, not value.
