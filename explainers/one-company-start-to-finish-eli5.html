MONETARY ARCHITECTURE / 26 MINELI5

One company, start to finish

Thirty-six parts taught one test at a time. This one runs them in order on one real company, Tata Consumer Products, from what it sells to what its price assumes, and ends with a one-page thesis: a worked example of the method, not a view on the shares.

Built from Tata Consumer Products’ Integrated Annual Report 2025-26 (audited consolidated statements, notes, board’s report, management discussion and governance report, printed pages as cited), its FY26 AGM presentation of 10 June 2026, its NSE shareholding filing for 31 March 2026 and NSE’s closing price of 30 March 2026, and the TCPL figures already worked out in Parts 15 to 28 · Read the source ↗

Part 36, “Screening for ideas”, built filters that find candidates and put TCPL through one: it passed on debt, growth, cash and pledges, and failed on ROCE and price. This part takes one candidate all the way through, using each earlier part once, in the series’ order, and linking back rather than re-teaching. The method

Business first, price last

The series is a route, and its order matters: understand the business, read its accounts, test the ratios, check that the profit is real, judge the people and the economy around them, and only then ask what the price assumes. Doing the price first tempts you to read everything after it as a defence of a number you already like.

Each step below names the part that teaches it. The sector parts (29 to 35) are for banks, exporters, cyclicals and the rest; TCPL is a consumer company, so only Part 32 applies. The numbers come from one place: TCPL’s own filings, and where Parts 15 to 28 already worked a TCPL figure out, this page uses theirs and says where two parts differ.

The business

What TCPL sells, to scale

Tata Consumer Products sells tea, coffee, salt, pulses, spices, ready-to-cook food and water, in India and abroad. Its AGM presentation splits FY26 revenue into four businesses public: India Beverages ₹6,467 cr, India Foods ₹6,588 cr, International ₹4,885 cr (tea abroad and US coffee) and Non-branded ₹2,387 cr (plantations and instant coffee), which sum to ₹20,327 cr before ₹37 cr of sales between them (slide 7).

The audited segment note groups the same sales differently, into India, International and Non-branded (Note 38, p. 477), so the two tables do not match line by line; Part 16 reconciles them. It names no single large customer, which is a gap in the disclosure, not proof there is none not public. Growth in the India core of tea and salt was “led by volume”, the report says (p. 223), the split Part 32 teaches you to ask for.

The moat

Is there a moat? Evidence, not adjectives

TCPL calls Tata Salt the largest salt brand in India and Tata Tea the second-largest tea brand, and says it reaches 290 million Indian households through 4.5 million shops (AGM slide 4) public. Those are its claims; the slide does not say by what measure. Part 17 asks for tests that numbers can fail. Two are below: does the gross margin hold when tea and coffee costs move, and does it take ever more advertising to hold it?

Gross margin (revenue less materials, purchases and the stock change) slipped 1.28 points to 41.57% in a year the report describes tea cost inflation “mitigated through calibrated price increases” (p. 179). Advertisement and sales charges were ₹1,225.48 cr, a smaller share of sales than in FY25 (Note 28, p. 455) public. That is consistent with pricing power, not proof of it: one year, and a line that mixes advertising with other sales costs.

The profit and loss

The P&L in two bars

Revenue from operations was ₹20,290.43 cr, up 15.2% public. Most of it goes on materials and goods bought for resale; what is left after staff and other costs is EBITDA of ₹2,791.80 cr, a 13.8% margin on this series’ definition (Part 3). TCPL’s own EBITDA is ₹2,815 cr, 13.9%, because it counts some other income (AGM slide 8).

Add other income of ₹164.75 cr, take off depreciation and amortisation of ₹626.68 cr, finance costs of ₹137.03 cr, net exceptional items of ₹20.06 cr, tax of ₹535.28 cr and a ₹90.70 cr loss from associates and joint ventures, and the profit for the year is ₹1,546.80 cr, of which ₹1,542.30 cr belongs to TCPL’s own shareholders: basic EPS ₹15.59 (P&L, p. 415) public.

Balance sheet and cash

What the profit sits on

Total assets were ₹34,452.96 cr on 31 March 2026, and more than half of them were bought rather than built: goodwill of ₹11,883.90 cr and other intangible assets, mostly brands, of ₹7,111.76 cr (balance sheet, p. 414) public. Part 4 explains goodwill: the price paid for a company above the value of what it owned.

The cash flow statement (Part 5) shows ₹2,421.85 cr of cash from operations, 1.57 times the year’s profit, and ₹448.57 cr spent on property, plant and intangibles, leaving free cash flow of ₹1,973.28 cr (p. 417) public. A business that turns profit into more cash than profit passes the first test of Part 13; part of the gap is ₹626.68 cr of D&A, a cost that uses no cash this year.

The ratios

Strong balance sheet, modest returns

Part 15 built TCPL’s dashboard; here are its verdicts in one place public. Safety is strong: borrowings of ₹2,120.31 cr sit against cash of ₹3,046.57 cr, so on the series’ strict definition TCPL has net cash of ₹926 cr, and EBIT covers interest 15.8 times (Part 11). Working capital is light: about 10 days from paying suppliers to being paid (Part 12). Returns are the weak spot, and they depend on what you divide by.

The same EBIT of ₹2,165.12 cr earns 8.55% on all capital employed, 16.13% if goodwill is left out and 34.29% if brands are left out too. TCPL’s own RoCE of 41.87% (MD&A, p. 224; ratio note, p. 483) starts from tangible net worth, so it is closest to the last bar. None is wrong (Part 10): the low ones ask whether the acquisitions earn their price; the high one says the underlying brands need little capital.

Go deeper: the working, and where parts differ

ROE = owners’ profit 1,542.30 ÷ owners’ equity 21,787.53 = 7.08%. ROCE = 2,165.12 ÷ (total equity 23,188.86 + borrowings 2,120.31) = 8.55%; take out goodwill 11,883.90 → 16.13%; take out other intangibles 7,111.76 as well → 34.29%. Interest cover = 2,165.12 ÷ 137.03 = 15.8×. Cash cycle = inventory 108.6 + receivables 20.7 − payables 119.3 days ≈ 10 days, on year-end balances and 365 days. Net cash differs between parts. Parts 15, 20 and 24 use ₹926 cr, cash and cash equivalents less borrowings. Parts 25 and 28 use TCPL’s own ₹2,978 cr (AGM slide 8), which also counts other bank balances and current investments; Part 4 reconciled it. The valuation below uses ₹2,978 cr, to stay with Part 28; on ₹926 cr each value is about ₹21 a share lower. TCPL’s own “21 days” of working capital is for its India business on its own definition (AGM slide 10).

Quality of earnings

Is the profit real?

Part 20’s checks, run on TCPL public: cash from operations grew with profit, and inventory fell. One line outran sales: trade receivables rose 32.0%, from ₹869.79 cr to ₹1,148.33 cr, against sales growth of 15.2%. Part 20 found TCPL’s own ratios, on average balances, nearly flat at 18 days, and no explanation in the report not public.

The rest is clean. Other income of ₹164.75 cr and net one-offs of ₹20.06 cr together are 8.5% of profit before tax. The tax rate was 24.6% against a 25.17% Indian rate. The auditor’s opinion is unmodified. The one gap to remember is between EPS figures (Part 6): TCPL’s adjusted EPS of ₹17.30 (AGM slide 8) is 11% above the audited ₹15.59, because it adds back amortisation of the Capital Foods and Organic India brands.

Management

Where the cash went, and what it bought

Part 18 followed the money public. In FY25 TCPL raised ₹2,980.57 cr, net, from a rights issue at ₹818 a share (Part 21), paid ₹1,809.00 cr for subsidiaries (Capital Foods and Organic India) and repaid ₹1,164.49 cr of commercial paper raised for acquisition funding (cash flow, pp. 417–418).

With the debt repaid, finance costs fell by ₹153 cr in FY26, which explains part of why profit grew faster than EBITDA: a financing choice, not operations. The new shares count too: weighted shares rose 1.06%, so EPS grew 19.4% while profit to owners grew 20.6% (Note 30, p. 456). Part 18 also checked a dated promise: a 16% EBITDA margin “by Q3” of FY26, discussed on the Q1 call, was not reached; the year closed at 13.9% (AGM slide 8). Dividends rose to ₹10 a share recommended for FY26 (p. 177).

Governance

Who owns it, and who checks

Part 19 read the filings public. Promoters hold 33.83%, Tata Sons alone 28.68%, with none of it pledged (NSE shareholding filing for 31 March 2026; annual report p. 254). Below half, the promoter cannot pass an ordinary resolution alone if everyone else votes against.

Four of the seven directors are independent (board’s report, p. 182), and the audit committee is all independent. Deloitte Haskins & Sells LLP gave an unmodified opinion, with two key audit matters; the larger is the UK and Europe goodwill of ₹2,184.50 cr, tested because of “competitive pressures and decline in black tea demand” there (auditor’s report, p. 405). It was not impaired in FY26.

The economy

Tea, coffee and the rupee

Consumer staples are defensive: people keep buying salt and tea in a slowdown. TCPL’s exposures are on the cost side and abroad (Part 22) public. International revenue grew 16% in rupees but 9% in constant currency (AGM slides 6–7): a weaker rupee added the rest. International margins fell on “an increase in coffee costs and the impact of U.S. tariffs” (p. 177).

The FY25 figure of about ₹4,211 cr is this page’s estimate from the 16% example; TCPL gives only the rate. The same rupee lifted the value of the overseas businesses by ₹908.17 cr, booked in other comprehensive income, not profit (p. 415). The risk note puts the effect of a 5% currency move on profit before tax, through the instruments on the books, at only ₹5.46 cr (Note 36, p. 467).

Price against its past

What the market pays, and paid

TCPL closed at ₹1,014.80 on NSE on 30 March 2026, the last trading day of FY26 public. On 98,95,61,780 shares (Note 14, p. 445) that is a market cap of ₹100,421 cr, and 65.1 times basic EPS of ₹15.59. Part 26 put that in TCPL’s own five-year band.

65.1 is below the five-year average of 72.4, so on its own history the FY26 price was not stretched. That is a comparison with itself: the whole band sits at 54 to 89 times earnings, so it says nothing about whether those multiples were ever justified. Part 15 used a later price, ₹1,086.30 on 6 November 2026, for a P/E of 69.7; this page stays at FY26’s close.

Value

Three stories against one price

The DCF of Part 24, the reverse DCF of Part 25 and the scenarios of Part 28 all start from TCPL’s EBIT of ₹2,165.12 cr public. Parts 25 and 28 take tax at the 25.17% Indian rate, so NOPAT (operating profit after tax) is ₹1,620.16 cr, and discount at 12%, Part 23’s cost of equity with a beta of 1 example.

Part 28’s three stories, weighted 25/50/25, give ₹405 a share; with a 30% margin of safety a buyer would pay about ₹284. The FY26 price is 2.5 times the weighted value. Turned around, as Part 25 did: the price needs NOPAT to grow about 28.9% a year for ten years, with new money earning 40%, against FY26 EBIT growth of 15.3%. The market may be using a lower discount rate, or a longer runway; the model cannot say which.

Go deeper: where Parts 24, 25 and 28 differ

Tax. Part 24 uses the effective rate, ₹535.28 cr ÷ ₹2,172.78 cr = 24.6%, for NOPAT of ₹1,631.7 cr and FY26 free cash flow to the firm of ₹1,955.0 cr; Parts 25 and 28 use 25.17%, for ₹1,620.16 cr. The difference moves the implied growth by 0.1 point. Net cash. Part 24 uses ₹926 cr; Parts 25 and 28 use ₹2,978 cr: about ₹21 a share. Return on new money. 40% sits near TCPL’s own RoCE of 41.87%, which leaves goodwill out; on the series’ 8.55%, acquisitions would have to be counted as reinvestment, as Part 24 warned. Price. ₹1,014.80 at FY26’s close here and in Parts 25, 26 and 28; ₹1,086.30 in Part 15. Not modelled: the associates’ loss and the non-controlling interest. Part 27’s sum of the parts is not used: TCPL’s segments are reported, but their separate values are not.

Go deeper: the assumptions behind the lab

The model is Part 24’s: free cash flow to the firm = NOPAT × (1 − growth ÷ return on new money), for FY27–FY36, then a terminal value growing at 5% (4% in the bear preset), discounted at your rate. NOPAT starts at ₹1,620.16 cr; net cash ₹2,978 cr is added and the sum divided by 98.956 crore shares. “What the price needs” solves for one growth rate for all ten years, as Part 25 did, at your return and discount rate; if no rate up to 60% reaches the price it says so. When growth is above the return on new money, reinvestment exceeds NOPAT and free cash flow is negative. Lights compare the unrounded number with the line. Counting lights treats every check as equal, which they are not.

TCPL in one page, as a worked example

Here is the page the method produces, written with the series’ rules of thumb rather than anyone’s judgement. The five hollow shapes are the checks a rule cannot answer; the lab leaves them to you.

This thesis is a worked example of the method, frozen at FY2026. It is not a view on whether to buy, hold or sell TCPL shares, and its assumptions (the 12% rate, the three stories, every line in the checklist) are teaching choices, not forecasts.

So what

Using the method on your own company

  1. Keep the order. Business, accounts, ratios, quality, people, economy, then price. Write down what you think the business is worth to an owner before you look at the multiple.
  2. Use the filings, and cite pages. Every number here has a page. If a number has no page, find out whose it is.
  3. Write the questions, not just the lights. TCPL’s checklist ends with three questions: do acquired brands earn their price, why did receivables jump, and what discount rate makes the price reasonable.
  4. Turn the price into a belief. A reverse DCF says what you would have to believe; decide whether you do, and what would show you were wrong.

A thesis says what one company might be worth. It does not say how much of your money it should get, when to sell, or what tax and costs take on the way. Part 38, “Portfolio, taxes and costs”, closes the series with those.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Run the tests in order, from the business to the price, cite a page for every number, and finish with what the price makes you believe: the checklist tells you where to dig, not whether to buy.
