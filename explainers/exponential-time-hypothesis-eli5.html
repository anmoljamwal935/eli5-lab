The Exponential Time Hypothesis, and OpenAI’s claim to break it

Some puzzles seem to get twice as hard every time you make them a little bigger. For 25 years, computer scientists bet that one famous puzzle can never escape that kind of runaway growth. On 26 October 2026, OpenAI published a proof, written by its AI, that says the bet was wrong. Here is what the bet was, why so much rested on it, and why nobody should call it settled yet.

The problem

Every extra switch doubles the work

Picture a wall of light switches, each either on or off, and a list of rules about which ones should be on. You want a setting that obeys every rule.

The obvious way is to try every setting. One switch has 2 settings, two switches have 4, three have 8. Each new switch doubles the list. With 300 switches the number of settings has 91 digits, more than the number of atoms in the observable universe.

The puzzle

Rules that each mention three switches

In our puzzle every rule names three switches and is happy if at least one of them is the way it wants. “Not-b” means the rule wants b off. Below, three switches and two rules: six of the eight settings keep both rules happy.

Computer scientists call this puzzle 3-SAT (“SAT” as in “satisfy the rules”). The switches are called variables and the rules clauses. Rule 1 here is the example used in OpenAI’s paper.

Why this puzzle

Many other problems hide inside it

Many real problems can be rewritten as switches and rules: filling a Sudoku, building an exam timetable with no clashes, checking that a computer chip has no design bug. Each choice becomes a switch; each requirement becomes a rule.

That makes 3-SAT a master puzzle. A truly fast way to solve it would give a truly fast way to solve all of them, so its true difficulty is one of the central questions in computer science.

Fast vs slow

Two kinds of growth, worlds apart

Computer scientists sort methods by how their work grows with the size, n. “Truly fast” methods grow like n×n×n (written n³): double the switches, eight times the work. Doubling methods grow like 2ⁿ: add one switch, double the work.

At 100 switches, on a computer doing a billion steps per second, the gap is a thousandth of a second against 40 trillion years, about 3,000 times the age of the universe.

What people tried

Clever tricks shrink the doubling, not the growth

Nobody has found a truly fast method. The best tricks skip most settings: a 1999 method multiplies work by 4/3 per extra switch instead of 2, and later work got this to about 1.308 (figures as listed in OpenAI’s paper).

That is a huge saving: at 100 switches the 1.308 method takes about 8 minutes at a billion steps a second. But at 200 switches it takes 6.6 million years. Every bar still doubles when the switches double.

The hypothesis

ETH: the multiplier can’t sink toward ×1

In 2001, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi wrote down a bet called the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH). Picture the extra work each added switch causes as a multiplier: ×2 for checking everything, ×1.308 for the best trick.

ETH says that however clever the method, this multiplier for 3-SAT stays above some floor that is bigger than ×1. Work can never stop growing at a steady percentage per switch. Nobody proved it; nearly everyone believed it.

Why it mattered

Hundreds of results were built on it

Proving that a problem cannot be solved faster is extremely hard. So researchers proved something easier: “if this problem could be solved much faster, then 3-SAT could be too, and ETH would be false.” Believing ETH meant believing the speed limit.

OpenAI says hundreds of papers in fields such as databases, biology and geometry rest on ETH or its stronger version (strong ETH). Some of these results can also be proved from other, separate guesses, which still stand.

The claim

OpenAI’s method: the multiplier keeps shrinking

OpenAI’s paper describes a method that counts every setting that keeps all the rules. Its work grows like 2 to the power of n divided by √(ln n). Here ln n is the natural logarithm, which grows very slowly: for a million switches it is about 14.

So the extra work per switch is no longer a fixed multiplier. It slides down towards ×1 as puzzles grow, and in the end dips under any floor you pick. That is exactly what ETH said could never happen.

In numbers

At a million switches, a smaller exponent

A worked example. Checking every setting of a million switches takes 2 to the power 1,000,000 steps. The claimed method divides that exponent by √(ln 1,000,000), about 3.72, leaving 2 to the power of roughly 269,000.

That is a gigantic improvement on paper, and still a number with about 81,000 digits. This sketch leaves out a fixed factor the paper does not spell out, which the lab below lets you vary.

The common mistake

Better than doubling is not fast

The new method is “sub-exponential”: it grows slower than any steady doubling, but much faster than n³. At 1,000 switches it sits right next to the best earlier method and nowhere near truly fast.

So it does not settle the famous P vs NP question, the million-dollar prize asking whether every puzzle whose answers are quick to check is also quick to solve. The paper itself warns that its speed-up does not carry over to every such puzzle.

Where it stands

Claimed, computer-checked, not yet accepted

OpenAI checked the proof with Lean, a program that verifies every logical step of a proof, like a strict spell-checker for maths. The exact statement it checked, written in Lean’s own precise language, is public.

Lean only confirms that the proof proves that statement. People must still confirm the statement really means “ETH is false” and read all 67 pages. Ryan Williams (MIT), Impagliazzo and Paturi are organising an open check, with no secrecy agreements and no pay.

An error in how the Lean statement defines the machine, the input or the running time would let a correct Lean proof prove the wrong thing. That gap is why the human review matters.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
ETH bet that every extra switch in 3-SAT must multiply the work by some fixed amount above one. OpenAI claims that multiplier keeps shrinking forever. The method is still astronomically slow, but if the proof holds, a foundation of computer science has moved.
