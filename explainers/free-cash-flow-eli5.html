Free cash flow and earnings quality

Profit depends on accounting choices; cash is much harder to dress up. Free cash flow is what is left in the till after the business has paid to keep itself running and growing, and it is the first test a reported profit has to pass.

The problem

A profit you cannot spend

Ramu Chai earned ₹50 cr of profit after tax in FY26 example, and its cash from operations (CFO, Part 5) was ₹58 cr example. Yet CFO is counted before the business buys anything for tomorrow. Ramu spent ₹45 cr on machines, vans and a new line. Same year, three numbers, and only the last is left over for the people who own and lend to the business.

That left-over is free cash flow. “Free” means free of the claims of running and growing the business, not free of every claim: lenders and owners still queue for it.

The definition

FCF is cash in, capex out

Take CFO from the cash flow statement and subtract capex, the cash paid for property, plant, equipment and other long-lived assets, shown under investing. Ramu: 58 − 45 = ₹13 cr example. It is a cash figure, so it moves with timing as well as with the business.

FCF = CFO − capex = 58 − 45 = ₹13 cr

Two things sit outside this figure. Ramu’s interest paid (₹10 cr) is in financing, so lenders are paid out of FCF, not before it. And a business that sells old machines or buys another company has cash flows that this simple version ignores. Later panels return to both.

Keeping up vs getting bigger

Maintenance and growth capex

Not all capex is equal. Maintenance capex replaces what wears out, so the business stands still. Growth capex adds capacity to earn more later. Companies rarely split them, so a rough proxy is depreciation (Part 6): the accounts’ own estimate of what wore out. Ramu’s depreciation is ₹12 cr example, so maintenance is about 12 and growth capex about 33.

The bottom bar is the useful one: CFO less maintenance alone is ₹46 cr, roughly what the business throws off if it stopped growing. FCF of ₹13 cr is low because Ramu chose to grow, which is a different story from a business that cannot afford to stand still.

Depreciation is a proxy, not a measurement. It spreads the old purchase price over past years, while replacing an asset costs today’s price, so true maintenance is usually higher. Treat 12 as a floor, not a fact.

Earnings quality

Does profit turn into cash?

CFO ÷ PAT asks how much operating cash each rupee of profit brought. Ramu’s ratio is 1.17, 1.18, 1.18, 1.18, 1.16 over FY22–FY26 example. Cash is a little above profit every year, steadily: depreciation reduces profit but costs no cash, and the cash tied up in stock and customers takes a little back.

Why a gap is a warning. If profit holds up while CFO falls behind, sales may be booked but not collected, or costs may be pushed out of the profit line (Part 6). A common rule of thumb is to look harder when CFO ÷ PAT sits below 0.8 in two or more years. That is a teaching marker, not an accounting rule; one weak year can be plain timing, which is why it takes two. Part 20 covers the wider set of red flags.

Go deeper: when a high ratio is not good news

A ratio far above 1 can come from stretching suppliers or collecting early, which cannot repeat for ever, and from profits depressed by a one-off charge. Look at several years, as the drawing does, before reading anything into one.

The price tag

FCF yield: cash per rupee of price

FCF yield is FCF ÷ market cap, the lavender price tag of Part 7. Ramu: 13 ÷ 1,000 = 1.3% example, at ₹100 a share and 10 cr shares. Its earnings yield, profit ÷ market cap, is 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5.0%. The gap is the capex.

FCF yield = 13 ÷ 1,000 = 1.3% · earnings yield = 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5.0%

When a business spends more on capex than depreciation, and its working capital grows, FCF yield sits below earnings yield. That is not a flaw by itself: it is a company paying now for growth (Part 9). Whether the spending pays off is a question of returns (Part 10). A low FCF yield with high returns can be fine; a low one with poor returns is the trap.

What the cash is for

Where free cash flow can go

Once the business is looked after, FCF can pay dividends, buy back shares, repay debt, fund acquisitions, or sit in the bank. Ramu’s FY26: FCF ₹13 cr plus ₹4 cr interest received plus ₹10 cr of new borrowing is ₹27 cr in. Interest paid of ₹10 cr and the ₹18 cr dividend (FY25’s, paid in FY26) is ₹28 cr out. The ₹1 cr gap came from the cash balance, 41 to 40 example.

So Ramu paid a dividend larger than its free cash flow, with help from borrowing. One year of that is nothing. A habit of it means the dividend rests on debt. Part 14 asks how much dividend is safe, Part 18 how management chooses between these uses, and Part 21 covers buybacks.

The lease trap

Leases can flatter CFO

Under Ind AS 116, the Indian leases standard, a company that rents shops, offices or warehouses for more than a year shows the right to use them as an asset and its rent obligation as a lease liability, much as IFRS 16 does. Its profit then carries depreciation on the asset plus interest on the liability, and the cash paid goes in financing, not in CFO. CFO therefore looks better than when rent was an operating cost.

Take a shop chain with CFO ₹120 cr, capex ₹60 cr and lease payments ₹20 cr example. This is a one-off case; it does not change Ramu’s accounts. Reported FCF is 60. But the shops are as needed as ever, so a stricter FCF subtracts the lease payments: 40. Treat lease liabilities as debt-like (Part 11 covers debt). TCPL’s statement does the same: its “Payment of Lease Liabilities” sits in financing.

One year can mislead

Judge FCF over several years

Ramu’s FCF was 15, 10, 8, 10, 13 over FY22–FY26 example: thin slices of a growing CFO, because capex climbed from 20 to 45 as the business grew. FY24’s ₹8 cr looks poor, yet it was a capacity-building year, and revenue kept growing about 11% a year.

Over the five years CFO added to ₹231 cr, capex to ₹175 cr and FCF to ₹56 cr, against ₹197 cr of profit. The reverse trap exists too: a business can flatter one year’s FCF by delaying capex or paying suppliers late (Part 12 covers working capital). Ask what the capex bought, and whether this year’s cash is repeatable.

TCPL: free cash flow and its quality

Tata Consumer Products, consolidated, year to 31 March 2026 public. Cash from operations was ₹2,421.85 cr (FY25: ₹2,056.65 cr). Capex, the “payment for property, plant and equipment including intangible assets”, was ₹448.57 cr (FY25: ₹459.54 cr). Free cash flow is 2,421.85 − 448.57 = ₹1,973.28 cr (FY25: ₹1,597.11 cr). Profit for the year was ₹1,546.80 cr (FY25: ₹1,287.10 cr), so CFO ÷ PAT is 1.57 (FY25: 1.60).

CFO ÷ PAT = 2,421.85 ÷ 1,546.80 = 1.57 · FY25: 2,056.65 ÷ 1,287.10 = 1.60

Cash runs well ahead of profit, mostly because ₹626.68 cr of depreciation and amortisation cost no cash, and working capital released ₹145.19 cr this year. Only two years sit in this report, so a five-year view like Ramu’s is not possible here. TCPL keeps 81.5% of its CFO after capex, while Ramu, a faster-growing small firm, keeps 22%. For TCPL’s own “free cash flow to EBITDA 107%” claim, which uses a different definition, see Part 5.

market cap = 98,95,61,780 shares × ₹1,086.30 (NSE close, 6 Nov 2026) = ₹107,496 cr FCF yield = 1,973.28 ÷ 107,496 = 1.8% · earnings yield = 15.59 ÷ 1,086.30 = 1.4% (P/E 69.7)

At that dated price, FCF yield is a little above earnings yield, the opposite of Ramu, because TCPL’s capex is well below its depreciation and amortisation. The market is paying about 54 times FCF for TCPL, so it is paying for growth, not for today’s cash.

TCPL: maintenance proxy and leases

What is TCPL’s maintenance capex? Not printed. Depreciation and amortisation was ₹626.68 cr, above the whole ₹448.57 cr of capex, which would say all capex is maintenance. But D&A has three parts public: depreciation of plant and equipment ₹187.61 cr, depreciation of leased (right-of-use) assets ₹96.40 cr, and amortisation of intangibles ₹342.67 cr, mostly acquired brands whose cost was paid in earlier years (Part 6).

Plant depreciation of ₹187.61 cr is the closer proxy. Capex is 2.4 times that, so about ₹261 cr (448.57 − 187.61, my arithmetic) looks like growth, though capex also includes software and other intangibles. CFO less that maintenance proxy is ₹2,234 cr. The trap runs both ways: total D&A would make TCPL look as if it only maintains; plant depreciation makes it look as if it grows.

Leases. TCPL’s financing section shows “Payment of Lease Liabilities” of ₹79.47 cr (FY25: ₹74.54 cr), so CFO excludes it. Note 26 shows ₹40.96 cr of interest expense on lease liabilities, and lease liabilities were ₹699.20 cr (₹608.64 cr non-current plus ₹90.56 cr current). The cash flow statement does not split principal from interest, and I cannot tell from the printed pages whether the ₹79.47 cr includes the ₹40.96 cr. So there are two strict figures:

if ₹79.47 cr is the whole lease payment: 1,973.28 − 79.47 = 1,893.81 (yield 1.76%) if interest sits elsewhere, on top: 1,893.81 − 40.96 = 1,852.85 (yield 1.72%)

Either way leases cost 4–6% of FCF, so TCPL is not a heavy lease user, and the lease trap barely changes the picture here. For a retailer with many shops it would matter much more.

Go deeper: why FCF here is before interest

TCPL’s ₹111.50 cr of finance cost paid is also in financing, so FCF (CFO − capex) is cash before lenders’ interest, as for Ramu. Some analysts subtract interest to get cash for owners only. This series keeps the plain CFO − capex so every part agrees.

So what

Three questions for any profit

  1. Is the cash there? Compare CFO with profit over several years, not one; below about 0.8 for two or more years deserves a closer look.
  2. What was the capex for? Split maintenance from growth, at least roughly, and judge the growth by its returns.
  3. What is hiding outside CFO? Leases, interest and one-year timing can all flatter the number.

Ramu’s FCF yield is 1.3% against an earnings yield of 5%, and TCPL’s is 1.8% against 1.4%. Part 14, “The other price tags: P/B, dividends, EV/EBITDA”, adds more ways to read a price, and asks how much dividend that cash can safely carry.

AN IDEA TO TAKE WITH YOU
Profit is what the accounts say; free cash flow is what is left after the business has paid to keep going and grow. Check it over several years, split maintenance from growth, and count the leases.
